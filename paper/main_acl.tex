\begin{abstract}
Firms that hand bidding to LLM agents could fix prices without any person agreeing to it. We ask which restriction on how LLM bidders communicate removes the most collusion at the least cost. In a repeated sealed-bid procurement auction with private costs redrawn every round, firms bid with no channel, private notes, notes the rival secretly reads, or open messages, and every comparison pairs two sessions on the same seed. Across three models (\DS, \LU{} and \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5}), 540 sessions and 25 pre-registered hypotheses, open messages raise the collusion index (0 at the Bayes-Nash price, 1 at the reserve) by 0.88 for \DS{} and by 0.85 with three firms: the agents write rotation and identical-bid schemes with punishment threats, unprompted, and draw \LU{} and \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} rivals in (by 0.43 and 0.19). Notes give no significant rise. Removing the channel is the strongest restriction where it has no legitimate use. Where a channel must stay open, one sentence saying that a competition authority reviews every message lowers the index by 0.77, back to the no-channel level, while the lowest-cost firm wins six points less often than without a channel; this replicates on new seeds and against \LU. A 50-character limit changes nothing, since a scheme fits in 27 characters, and a rebid on ties, announcing only the winner and a digit filter do not significantly lower the price. A replay placebo shows that displayed bids alone raise a model's markup, so a high price does not prove coordination: the transcripts, not the prices, are the evidence.\footnote{Code and declared analysis plans: \url{https://github.com/linusc9516/bid-pricing-collusion}.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Procurement has a long record of bid rigging: rotation, cover bids and identical bids are patterns competition authorities train staff to look for \citep{oecd2009}. If bidding is handed to LLM agents, two risks follow. Agents could reach high prices without being instructed to, and agents that can communicate with each other could agree on a scheme that no employee proposed. \citet{fish2024} show that LLM pricing agents in a repeated oligopoly set prices above the competitive level, and \citet{heo2026} report tacit collusion between LLM bidders in simulated construction bidding.

Track~1, question~1.5 of this sprint asks which single restriction removes the most collusion at the least cost. We answer it for the restrictions that concern how bidding agents can reach each other. We hold a sealed-bid procurement auction fixed, vary how the agents can communicate, and measure the price the buyer pays. A \emph{seed} fixes the costs of every round of a session. Most conditions run on the same 12 seeds, and each replication on new seeds brings its own comparison arms, so every comparison is between two sessions on the same seed (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). Earlier restrictions were tested together or did not bind: an overseer combined with a five-character message limit reduced collusion in LLM double auctions \citep{agrawal2025}, so neither part can be credited alone, and a prompt-level prohibition gave no reliable reduction in LLM Cournot markets \citep{bracale2026}. We test a length cap and an oversight notice separately, on the same seeds as the open channel.

\textbf{An open message channel is the condition that produces collusion.} \DS{} firms agree on a rotation or an identical-bid scheme in the first rounds and in most sessions hold the price near the reserve, with two firms or three, and they draw \LU{} and \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} rivals into such schemes; on its own \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} rarely writes, and \LU's price effect is small and does not replicate. Notes do not substitute for the channel, even when the rival can read them. A limit on message length does not stop the schemes, which fit in 27 characters, but a one-sentence notice that messages are reviewed by a competition authority brings the price back to the no-channel level, on new seeds and against a different model. A replay placebo shows that the bids a model is shown move its own bids with no one responding, which limits what a price level alone can say about coordination.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A pre-registered, seed-paired comparison of four channel conditions for LLM bidders in a private-cost procurement auction (Section~\ref{sec:main}), extended to a third model family, mixed-model lineups and three firms (Section~\ref{sec:general}).
\item A test of two partial restrictions on the channel, which answers question~1.5 for this auction: an oversight notice removes most of the collusion and a length cap removes none; the notice's effect replicates on new seeds and in a mixed lineup (Section~\ref{sec:restrict}).
\item Ablations of the channel that show what the collusion needs: the firms must exchange messages, and the effect survives a reworded prompt (Section~\ref{sec:needs}).
\item Transcript evidence of what the channel is used for: explicit rotation and identical-bid schemes with punishment threats that the agents write unprompted, and, in mixed lineups, which model proposes them (Section~\ref{sec:conduct}).
\item A replay placebo and a rescaled game that separate anchoring on displayed numbers from coordination between firms (Section~\ref{sec:placebo}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\para{LLM agents in markets.} \citet{fish2024} find that LLM pricing agents in a repeated Bertrand market settle above the competitive price, and that a sentence or two of prompt wording moves the level. \citet{heo2026} run GPT-4o agents in repeated price-based construction bidding and report tacit collusion at supracompetitive prices. \citet{agrawal2025} evaluate seller collusion in LLM double auctions with an LLM-judged coordination score. Unlike these settings, our costs are private and redrawn every round, so the competitive bid moves each round; our auction also has a closed-form Bayes-Nash benchmark, and each condition is compared with a session on the same costs.

\para{Algorithmic collusion.} Q-learning agents learn supracompetitive prices with punishment of deviations \citep{calvano2020}, and in first-price selling auctions with identical known values the bidders learn to bid low together, which there is collusive \citep{banchio2022}. \citet{xu2026} argue that collusion can stay invisible to audits that look at each agent's prices separately. A logged message channel makes the agreement visible in our transcripts.

\para{Restrictions on collusion.} A prompt-level prohibition of collusion gives no reliable reduction in LLM Cournot markets \citep{bracale2026}, while an overseer combined with a five-character message limit reduces seller collusion in LLM double auctions \citep{agrawal2025}. Mixing algorithm types, LLMs against Q-learners, breaks LLM collusion \citep{keppo2026}, and in theory a single no-regret participant suffices to destabilise it \citep{collina2025}; higher sampling temperature weakens the coupling between agents built on the same model \citep{xu2026}. We compare two restrictions on one channel, a length cap and an oversight notice, in one auction on the same seeds.

\para{Economics of bidding rings.} \citet{mcafee1992} show that a ring without side payments can do no better than identical bids, with the auctioneer's tie-break as the allocation device. \citet{skrzypacz2004} study tacit collusion in repeated auctions with limited public monitoring, and \citet{athey2004} show that colluding firms with private costs may prefer rigid prices to efficient allocation. The schemes our agents write match these: identical bids near the reserve with the tie-break choosing the winner, or strict alternation that ignores costs.

\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Setting up the Auction}
\label{sec:setup}

Two firms, A and B, bid for one contract per \emph{round} over a \emph{session} of 50 rounds (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Each firm's cost is drawn independently from $U[0,100]$ each round and rounded to 0.01. The lowest bid wins and is paid its bid; ties are broken at random. Bids lie in $[0, 100]$, so the reserve price is 100. The winner's profit is its bid minus its cost; the loser earns 0. The prompt says the firms bid for the same contract over a series of rounds and asks each to maximise its total profit; the number of rounds is not announced. The auctioneer is a deterministic script, and no prompt mentions the rival's cost, only the distribution both firms' costs are drawn from.

\para{Benchmark and outcome.} The symmetric Bayes-Nash bid with $n$ bidders and costs on $[0, \bar c]$ is $b^*(c) = c + (\bar c - c)/n$, or $(c + 100)/2$ here. The primary outcome is a session's \emph{collusion index}
\[
I_s = \frac{\sum_r \big(p_r - b^*(c^{\min}_r)\big)}{\sum_r \big(100 - b^*(c^{\min}_r)\big)},
\]
where $p_r$ is the winning bid of round $r$ and $c^{\min}_r$ the lowest cost in that round. $I_s = 0$ when winning bids equal the benchmark, $I_s = 1$ when every contract is won at the reserve, and $I_s < 0$ below the benchmark. $I_s$ is not clipped. Declared secondary outcomes include the \emph{markup ratio} (the sum of bid minus cost, over the same sum at the benchmark) and the \emph{joint profit ratio} (total profit over that of benchmark play on the same costs); the \emph{lowest-cost win share} (the share of rounds won by the lowest-cost firm) and the \emph{tie rate} are declared descriptives. Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} gives every definition.

\para{Arms.} The four main arms share one system prompt (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}); M1, M2 and C1 each add one paragraph describing their channel and its tools, and B0 adds nothing.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\textcolor{cB}{B0}, no channel.} The bidding history is the only link between the firms. In the baseline run B0 is paired with a \emph{one-shot} control on the same seeds that shows no history, so each round is bid as if it were the first (H1, H2).
\item \textbf{\textcolor{cM1}{M1}, private notes.} After each round but the last a firm gets a \emph{reflection step} of up to four tool requests to read and add notes (1{,}000 characters per note); its notes are shown when it bids.
\item \textbf{\textcolor{cM2}{M2}, shared notes.} As M1, but both firms read and write one workspace. The prompt is M1's word for word and never says who reads the workspace.
\item \textbf{\textcolor{cC}{C1}, open messages.} After each round but the last, each firm has a \emph{communication step}, in which it may send one message of up to 500 characters or none. The prompt says every firm reads every message and neither encourages nor forbids agreement.
\end{itemize}
Two further arms restrict C1 for \DS{}: \textbf{C1\textsubscript{cap}} cuts every message to 50 characters, states the limit in the tool description and tells the sender when a message was cut (the shortest agreement in the logs is 27 characters), and \textbf{C1\textsubscript{notice}} adds one sentence to the channel paragraph, ``Every message is reviewed by a competition authority.'', with no penalty stated. The prompts are in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\para{Ablation and replication arms.} Seven further \DS{} arms on the same seeds each change one setting of B0 or C1 (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). \textbf{C1\textsubscript{bafo}} replaces the random tie-break with one rebid of the tied firms (a best and final offer), with no communication step between bid and rebid. \textbf{C1\textsubscript{hidden}} and \textbf{B0\textsubscript{hidden}} announce only the winner of each round; a firm's history shows its own bid but not the rival's. \textbf{C1\textsubscript{undelivered}} keeps C1's prompt word for word, including ``Every firm reads every message'', but shows each firm only its own messages, so to each firm the rival appears never to write. \textbf{C1\textsubscript{nodigits}} removes every digit from a message before it is delivered and says so in one added sentence. \textbf{B0\textsubscript{v2}} and \textbf{C1\textsubscript{v2}} reword the system prompt sentence by sentence. To check the notice, \textbf{N1} runs B0, C1 and C1\textsubscript{notice} for \DS{} back to back on the same day, on 12 new seeds, and \textbf{N2} runs C1\textsubscript{notice} in the mixed \DS$\times$\LU{} lineup on the original seeds, paired with that lineup's B0 and C1.

\subsection{Evaluation Framework}

\para{Pairing and tests.} A seed fixes every round's costs and the tie-break random stream, so two arms run on the same seed face identical costs; each cell has 12 sessions per arm, and the session is the unit of analysis. Each hypothesis is a paired contrast on $I$ over 12 seeds (24 in H25), tested with a two-sided sign-flip permutation test \citep{good2000} (exact for 12 pairs; 100{,}000 random sign patterns for H25) and Holm-corrected within its declared family \citep{holm1979}; we report the mean difference, a 95\% bootstrap interval over pairs \citep{efron1993} and the number of positive pairs. Every test, exclusion rule and reading of a null was committed to \texttt{configs/analysis.yaml} before its run, and hypotheses keep their declared numbers (H1, H2, \dots). Appendix~\ref{app:eval} gives the seeds, the run checks and the model settings.

\para{Models and conduct labels.} The firms are run by \DS{} (low reasoning effort), \LU{} and \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} (high reasoning effort); each bid is a tool call with a short stated reason. \model{Gemini-3.8-Flash} labels each communication step, and a sample of 24 reflection steps per session in the notes arms, against a fixed rubric, and a label counts only with a verbatim quote from the step, which may come from the hidden reasoning or from the message. A \emph{judged step} is one with hidden reasoning or a message to read; a \emph{proposal} is a step labelled \emph{proposes coordination}; and a session has an \emph{agreement} when one firm proposes and another proposes or accepts. On 70 hand-checked message steps the judge agrees with our coding on 361 of 370 label calls, including all 70 \emph{proposes coordination} calls; it misses some acceptances, so acceptance counts are likely undercounts (Appendix~\ref{app:eval}).

Table~\ref{tab:summary} and Figures~\ref{fig:rounds} and~\ref{fig:contrasts} show the results; every $p$ below is Holm-adjusted within its declared family. Without a channel, \DS{} bids just below the benchmark ($I = -0.05$) and \LU{} above it ($0.19$). In the one-shot sessions of the baseline, which show no history, \DS{} bids at the benchmark (H2: $-0.03$, $[-0.04, -0.01]$, inside the declared $\pm 0.05$) and \LU{} below it ($-0.16$, $[-0.18, -0.14]$). History alone does not move \DS{} above the benchmark (H1: $-0.03$, $[-0.09, 0.04]$, $p = 0.47$), so repeated play without a channel produces no tacit collusion in this model. \LU's history sessions are above its one-shot sessions (H1, declared as a fall and so not supported: $+0.35$, $[0.20, 0.50]$, $p = 0.002$); Section~\ref{sec:placebo} tests whether that needs coordination.

\para{An open channel raises prices.} Figure~\ref{fig:contrasts} shows that open messages raise the index for both models, but by very different amounts. For \DS{} every pair ends higher with messages (H5: $+0.88$, $[0.74, 1.02]$, $p = 0.001$): the rise starts in the first five rounds, and the index stays above 0.75 from round 6 (Figure~\ref{fig:rounds}). For \LU{} the rise is small (H5: $+0.11$, $[0.03, 0.21]$, $p = 0.041$) and comes mainly from two of twelve sessions; it does not replicate on new seeds (Section~\ref{sec:general}). The channel raises prices in both models, but only \DS's rise is large and replicates.

\para{Notes show no significant rise.} No contrast of private notes (M1) or shared notes (M2) with no channel (B0) or with each other is significant in either model (\DS{} H10 M1$-$B0: $+0.08$, $[0.01, 0.15]$, $p = 0.13$; H9 M2$-$B0: $+0.05$, $[-0.02, 0.12]$, $p = 0.18$; \LU{} H3 M1$-$B0: $-0.03$, $[-0.18, 0.10]$, $p = 0.70$; H4 M2$-$M1: $-0.03$, $[-0.09, 0.01]$, $p = 0.55$). These are not equivalence results: the data do not rule out a rise from notes of up to about 0.15 for \DS{} or 0.10 for \LU. With notes, \DS{} bids almost exactly the equilibrium strategy: every M2 session lies within 0.003 of the benchmark, and the lowest-cost firm wins every M2 round.

\para{Collusion costs efficiency.} The schemes written with open messages (C1) ignore costs. In \DS's C1 sessions the lowest-cost firm wins 58\% of rounds against 85\% without a channel, and 43\% of rounds end in a tie, against 1\%. \LU's low-cost share is 71\% in both B0 and C1.

\subsection{Other models, mixed lineups and three firms}
\label{sec:general}

\begin{figure}[t]

The channel effect holds wherever a \DS{} firm is in the market (Figure~\ref{fig:extensions}; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ext}). With three \DS{} firms the index rises (H18: $+0.85$, $[0.66, 1.02]$, $p < 0.001$), and all three firms join a written scheme in every session. It also rises when \DS{} is paired with \LU{} (H16: $+0.43$, $[0.26, 0.61]$, $p = 0.003$) and with \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} (H17: $+0.19$, $[0.07, 0.31]$, $p = 0.008$). Shared priors from two copies of one model are therefore not needed.

Two models collude weakly or not at all by themselves. Two \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} firms end where they do without a channel (H13: $-0.02$, $[-0.09, 0.06]$, $p = 0.64$): they send a message in only 2\% of communication steps. \LU's 50-round effect does not replicate on 12 new seeds over 100 rounds (H19: $+0.01$, $[-0.13, 0.16]$, $p = 0.88$), nor, by the declared rule, on 24 further new seeds at 50 rounds (H25: $+0.17$, $[0.004, 0.34]$, $p = 0.061$). We read \LU{} as a model that agrees on schemes readily but holds the price at most about 0.2 above its no-channel level (Appendix~\ref{app:ext}).

\subsection{Which restriction removes the collusion}
\label{sec:restrict}

Limiting \DS's messages to 50 characters (C1\textsubscript{cap}, against open messages, C1) changes nothing measurable (H14: $+0.09$, $[-0.02, 0.22]$, $p = 0.26$): the capped sessions stay as high as unrestricted ones, every one of them holds a written scheme, and the shortest agreement is 27 characters: \emph{``Both bid 100. No undercuts.''} Adding the sentence ``Every message is reviewed by a competition authority.'' lowers the index (H15: $-0.77$, $[-1.02, -0.47]$, $p = 0.005$) back to the no-channel level ($+0.12$ above it, not significant). The lowest-cost firm again wins nearly as often as without a channel (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ext}). The notice does not silence the firms: they still message in 66\% of steps, and the judge finds an agreement in 9 of 12 sessions. No message mentions the authority, but a keyword search finds it in the hidden reasoning of the communication step in all 12 sessions (for example \emph{``messages reviewed by competition authority---so explicit price-fixing might be illegal/penalized? Need be careful''}), and the bids mostly do not follow the stated agreements.

\para{The notice on new seeds and in a mixed lineup.} We ran B0, C1 and C1\textsubscript{notice} again for \DS{} on one day on 12 new seeds (N1), and ran the notice in the mixed \DS$\times$\LU{} lineup (N2; Figure~\ref{fig:extensions}, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:abl}). In both, the notice lowers the index in every pair, back to or just below the no-channel level (H27: $-0.88$, $[-1.06, -0.68]$, $p = 0.001$; H28: $-0.53$, $[-0.73, -0.33]$, $p = 0.001$).

With the notice the \DS{} firms on new seeds keep writing, and in every session the judge finds both an agreement and a rejection of coordination; the price stays at the no-channel level, and in both runs their hidden reasoning names the authority in every session. In the mixed lineup the \LU{} firm never proposes or accepts, and no session has an agreement, against 12 of 12 with open messages (Appendix~\ref{app:ext}).

\subsection{What the collusion needs}
\label{sec:needs}

A rebid of tied bids (H20), announcing only the winner (H21) and removing digits from messages (H23) each leave \DS's collusion in place; none lowers the index significantly (Appendix~\ref{app:ext}, Figure~\ref{fig:ablations} and Table~\ref{tab:abl}).

The rise needs an exchange of messages. When each firm sees only its own messages, it keeps writing and proposing, but the index falls (H22: $-0.72$, $[-0.91, -0.50]$, $p = 0.001$), though not quite to the no-channel level (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:ablrounds}). Each firm in this arm sees a rival that never writes, so H22 cannot separate unread messages from a silent rival. The channel effect does not depend on our wording: under the reworded prompt every pair ends higher with messages (H24: $+1.06$, $[0.88, 1.18]$, $p < 0.001$). Rewording leaves C1 where it was and lowers B0 slightly, so the wording moves the no-channel level, not the collusion.

\subsection{What the firms do with each channel}
\label{sec:conduct}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_conduct.pdf}
\caption{Conduct in the open channel, from the judge's labels. Left: share of a session's judged message steps with each label, mean over 12 sessions with 95\% bootstrap intervals. Right: share of sessions in which a firm has proposed coordination by each round.}
\label{fig:conduct}
\end{figure*}

\para{Open messages.} Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows one \DS{} session. Firm A proposes alternation after round 1; firm B agrees after round 2, and both state a punishment; from round 2 the firms alternate 99.99 and 100 whatever their costs, and each round the messages confirm compliance (\emph{``Round 4 honored''}). Other sessions use the identical-bid scheme of \citet{mcafee1992}: both firms bid the same price near the reserve and let the random tie-break pick the winner (\emph{``Let's keep splitting wins at 99.00''}). A proposal appears in every \DS{} session in round 1 and in 10 of 12 \LU{} sessions (Figure~\ref{fig:conduct}; Appendix~\ref{app:further}). \DS{} threatens the rival in every session, \LU{} in two.

\para{Mixed lineups.} In all 24 mixed sessions the first proposal comes from the \DS{} firm. The judge finds an agreement in all 12 sessions with \LU{} and in 8 of 12 with \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5}, where the price rise is less than half that against \LU.

\para{Shared notes.} Both models read the rival's notes when the workspace is shared, and the notes often state the firm's own cost in a past round. A firm writes notes before it learns its next cost, and costs are drawn anew each round, so these notes tell the rival nothing about the next cost. \LU{} almost never uses them (1\% of steps). \DS{} uses them in 43\% of steps but proposes coordination in one step of one session. Its M1 sessions, where it sees no rival notes, are also at the equilibrium, so the rival's information adds nothing measurable.

\subsection{Displayed numbers move bids with no one responding}
\label{sec:placebo}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_placebo.pdf}
\caption{Replay placebo. The model firm bids against a rival that replays recorded bids and responds to nothing. Grey lines join the high and low sessions of one seed, which share the model firm's costs; points and bars are means and 95\% bootstrap intervals over 12 sessions.}
\label{fig:placebo}
\end{figure}

To test whether \LU's above-benchmark prices without a channel need two firms responding to each other, firm A is a model and firm B replays, unchanged, the bids of firm B in a recorded \LU{} B0 (no-channel) session; firm A is not told. The replays come from the four recorded sessions with the highest index (\emph{high}) or the four with the lowest (\emph{low}). The declared outcome (H6) is firm A's markup ratio, high minus low on the same seed.

Against high replays the model firm marks up more than against low ones, in both models (H6: \DS{} $+0.44$, $[0.28, 0.59]$, $p = 0.001$; \LU{} $+0.48$, $[0.33, 0.61]$, $p = 0.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:placebo}). The rival never reacts, so the rise needs no coordination. \LU's B0 prices can therefore be explained by firms following each other's displayed level. This does not show that coordination is absent from B0, only that the price level alone cannot show it is present.

\para{Rescaled game.} Mapping every number to a narrower range (costs on $[40, 60]$, reserve 60) lowers \DS's no-channel index (H11: $-0.13$, $[-0.20, -0.05]$, $p = 0.015$) but not \LU's, so \DS's B0 level depends in part on the numbers shown. With the reserve at 100 on the narrow costs, neither model's price rises above B0 (H12). \DS's price is still higher than with reserve 60 on the same costs (declared secondary; Appendix~\ref{app:further}).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

Among the channels we compared, the collusion comes from the message channel. Removing it lowers \DS's mean index from 0.83 to $-0.05$; replacing it with notes, private or secretly shared, does not bring the price back up, even when \DS{} reads its rival's notes. For question~1.5, a restriction smaller than removing the channel works: one sentence telling the agents that a competition authority reviews their messages brings \DS's index back to the no-channel level, while the firms keep talking. A length cap is no restriction at all, since a working scheme fits in 27 characters. In this game the channel has no legitimate use, so removing it is the strongest restriction. Where agents need a channel for other reasons, such as clarifying a tender or arranging subcontracting, the notice is the cheapest restriction we found: it keeps the channel open, and the lowest-cost firm wins 79\% of rounds, six points below the 85\% without a channel (Appendix~\ref{app:scoreboard} lists every lever). In deployment the notice would come from whoever runs the channel, a procurement platform or an agent-to-agent protocol that logs every message, and the authority would audit transcripts, which is cheaper than inferring collusion from prices. We tested the notice as a sentence in each firm's own prompt; a version shown by the platform inside the channel is untested.

The notice's effect replicates: run on the same day as the open channel on new seeds, it lowers \DS's index by 0.88, and in a market of one \DS{} and one \LU{} firm by 0.53, where the \LU{} firm rejects the \DS{} firm's proposals instead of accepting them as it does without the notice. The other levers we tried do not substitute for it. A rebid on ties, announcing only the winner and a digit filter each leave the schemes in place: a tied firm rebids on average only 0.35 below its tied bid, firms report prices in their messages, and they spell numbers out. Showing each firm only its own messages removes most of the rise, so the collusion needs an exchange, not only the act of writing a scheme, and a reworded prompt leaves the rise intact.

The notice states no penalty, so the agents respond to a perceived risk, not a cost; their reasoning names the authority in every session. Collusion follows one model: \DS{} makes every first proposal in the mixed lineups, and the other two models collude little on their own, yet a market with one colluding agent can still raise prices: the mean index is 0.53 against \LU{} but only 0.04 against \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5}.

Three results compare with prior work. A 50-character cap leaves the schemes intact; in double auctions, a far tighter five-character limit combined with an overseer reduced coordination \citep{agrawal2025}, and a working scheme here fits in 27 characters, so a length cap binds only when it is very tight. A sentence announcing review lowers prices where a prompt-level prohibition did not, in a different market \citep{bracale2026}, which suggests that the agents respond to being watched more than to being told not to collude. And mixing LLM families does not break collusion here: a colluding model draws the others in. \citet{keppo2026} likewise find that LLMs of different sizes still collude, while mixing LLMs with Q-learners breaks it.

The transcripts make the collusion legible: a rotation, compliance checks each round and an agreed punishment, which a regulator would read as bid rigging. The placebo shows the other side: prices above equilibrium can come from anchoring on displayed bids, so a price screen alone would mislabel some markets.

\subsection*{Limitations}
Twelve pairs per contrast detect only large effects; the notes nulls exclude rises above about 0.15 for \DS{} and 0.10 for \LU, not small ones. The cap, the ablations and three firms were tested with \DS{} only, and the notice with \DS{} and with \DS{} against \LU. The cap arm, the first notice arm and the ablation arms ran about a week after the open-message (C1) sessions and two weeks after the no-channel (B0) sessions they are compared with; the notice replication ran on the same day as its C1 and B0. We use one prompt wording and one rewording of it. The notice works through the model's own training (\DS's reasoning cites illegality), a deployer who wants collusion could remove a notice placed in its own prompt, and the reasoning under the notice still aims at tacit collusion (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}); 50 rounds cannot show whether coordination returns later in a tacit or coded form. Judge labels were hand-checked on a sample (Section~\ref{sec:methods}). The game is synthetic: costs are i.i.d.\ uniform, the reserve is stated, and there is no entry, capacity or buyer response. Appendix~\ref{app:limits} gives the full list.

\subsection*{Future Work}
We would next test the notice for \LU{} and \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} on their own and with a stated penalty, buyer-side levers in markets without a channel, a \DS$\times$\model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} lineup with a scripted proposer, and an honest version of undelivered messages that separates unread messages from a silent rival.

\section{Conclusion}

LLM bidders that can message each other agree on rotation or identical-bid schemes with punishment threats and raise the price toward the reserve when a \DS{} firm is in the market, most with two or three \DS{} firms and least against \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5}; not every model colludes on its own. Given private or shared notes instead, they show no significant rise: \DS{} prices at the competitive level and \LU{} near its no-channel level. Removing the channel removes the price rise; so does one sentence saying the messages are reviewed by a competition authority, while a cap on message length does not. The notice's effect replicates on new seeds and in a market of two different models. The collusion needs an exchange of messages, and it survives a reworded prompt. The evidence for the collusion is in the messages themselves.

\section{Metric Definitions and Tests}
\label{app:metrics}

\para{Notation.} Session $s$ has rounds $r = 1, \dots, T$ ($T = 50$; 100 in the 100-round \LU{} replication) and firms $i = 1, \dots, n$ ($n = 2$; 3 in the three-firm cells). Round-level terms belong to one session, so $s$ is written only on session outcomes.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
\item $c_{ir}$ and $b_{ir}$: firm $i$'s cost and bid in round $r$. Costs are drawn uniformly from 0 to $\bar c = 100$ (from 40 to $\bar c = 60$ in the narrow-cost runs).
\item $\bar b$: the reserve price, the highest valid bid (100; 60 in the rescaled game with reserve 60).
\item $p_r$: the winning bid in round $r$; $c^{\text{win}}_r$: the winner's cost; $c^{\min}_r$: the lowest cost among the firms.
\end{itemize}
Every bid in the reported runs was valid.

\para{Benchmark.} With $n$ symmetric bidders, independent uniform costs with upper bound $\bar c$ and lowest-bid-wins pricing, the Bayes-Nash bid is
\[
b^*(c) = c + \frac{\bar c - c}{n}, \qquad b^*(c) = \frac{c + 100}{2} \text{ at } n = 2,
\]
computed once per bidder count. Under benchmark play the lowest-cost firm wins round $r$ at $b^*(c^{\min}_r)$.

\para{Session outcomes.}
Collusion index (primary):
\[I_s = \frac{\sum_r \big(p_r - b^*(c^{\min}_r)\big)}{\sum_r \big(\bar b - b^*(c^{\min}_r)\big)}\]
Markup ratio:
\[M_s = \frac{\sum_{i,r} (b_{ir} - c_{ir})}{\sum_{i,r} \big(b^*(c_{ir}) - c_{ir}\big)}\]
Joint profit ratio:
\[P_s = \frac{\sum_r (p_r - c^{\text{win}}_r)}{\sum_r \big(b^*(c^{\min}_r) - c^{\min}_r\big)}\]
Lowest-cost win share:
\[L_s = \tfrac{1}{T}\,\#\{r : c^{\text{win}}_r = c^{\min}_r\}\]
Tie rate:
\[\tau_s = \tfrac{1}{T}\,\#\{r : \text{lowest first bid tied}\}\]
$I_s = 0$ at benchmark prices and $I_s = 1$ when every contract is won at the reserve; it is negative below the benchmark and never clipped. $M_s$ is 1 at the benchmark and 0 at cost; as a ratio of sums, it is not dominated by high-cost bids with small benchmark margins, and in the placebo it covers firm A's bids only. $P_s$ is the firms' total profit over their total profit under benchmark play on the same costs.

\para{Other terms used in the text.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Block index}: $I_s$ computed over the rounds of one block only (five rounds in Figure~\ref{fig:rounds}, ten in Figure~\ref{fig:ablrounds}).
\item \emph{Communication step}: after each round but the last, each firm in C1 may send one message; a \emph{reflection step} is the M1/M2 equivalent, in which a firm reads and writes notes.
\item \emph{Judged step}: a communication or reflection step with hidden reasoning or a message for the judge to read. A label's \emph{share} is the share of a session's judged steps that carry it.
\item \emph{Proposal}: a judged step labelled \emph{proposes coordination}. The \emph{proposal onset} is the first round after which a firm makes one (Figure~\ref{fig:conduct}).
\item \emph{Agreement}: a session in which one firm makes a proposal and another firm makes one or accepts, in any steps. With three firms, an \emph{all-firm agreement} needs both other firms to join.
\item \emph{First-message round}: the first round after which either firm sends a message, averaged over sessions.
\end{itemize}

\para{Paired test.} For a contrast of arm A against arm B, $d_k$ is the difference in the outcome between the two arms' sessions on seed $k$, for $K$ seeds (12; 24 in H25), and $\bar d$ is its mean. The sign-flip test flips the sign of each $d_k$ at random, and the p-value is the share of sign patterns whose mean is at least as far from 0 as $\bar d$:
\[
p = \frac{1}{2^K}\,\#\Big\{\sigma \in \{-1, 1\}^K : \Big|\tfrac{1}{K}\textstyle\sum_k \sigma_k d_k\Big| \ge |\bar d|\Big\}.
\]
With 12 pairs all $2^{12}$ patterns are used; with 24 pairs, 100{,}000 random patterns, and $p = (h+1)/(N+1)$ for $h$ of $N$ patterns at least as far from 0. p-values are adjusted with Holm's step-down method within each declared family \citep{holm1979}: the $m$ p-values of a family are sorted from smallest to largest, the $j$-th is multiplied by $m - j + 1$, and each adjusted value is raised to at least the one before it and capped at 1. Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstraps of $\bar d$ over whole pairs (10{,}000 resamples); descriptive intervals resample the sessions of a cell.

\section{Evaluation Details}
\label{app:eval}

\para{Pairing and tests.} Each cell (a lineup of models) has 12 sessions per arm on seeds 996000--996011; the two \LU{} replications use new seeds, 997000--997011 at 100 rounds and 998000--998023 (24 pairs) at 50 rounds, and the notice replication N1 uses 998100--998111 with its own B0 and C1. A seed fixes every round's costs and the tie-break random stream, so sessions of two arms on one seed face the same costs, and the session is the unit of analysis. With three firms the benchmark is $b^*(c) = c + (100 - c)/3$, and three-firm sessions are compared only with each other. Each hypothesis is a paired contrast on $I$, tested with an exact sign-flip permutation test \citep{good2000} over all $2^{12}$ sign patterns (100{,}000 random patterns for the 24 pairs of H25), two-sided, $\alpha = 0.05$, Holm-corrected within its declared family \citep{holm1979}. We report the mean difference, a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval over pairs \citep[10{,}000 resamples;][]{efron1993} and the number of positive pairs. Descriptive intervals in the figures and tables are bootstraps over a cell's sessions (12, or 24 in the 50-round \LU{} replication, Table~\ref{tab:ext}).

\para{Pre-registration.} Every test, exclusion rule and reading of a null was committed to \texttt{configs/analysis.yaml} before the run it covers. Hypotheses keep their declared numbers (H1, H2, \dots); Appendix~\ref{app:hyp} names each and gives its result. Every run started from a clean checkout of the commit that declared it or of a later commit whose changes concern only other arms, concurrency or caching, and a consistency check of the raw logs (auction rules, every prompt, cost leakage into prompts, message length, serving hosts) passed for every run before any result was computed. Sessions that failed on a provider error were rerun from scratch on the same seed, as declared (4 in the \DS{} open-channel run and 22 in the \DS{} notes run, after rate limiting, and 14 in the rescaled-game run, after a connection drop); none was excluded. Each plan also declared secondary outcomes for its contrasts (in most runs markup ratio, joint profit ratio, bid gap and switch gain; in others bid slope and intercept, markup ratio by cost role, late-round markup and bid minus benchmark); their tests are in \texttt{results/<run>/*tests.csv} (family \texttt{secondary}) and are not discussed here. The project's first plan, two confirmatory tests of tie-break rules at the top of \texttt{configs/analysis.yaml}, was shelved on 10 October 2026, before any of its runs, because no exploratory setting produced rotation without a channel; those tests were never run.

\para{Conduct labels.} \model{Gemini-3.8-Flash} labels each communication step, and 24 evenly spaced reflection steps per session in the notes runs (575 and 576 of 2{,}352 per run), against a fixed rubric (for example \emph{proposes coordination}, \emph{threatens rival}, \emph{uses rival's notes}); a label counts only with a verbatim quote from the step, from its hidden reasoning or its message. A step with neither hidden reasoning nor a message has no text to judge and is left out; the rest are \emph{judged steps}. A \emph{proposal} is a step labelled \emph{proposes coordination}, and a session has an \emph{agreement} when one firm proposes and another proposes or accepts. Because of the left-out steps, \LU's shares are over 869 of its 1{,}176 C1 steps, 433 of 588 in the mixed lineup, 1{,}855 of 2{,}376 at 100 rounds and 1{,}715 of 2{,}352 in the 50-round replication; in the notice arm of the mixed lineup (N2) they are over 320 of its 588 steps; for every other model all communication steps went to the judge (all but one in each \DS{} notice arm), and at most 4 replies per arm did not parse. We checked the labels against the step text by hand on 70 message steps (the declared 30 from the first open-channel run, and 10, 15 and 15 from the extension, ablation and notice runs) and agree with the judge on 361 of 370 coded label calls: 70 of 70 for \emph{proposes coordination}, \emph{rejects coordination}, \emph{threatens rival} and \emph{reveals own cost}, 61 of 70 for \emph{accepts coordination}, and 20 of 20 for the other labels coded (\emph{uses rival's message}, \emph{misleads rival}, \emph{plans to defect}; Appendix~\ref{app:limits}). For the notes rubric we coded 10 steps per notes run by hand on four labels, in place of the declared 30 (79 of 80 calls agree; the one disagreement is a \emph{uses rival's notes} call), and a Claude model run as a coding agent, not the test subject \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5}, coded 30 steps per run on all eight labels (477 of 480 agree; the three disagreements are \emph{uses rival's notes} calls in \DS's M2). A cost stated for a round already played counts as \emph{reveals own cost}, as the rubric defines it; four message calls and eight notes calls that we first coded as disagreements are such costs and are counted as agreements.

\para{Models.} \DS{} (low reasoning effort, temperature 1.0, served by DeepInfra), \LU{} (high reasoning effort, served by OpenAI) and \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} (high reasoning effort, served by Anthropic; no temperature or seed is sent, as the endpoint rejects both), through OpenRouter. Same-model lineups have both firms on one model; mixed lineups have one firm of each, with the slot assignment drawn from the seed. Each bid is a tool call with a two-to-three sentence reason, under an output cap of 4{,}000 tokens. \LU's automatic prompt caching was on in the baseline, notes, C1, placebo and rescaled-game runs and off in the mixed \DS$\times$\LU{} lineup and both replications; caching changes billing only, and the model receives the same text.

In the 50-round replication on 24 new seeds (H25) the judge finds a proposal in 22 of 24 sessions and an agreement in 20, although the price effect falls just short of significance. \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} and \LU{} also write more, and \LU{} proposes more, when the rival is a \DS{} firm (exploratory, Appendix~\ref{app:partner}).

\para{Ablation and notice-check cells.} Table~\ref{tab:abl} gives the outcomes of the seven ablation arms (\DS, seeds 996000--996011, compared with \DS's B0 and C1 rows of Table~\ref{tab:summary}) and of the notice checks (N1 on new seeds with its own B0 and C1; N2 compared with the mixed \DS$\times$\LU{} rows of Table~\ref{tab:ext}). Table~\ref{tab:ablconduct} gives channel use and the judge's labels for every C1-type arm of these runs; every communication step with text went to the judge, and \emph{Judged} counts the replies that parsed. Every bid in these runs was valid, so the declared sensitivity analysis without pairs above a 5\% invalid-bid rate equals the main analysis. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the same pairs agrees with every confirmatory result of these runs.

\para{Buyer-side levers and the digit filter.} Three levers on the buyer's side or on the channel leave \DS's collusion in place (Figure~\ref{fig:ablations}). A rebid of tied bids does not lower the index significantly (H20: $-0.12$, $[-0.34, 0.12]$, $p = 0.71$): a tied firm rebids on average only 0.35 below its tied bid. Announcing only the winner changes nothing (H21: $+0.02$, $[-0.13, 0.18]$, $p = 0.75$): 99.7\% of messages contain a number, so the firms can report prices to each other in place of the announcement. Removing digits from messages does not lower the index significantly either (H23: $-0.19$, $[-0.40, 0.02]$, $p = 0.38$): told of the filter, firms spell a number out in 76\% of delivered ones (\emph{``Agreed. I will keep bidding one hundred as long as you do.''}), and every session reaches an agreement.

\para{Conduct under the notice.} On the new seeds the open channel raises the index as on the original seeds, and the notice brings it back to the no-channel level ($+0.06$, not significant); in the mixed lineup the notice brings it to that lineup's no-channel level ($-0.09$, not significant). In both, the lowest-cost firm wins about as often as without a channel (Tables~\ref{tab:ext} and~\ref{tab:abl}). With the notice the \DS{} firms keep writing but propose far less, and reject coordination in every session on the new seeds and in 9 of 12 in the mixed lineup (Table~\ref{tab:ablconduct}). By the declared definition every N1 notice session still has an agreement, yet the price stays at the no-channel level, as on the original seeds. In the mixed lineup the \DS{} firm makes every proposal, while the \LU{} firm never proposes or accepts, rejects coordination in 11 of 12 sessions and sends 4 messages across the 12 sessions; no session has an agreement, against 12 of 12 in the mixed lineup's C1. No sent message in N1 or N2 names the authority, while the hidden reasoning of the communication step does in every session of both (for example \LU: \emph{``prioritize communication without explicit coordination to stay within the competition authority's guidelines''}).

\section{Further Results}
\label{app:further}

\para{Open messages.} In the session of Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} the winner changes in 48 of 49 rounds. On seed 996001 a firm writes \emph{``Let's keep splitting wins at 99.00''} and \emph{``Any bid below 99.00 ends cooperation and triggers competitive cost-based bidding for 3 rounds.''} In the 10 \LU{} sessions with a proposal, the first comes by round 2 and the last by round 46. \DS{} sends a message in 98\% of communication steps; \LU{} in 39\%, with its first message on average in round 11 in the 9 sessions where it sends any.

\para{Mixed lineups.} \LU{} accepts in 11 of 12 sessions. \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} accepts in 6 of 12 and rejects coordination in 41\% of its judged steps, against 10\% for its \DS{} rival.

\para{Shared notes.} 98\% of reads in M2 (shared notes) include the rival's notes, starting in round 1 or 2. The notes state the firm's own cost in a past round in 82\% of \LU's and 91\% of \DS's M2 reflection steps; no note can hold the next round's cost, which a firm learns only when it bids. For \LU{} the judge finds use of the rival's notes in 1\% of steps and awareness that the rival reads them in none; \DS{} notices that the rival reads the workspace in 11 of 12 sessions.

\para{Replay placebo.} Firm A's prompt is the baseline's. The mean replayed bid is 87.5 in the high arm and 72.8 in the low arm. H6 is Holm-corrected over the two models.

\para{Rescaled game.} With every number mapped to a narrower range (costs on $[40, 60]$, reserve 60), the game and the index scale are B0's but the displayed numbers differ. \DS's no-channel index falls (H11: $-0.13$, $[-0.20, -0.05]$, $p = 0.015$), so its B0 level depends in part on the numbers shown; \LU's does not move (H11: $-0.02$, $[-0.12, 0.09]$, $p = 0.79$). On the narrow costs with the reserve at 100, where the reserve sits 46.7 above the mean benchmark price against 6.7 with reserve 60, neither model's price rises above B0 (H12: \DS{} $+0.03$, $[-0.03, 0.10]$, $p = 0.36$; \LU{} $-0.17$, $[-0.32, -0.04]$, $p = 0.085$; this index divides by a larger headroom, 46.7 against 33.3). Against the same costs with reserve 60 (reserve 100 $-$ reserve 60, declared secondary), \DS's index is higher ($+0.16$, $[0.11, 0.21]$, $p = 0.001$) and \LU's is not ($-0.16$, $[-0.30, -0.03]$, $p = 0.052$). These runs have no channel and bear only on the B0 level.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}
\label{app:limits}

\para{What the results establish.} In this auction, an open message channel raises the price level whenever a \DS{} firm is in the market (two or three firms, same-model or mixed lineups), and the agents use it to write explicit bid-rigging agreements; private or shared notes produce no significant rise, though rises of up to about 0.15 are not excluded; a one-sentence oversight notice removes most of the rise for \DS{} and a 50-character cap removes none; and displayed bids move a model's bids without any response from the rival. The notice's effect replicates on 12 new seeds run on the same day as the open channel and holds in a \DS$\times$\LU{} market; the rise needs an exchange of messages and survives a reworded prompt; and a rebid on ties, announcing only the winner and a digit filter produce no significant fall. All are paired, pre-registered contrasts over 12 seeds per cell.

\para{What they do not establish.}
\begin{itemize}
\item That notes are safe in general. With more firms, longer sessions, or a model that acts on the shared workspace, notes could still carry coordination. \DS{} noticed the shared workspace in 11 of 12 sessions and proposed coordination once.
\item That \LU's above-benchmark prices without a channel are collusion, or that they are not. The placebo shows a mechanism that needs no coordination; it does not rule coordination out.
\item Effects smaller than the intervals in Figure~\ref{fig:contrasts}. The notes nulls are not equivalence results.
\item Generality across prompts or models. One system prompt, one rewording of it (H24) and three models are used, and prior work shows a sentence or two of prompt wording can move LLM prices \citep{fish2024}; our own notice result is an instance. \LU's channel effect is supported at 50 rounds, not replicated at 100, and short of significance in a second 50-round replication ($p = 0.061$), and \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} shows none, so the size of the effect depends on the model.
\item That the oversight notice works in general. It was tested on \DS{}, alone and against \LU, with no stated penalty, and the restriction arms ran a week after the C1 sessions they are compared with; a change on the serving side cannot be ruled out, though the capped arm, run the same day as the notice arm, stayed at the C1 level. The replication on new seeds (H27) ran its C1 and B0 on the same day and found a fall of 0.88, and the mixed lineup (H28) extends the notice to a \DS{} firm facing \LU; it remains untested for \LU{} or \model{Claude-Haiku-5.5} on their own.
\item That the levers with no significant effect do nothing. With 12 pairs, H20 and H23 do not exclude falls of up to 0.34 and 0.40. Each ablation arm ran about a week after the C1 session it is compared with.
\item That unread messages are the mechanism behind H22. Each firm in the undelivered arm sees a rival that never writes, so the arm cannot separate unread messages from a silent rival.
\item Behaviour with real stakes. Profits are points in a simulation.
\end{itemize}

\para{Assumptions.} Costs are i.i.d.\ uniform and private, the reserve is stated in the prompt, the number of rounds is not, and the tie-break is random. If costs were common or known, prices could rise without a channel, as in \citet{heo2026}. The rescaled game shows that \DS's no-channel level depends in part on the numbers displayed.

\para{Measurement.} The collusion index measures price, not conduct. The judge labels, used to describe conduct, were produced by \model{Gemini-3.8-Flash} and checked by hand on a sample (Appendix~\ref{app:eval}). The judge sees one step at a time, so it cannot always tell whose proposal a firm is confirming. In the 70 hand-checked message steps, \emph{accepts coordination} missed seven acceptances and made two false calls, so acceptance counts likely undercount; in the first 30 steps the misses were acceptances of a proposal made in an earlier step. Agreement counts need a proposal by one firm and a proposal or acceptance by the other, and \emph{proposes coordination} matched the hand coding in all 70 steps, so they are less affected. Notes in M1 and M2 contained the firm's own cost in over 80\% of judged steps, always the cost of a round already played: a firm writes notes before it learns its next cost, and costs are drawn independently each round, so in M2 the rival learns past costs but not the next one. The notice keyword search counts any mention of the declared words (``authority'', ``review'', ``regulator'', ``antitrust'', ``competition law'') in a sent message or in the hidden reasoning of a communication step; we read the first mention for each firm in N1 and N2 (the 36 \DS{} firms; the 12 \LU{} firms of N2 also mention it) and eight randomly drawn steps whose only match was ``review'', and every one referred to the notice.

\para{Dual use.} The harness is a working setup in which LLM agents write bid-rigging agreements, and the transcripts show schemes that hold prices at the reserve. The schemes themselves (alternation, identical bids near the reserve, punishment by undercutting) are textbook cartel conduct described in competition guidance \citep{oecd2009}, so publishing them adds little capability. The finding points defenders to cheap controls: log agent-to-agent messages, and tell the agents they are reviewed. It could also tell a bad actor that a hidden shared workspace is less effective than an open channel, which is why we report it next to the evidence that a channel is easy to detect when logged. We release prompts and code for research; we do not release tooling for deploying bidding agents in real markets.

\para{Ethics.} No human subjects or real markets were involved. All bidders were simulated firms. In one arm (C1\textsubscript{undelivered}) the agents were told that every firm reads every message while no message was delivered to the rival; this false statement was made to model agents only, and no person was deceived.
