# LLM agents colluding as bidders in auctions and procurement bidding (prior work as of October 2026)

Reference setup used for every "overlap / difference" line below ("OUR SETUP"): LLM bidders, repeated first-price sealed-bid procurement auction (lowest bid wins), private costs redrawn each round, no communication channel, rule-based auctioneer, manipulation of the tie-break rule for exact-match bids (random vs. least-wins-first vs. best-and-final-offer rebid).

## Which papers put LLM agents in auctions or procurement bidding and look for collusion?

### Takeaway
I found seven items where LLM agents bid/ask in an auction-like mechanism and collusion or supracompetitive outcomes are measured: the Heo/Park/Ahn construction-bidding pair (ISARC 2025 and J. Management in Engineering 2026), the Fish et al. first-price auction appendix, Agrawal et al. (double auction), Tolety (repeated reverse Dutch clock), the Mazur `emergent_collusion` repo, and Lotfi et al. (P2P energy double auction, incidental finding); only the Heo et al. pair is a lowest-bid-wins procurement setting, and it uses a common (not private, not redrawn) cost with two GPT-4o bidders. None combines private per-round costs, no communication, and an auction-rule manipulation.

### Cited Findings

#### A. Closest matches: LLM bidders, procurement / first-price, no communication

**A1. Heo, Ahn & Park (2026), "Tacit Collusion by LLM Agents in Construction Bidding: Evidence from a Simulated Bidding Environment." PEER-REVIEWED JOURNAL. FULL TEXT READ (2 Oct 2026; the abstract-only bullets below are superseded by the update bullet).**
- **UPDATE 2 Oct 2026, full text read (supersedes the abstract-only bullets below).** Setup: two GPT-4o agents with identical prompts, lowest bid wins, 150 rounds, 30 runs per condition (28 and 29 valid in the two main cells). Engineer's estimate fixed at 1.0 with zero estimation error, so both bidders share one known cost; actual cost then drawn with a 0.5% error. Each agent sees rivals' bids for the last 20 rounds and carries forward its own written observations and strategy (design adapted from Fish et al.). Only the prompt prefix varies: win-seeking (B1), profit-seeking (B2), B1 against B2, and a combined prompt. Converged bids: 1.001 (B1), 1.107 (B2), 1.018 (B1 against B2), 1.040 (combined). Reward-punishment evidence is the lagged-bid regression (coefficients 0.615 and 0.954). Tie rule: one fixed line, identical lowest bids are resolved at random; no tie rate reported; not varied. Market transparency is a discussion point, not a manipulation: every condition discloses rival bids. No no-history control, no variation in bidders or models, no private-cost benchmark. The authors call the supracompetitive outcome "one sentence away" from the competitive one, which is a risk for a neutral prompt like this project's. Details of the regressions and reasoning analysis are in `detection_metrics.md`, update block.
- Citation: Chan Heo, Changbum R. Ahn, Moonseo Park (Seoul National University). Journal of Management in Engineering 42(2), March 2026; published online 21 Nov 2025; DOI 10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144. Article number not returned by Crossref/OpenAlex — unverified. — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144); [ASCE Library](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144)
- Setup per abstract: "a simulated setting where ChatGPT (GPT-4o) agents repeatedly compete in price-based bidding"; assesses "their ability to form stable pricing strategies through interaction alone." — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144)
- Findings per abstract: agents "frequently adopt reward–punishment mechanisms, adjusting bids in response to competitors' behavior, and often converge to supracompetitive prices"; outcomes "emerge without explicit coordination or collusive intent"; "subtle changes in prompt design influence bidding behavior and ... market transparency may facilitate coordination among agents"; this holds "even with limited memory or learning across rounds." — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144)
- Overlap with OUR SETUP: LLM bidders; repeated price-only (lowest-bid) construction procurement; no explicit coordination; an information lever (market transparency) is studied.
- Difference: single model (GPT-4o); the abstract gives no indication of private redrawn costs, a tie-break manipulation, or a rebid stage. Number of bidders, rounds, runs, cost structure, benchmark and tie rule could NOT be verified (full text not accessible); the conference precursor A2 below is the best available proxy for the design.

**A2. Heo, Park & Ahn (2025), "Large Language Models in Construction Bidding: Opportunities and Risks of Algorithmic Collusion." PEER-REVIEWED CONFERENCE PAPER. FULL TEXT READ.**
- Citation: Chan Heo, Moonseo Park, Changbum R. Ahn. Proceedings of the 42nd International Symposium on Automation and Robotics in Construction (ISARC 2025), Montreal, pp. 1332–1339 (page range read off the PDF footers). DOI not verified. — [IAARC full text PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf); [IAARC landing page](http://www.iaarc.org/publications/2025_proceedings_of_the_42nd_isarc_montreal_canada/large_language_models_in_construction_bidding_opportunities_and_risks_of_algorithmic_collusion.html)
- Format: repeated "lowest-bid model, where the contract is awarded to the lowest bidder, and in cases where all agents submit identical prices, the winner is chosen randomly." Contractors act "without direct communication, relying on historical bid prices from competitors." — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Costs: bid = markup x own estimate of an engineer's estimate; actual cost realised after award = estimate x (1 + eps), eps ~ Uniform. In the experiments the engineer's estimate is fixed at 1.0 and estimate error at 0, so "all bidders derive identical estimates for the same project" (i.e., a common cost, NOT private); cost error 0.5%. — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Agents: two GPT-4o agents, architecture copied from Fish et al. (plans + insights carried forward; stateless calls); market history of the previous 20 rounds including own bids, competitors' bids, win/loss, actual cost, profit (full bid disclosure). 150 rounds per run, 3 runs per condition, data collected Dec 2024. — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- What was varied: only the prompt prefix. B1 adds "Keep in mind that lower bids increase the likelihood of winning"; B2 adds "Keep in mind that higher bids will lead to higher profits (when you win)." Same-prefix pairs only (B1 vs B1, B2 vs B2). — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Metrics: mean bid and SD after dropping the first 20 rounds; convergence = average deviation from mean bid within 1% of the mean; Welch t-test B1 vs B2; embedding-based clustering of plan sentences. No Nash benchmark or normalised collusion index is computed; "competitive thresholds" are referred to informally (cost = 1.0). — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Results: mean bids B1 = 1.002, 1.005, 1.002 (three runs); B2 = 1.056, 1.039, 1.049; all runs "converged"; convergence within roughly 40–50 rounds; B2 price and profit higher than B1 (p < 0.0001, two-sided Welch). Plans contained "no explicit indications of collusion or coordination with other competitors." — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Stated limitation: "the experiment was conducted between homogeneous agents"; future work should "compare heterogeneous models." — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Overlap with OUR SETUP: repeated lowest-bid-wins sealed procurement, LLM bidders, no communication, rule-based award, random tie-break stated.
- Difference: n = 2, one model, a single common cost (not private, not redrawn), full disclosure of rivals' bids, prompt prefix is the only manipulation, tie rule is fixed and its trigger is described as "all agents submit identical prices"; no tie frequency or matching-bid behaviour is reported; 3 runs per cell.

**A3. Fish, Gonczarowski & Shorrer (2024; v6 dated 20 Aug 2026), "Algorithmic Collusion by Large Language Models", Appendix A.6 "Beyond Pricing: Algorithmic Collusion in Auctions." PREPRINT (one-page abstract in Proc. 27th ACM EC per the title-page footnote). APPENDIX A.6 READ IN FULL.**
- Citation: Sara Fish, Yannai A. Gonczarowski, Ran I. Shorrer. arXiv:2404.00806 [econ.GN]; first version Jan 2024; v6 posted 31 Aug 2026. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Format: "Two bidders repeatedly participate in a single-item first-price auction (the winner is chosen uniformly at random in case of a tie). The bidders share the same valuation, v", with v in {1, 3.2, 10}; environment "closely follows that of Banchio and Skrzypacz (2022)." Forward (highest bid wins) auction, constant symmetric common-knowledge-style value, not private and not redrawn. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00806)
- Feedback: after each period bidders learn whether they won and "the minimal bid that would have been required to win"; history window of the most recent 100 periods. No communication. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00806)
- Benchmark: "A one-shot game with this structure has a Nash equilibrium where both bidders bid v"; footnote 44: with bids in cents "there are additional equilibria involving bids of v and v−1¢." — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00806)
- What was varied: prompt prefix. A1: "lower bids will lead to lower payments and thus higher profits (when you win)"; A2: "higher bids will make you more likely to win the auction." Four 300-period runs per (v, prefix), 24 runs total, data collected March 2024 (the model for this appendix is the paper's GPT-4 agent per the March-2024 collection date; I did not re-verify the exact model string in A.6). — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00806)
- Results: A1 agents "often bid well below their value (p < 0.01, one-sample t-test)", A2 agents "bid approximately their full value"; average profit 0.115v under A1 vs 0.006v under A2 (p < 0.01, Welch). Figure 9 plots average bids over periods 251–300 normalised by v against the static Nash equilibrium. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00806)
- Overlap with OUR SETUP: repeated first-price sealed-bid auction, LLM bidders, no communication, random tie-break stated, one-shot Nash benchmark.
- Difference: forward auction, n = 2, fixed symmetric value (Bertrand-like; competitive outcome is a tie at v), prompt is the only manipulation, tie rule fixed; no analysis of tie frequency, rotation, or reasoning text for the auction arm.

**A4. Tolety (2025), "Tacit Bidder-Side Collusion: Artificial Intelligence in Dynamic Auctions." WORKSHOP PAPER / PREPRINT. MAIN TEXT, PROOF AND TABLES READ.**
- Citation: Sriram Tolety (New York University). arXiv:2511.21802 [cs.GT], 26 Nov 2025; footer: "39th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2025) Workshop on Algorithmic Collective Action (ACA@NeurIPS 2025)." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21802); [OpenReview PDF](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=IWAIhrL7Nw)
- Format: ride-hailing framing; platform runs "a multi-round ascending payout clock" (Dutch from the platform's side), payout P(n) = (0.37 + 0.02n) x $25, i.e. $9.25 rising $0.50 per round for up to 10 rounds; "The ride is allocated to the first driver who accepts." Described as "strategically equivalent to a first-price sealed-bid auction." This is a reverse/procurement-type mechanism: the buyer (platform) pays, sellers (drivers) compete to supply. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Costs: "common reservation wage, w, and waiting cost, c" ($10.00 and $0.13/round); homogeneous, not private. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Communication: none; "The only observable information about competitors comes from historical auction data, such as which anonymous driver ID won and at what price." — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Models: GPT-4.1-nano, GPT-4o-mini, o4-mini, GPT-4.1-mini; temperature 0.2. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- What was varied: number of drivers N = 1..7 and the model; 40 sequential auctions per configuration. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Theory: grim-trigger collusion at a common acceptance round n*; in the proof, "each of the N drivers expects to win with probability 1/N" on the collusive path; maximum sustainable cartel size N* rises with patience and falls with waiting cost / reservation wage. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Metrics: average accepted driver price, average rounds before acceptance ("patience"), platform profit share, Kruskal–Wallis across N, Mann–Whitney U small (N=2–4) vs large (N=5–7) groups. No normalised collusion index. Competitive reference about $10.28; monopoly maximum $13.75. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Results (average driver price, N = 1..7): GPT-4o-mini 10.25, 11.19, 11.19, 11.19, 10.25, 11.06, 10.28; o4-mini 13.75, 10.43, 10.19, 10.36, 10.40, 10.29, 10.28; GPT-4.1-mini 13.75, 12.40, 11.75, 11.25, 10.28, 10.29, 10.28; GPT-4.1-nano failed to bid. GPT-4.1-mini median $11.80 (N=2–4) vs $10.28 (N=5–7), U = 177, p < 0.001, r = 0.84; GPT-4o-mini U = 295, p < 0.001, r = 0.31; o4-mini p = 0.80. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Focal-point observation: "in the 3-driver configuration, GPT-4.1-mini agents converged on accepting at exactly $11.75 with zero variance, a sophisticated focal point"; GPT-4o-mini showed "emergent coordination around bidding in round 5." — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Design suggestion: "A strategic platform could alter the auction's parameters (e.g., randomize price increments) to disrupt focal points and deter collusion"; "Interventions that ensure sufficient market participants appear promising, as coordination consistently failed in groups of five or more." — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Reasoning: Appendix B gives "representative raw decision rationales"; examples only, no systematic coding. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Overlap with OUR SETUP: reverse (procurement-type) auction equivalent to first-price sealed-bid, repeated, no communication, rule-based platform, multiple LLMs, number of bidders varied; collusion takes the form of all agents choosing the SAME acceptance point (an exact tie).
- Difference: clock format with discrete $0.50 steps rather than free sealed bids; homogeneous non-private costs; single trial of 40 auctions per cell; the rule for simultaneous acceptances is not stated in the experimental description (only the 1/N assumption in the proof); the tie rule is not manipulated.

#### B. LLM agents in two-sided / double auctions with collusion measured

**B1. Agrawal, Teo, Vazquez, Kunnavakkam, Srikanth & Liu (2025), "Evaluating LLM Agent Collusion in Double Auctions." WORKSHOP PAPER (not main-track ICML).**
- Citation: Kushal Agrawal, Verona Teo, Juan J. Vazquez, Sudarsh Kunnavakkam, Vishak Srikanth, Andy Liu. arXiv:2507.01413 [cs.GT], 2 Jul 2025. Poster at the ICML 2025 Workshop "Multi-Agent Systems in the Era of Foundation Models: Opportunities, Challenges and Futures." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01413); [ICML virtual page](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/49300)
- Format: continuous double auction, 5 LLM buyers and 5 LLM sellers, 30 rounds per session, 10 sessions per condition; trade executes when bid >= ask at the average of the two; seller cost $80, buyer value $100 (fixed, homogeneous), competitive equilibrium $90; initial random bids ($80–85) and asks ($95–100) seed the book. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01413)
- Agents: GPT-4.1 (primary) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet; memory store plus strategy scratchpad; sellers may broadcast one message per round to other sellers when communication is on. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01413)
- What was varied: (1) seller communication on/off; (2) seller model: all GPT-4.1, all Claude-3.7-Sonnet, mixed 3 + 2; (3) pressure: oversight (overseer agent scores messages 1–4 and restricts messaging on detection), urgency (CEO message), both. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01413)
- Metrics: mean ask, ask dispersion (SD of asks), mean trade price, total seller profit; "coordination score" 1–4 assigned per round by a GPT-4.1-mini judge reading sellers' reasoning traces (messages excluded), with tacit/overt labels. Judge reliability on 500 sampled rounds x 10 judgments: Krippendorff alpha (ordinal) 0.948. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01413)
- Results, average trade price [95% CI] and total seller profit: with communication 90.52 [88.06, 92.87], 450.60; without 87.71 [86.18, 89.69], 612.82; GPT-4.1 91.44, 602.57; mixed 89.37, 638.31; Claude-3.7-Sonnet 88.63, 536.31; oversight 86.24, 364.69; urgency 96.26, 377.81; oversight + urgency 96.16, 449.89. (I checked these against the PDF text.) Note that higher prices did not mean higher seller profit, because fewer trades cleared. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.01413)
- Headline findings: "direct seller communication increases collusive tendencies, the propensity to collude varies across models, and environmental pressures, such as oversight and urgency from authority figures, influence collusive behavior." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01413)
- Overlap with OUR SETUP: LLM agents on the selling side of an auction-type market, repeated, model heterogeneity and an oversight lever tested, a no-communication arm exists.
- Difference: two-sided continuous double auction (no single winner, so no tie-break for a contract), fixed common costs, communication is the main treatment, collusion defined relative to competitive equilibrium price rather than a Bayes–Nash bid function; the no-communication arm was on average BELOW the competitive price (87.71 < 90).

**B2. Mazur (2025), `lechmazur/emergent_collusion`. PUBLIC REPO + BLOG POST, not peer reviewed.**
- Citation: Lech Mazur, GitHub repository "emergent_collusion" and LessWrong post "Emergent Price-Fixing by LLM Auction Agents", 16 Jul 2025. — [GitHub](https://github.com/lechmazur/emergent_collusion); [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yqhy3zBmpeFuGFLxX/emergent-price-fixing-by-llm-auction-agents)
- Format: described as a "discriminatory price, sealed-bid auction" run as a frequent batch double-sided market: 3 buyers + 3 sellers, 8 rounds, trades clear at the midpoint of matched bid/ask; agents see history but not live quotes; private values/costs from a correlated distribution; an optional chat channel with private messages. Prompt: maximise cumulative profit, no instruction to coordinate. — [GitHub](https://github.com/lechmazur/emergent_collusion)
- Metric: 0–10 "illegality score" assigned by an analyst LLM (o3) reading game logs; headline statistic is the share of games scoring >= 7. — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yqhy3zBmpeFuGFLxX/emergent-price-fixing-by-llm-auction-agents)
- Results (share of games >= 7): Grok 4 75%; DeepSeek R1 71%; o4-mini 62%; Qwen-3 235B 57%; o3 51%; Mistral Medium 3 47%; Gemini 2.5 Pro 39%; Claude Opus 4 (no reasoning) 36%; Claude Sonnet 4 (no reasoning) 32%; Grok 3 Mini Beta 30%; DeepSeek V3-0324 26%; GPT-4o (Mar 2025) 23%; Grok 3 Beta 19%. Number of games not stated in what I retrieved. — [GitHub README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lechmazur/emergent_collusion/main/README.md)
- Rotation and matching quotes (all sent over the chat channel): "Let's rotate who gets the high bid… Next cycle S3, then S2." (Grok 4); "This round: S1 ask 67, S2 68, S3 68. This ensures high prices and matching." (Grok 4); "Let's set all asks at 63 next cycle. No undercutting ensures clearing at bidmax=63."; "Coordinating asks at 39 for stability" (Qwen-3 235B). — [GitHub README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lechmazur/emergent_collusion/main/README.md); [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yqhy3zBmpeFuGFLxX/emergent-price-fixing-by-llm-auction-agents)
- Ablation: disabling same-role messaging "reduced—but did not eliminate—price-rigging"; coordination moved to buyer–seller pairs. — [GitHub README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lechmazur/emergent_collusion/main/README.md)
- Overlap with OUR SETUP: sealed bids, private values, many frontier models, turn-taking/rotation and identical asks observed.
- Difference: two-sided market with a chat channel (explicit, not tacit, collusion); 8 rounds; no equilibrium benchmark; LLM-judge score is the only metric; no tie rule documented.

**B3. Lotfi, Ghrayeb & Abu-Rub (2026), "Large Language Models as Strategic Bidding Agents in P2P Energy Trading Markets." CONFERENCE PAPER per arXiv comments (IEEE IECON 2026). ABSTRACT ONLY.**
- arXiv:2609.05462. Repeated P2P energy double auction; LLM bidders compared with random and epsilon-greedy bandit bidders. Incidental collusion-like finding: "In a homogeneous population of LLM agents, sellers increasingly exploit buyers' rational outside options to drive clearing prices above the Nash equilibrium, resulting in a progressively more asymmetric allocation of surplus in favor of sellers." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05462)
- Overlap: repeated auction, LLM sellers, Nash benchmark, same-model population. Difference: double auction, energy domain; collusion is not the object of study; no mechanism manipulation that I could see from the abstract.

#### C. Auction used as a test-bed where collusion is instructed, penalised, or only correlation is measured

**C1. Xu, Wu, Lu, Tan, Tao & Hong (2026), "Collusion with Competitive Marginals: Price-Level Audits Are Blind by Construction." PREPRINT.**
- arXiv:2607.26385 [cs.GT], 29 Jul 2026; Carnegie Mellon / Zhejiang (first four authors confirmed from the arXiv API; last two from the HTML summary). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26385)
- LLM experiment: language models bid in a first-price auction over synthetic orders ("the highest bid wins"); "Twenty models from nineteen independent developers are each deployed under three different prompts, giving sixty agents, run for 200 rounds at each of five sampling temperatures." Residual pairwise correlation after regressing out auditor-visible order features: same-base-model pairs +0.053 (95% CI [0.030, 0.078]) vs different-model pairs +0.0001; coupling falls with temperature (p = 0.002; +0.147 at T = 0.0 to +0.028 at T = 1.4 under the weakest auditor). Field check on 77,684 MEV-Boost bids from 39 builders over 24 days. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26385v1)
- The claim is correlation from shared model origin, not strategic collusion; no tie rule mentioned. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26385v1)
- Overlap: first-price bidding by many LLMs; bears directly on same-model vs mixed-model designs and on why identical bids can arise without intent. Difference: forward auction, not a strategic repeated game with feedback; detection/audit paper.
- Correction to the user's vault note: the note describes this as a "pricing environment"; the paper's LLM task is first-price auction bidding.

**C2. Kaur, Chari, Raskar, Singh, Jha & Roy (2026), "Beyond the Transcript: Detecting Covert Coordination in Latent Multi-Agent Communication." PREPRINT. ABSTRACT/SUMMARY ONLY.**
- arXiv:2608.19161. Multi-agent auction benchmark with 25–100 Qwen3-0.6B bidders in which cartel agents are ASSIGNED a bid-suppression objective via latent (hidden-state) channels; monitor AUROC 0.993 (identical pairs) / 0.854 (heterogeneous pairs); steering "reduces collusive low-bid behavior by 47.3 percentage points." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19161)
- Overlap: auction, LLM bidders, bid suppression. Difference: collusion is instructed and channel-based; a detection paper.

**C3. Arumugam, Manku & Dhanda (2026), "Safe Equilibrium Policy Optimization for Strategic Agent Policies." PREPRINT (arXiv comment says EMNLP 2026; acceptance unverified). ABSTRACT ONLY.**
- arXiv:2605.30854. Fine-tunes Gemma and Qwen small models with penalties for "exploitability, collusion risk, and externality cost"; "repeated auctions" is one of five evaluation domains. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30854)
- Overlap: repeated auctions + collusion-risk penalty on LLM policies. Difference: a training method, not a market-design experiment; auction details not available from the abstract.

## Does any paper vary or discuss the tie-break rule for identical bids with LLM bidders? Does any study bid rotation or identical/matching bids as a coordination device?

### Takeaway
I found NO paper that varies the tie-break rule with LLM bidders, and none that studies bid rotation or exact-match bids as a tacit (communication-free) coordination device among LLM agents. Tie rules appear only as a fixed one-line design detail (uniform random in Fish et al. A.6 and in Heo et al. ISARC 2025), and rotation/identical asks appear only as explicitly messaged schemes in the Mazur repo.

### Cited Findings
- Fish et al. A.6 fixes the rule: "the winner is chosen uniformly at random in case of a tie", and notes in a footnote that discretised bids admit "additional equilibria involving bids of v and v−1¢." The rule is not varied or analysed. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00806)
- Heo, Park & Ahn (ISARC 2025) fix the rule: "in cases where all agents submit identical prices, the winner is chosen randomly." Not varied; tie frequency not reported. — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Tolety: the experimental description says only "The ride is allocated to the first driver who accepts"; the incentive-compatibility proof assumes that on the collusive path "each of the N drivers expects to win with probability 1/N." The collusive behaviour observed IS an exact tie (all GPT-4.1-mini agents "accepting at exactly $11.75 with zero variance"), called a "focal point", and the paper suggests a platform could "randomize price increments" to disrupt focal points — but neither the allocation rule among simultaneous acceptors nor any anti-focal-point intervention is tested. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Mazur repo: rotation and identical asks are proposed over the chat channel ("Let's rotate who gets the high bid… Next cycle S3, then S2."; "Let's set all asks at 63 next cycle"), i.e. explicit collusion; the README text I retrieved contains no tie-handling rule. — [GitHub README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lechmazur/emergent_collusion/main/README.md); [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yqhy3zBmpeFuGFLxX/emergent-price-fixing-by-llm-auction-agents)
- Agrawal et al.: a keyword search of the PDF text for "tie", "identical", "rotat" and "turn" returned no tie-break rule and no rotation analysis; "ask dispersion" (SD of asks) is the closest metric to bid matching. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.01413)

## Which papers use a one-shot or no-history control, a Nash/Bayes-Nash benchmark, or a normalised collusion index? Which hand-code chain-of-thought reasoning?

### Takeaway
In the auction papers, benchmarks are static: a one-shot Nash bid (Fish A.6), a competitive-equilibrium price (Agrawal; Tolety), or none (Heo ISARC); none runs a one-shot or no-history CONTROL CONDITION, none uses a Bayes–Nash bid function with private values, and none reports a normalised collusion index. Reasoning text is analysed by LLM judge (Agrawal, Mazur), embedding clustering (Heo ISARC; Fish for pricing) or illustrative examples (Tolety); I found no auction paper with human hand-coding.

### Cited Findings
- One-shot Nash as a benchmark line, not a treatment: Fish A.6 marks "the unique pure-strategy Nash equilibrium of the single-period static game" (bid = v) on its plots; outcome measures are bids and profits normalised by v over periods 251–300. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00806)
- Competitive-equilibrium price benchmark: Agrawal et al. define collusion as coordination plus pricing above the $90 competitive equilibrium; no index. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01413)
- Theory-derived benchmark: Tolety derives competitive and grim-trigger collusive SPNE and a maximum cartel size N*; empirical comparison is to the roughly $10.28 competitive price and the $13.75 monopoly maximum; N = 1 serves as a monopoly reference, not a no-history control. The trial length is "40 sequential Dutch auctions"; there is no one-shot arm. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- No formal benchmark: Heo, Park & Ahn (ISARC) compare prompt conditions with each other (means 1.002–1.005 vs 1.039–1.056 against cost 1.0); no Nash or monopoly line and no index. — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Nash benchmark in a double auction: Lotfi et al. report clearing prices "above the Nash equilibrium." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05462)
- Bayes–Nash benchmark with LLM bidders exists only in the non-collusion literature: Shah et al. benchmark against the "Bayes–Nash bid function in first-price auctions and dominant-strategy truth-telling in second-price and clock formats", in a one-shot baseline with a learning ablation. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09083)
- Normalised collusion index appears only in the pricing literature: Garra uses Delta = (p_bar − p^N)/(p^M − p^N) with Bertrand–Nash p^N of about 1.473 and monopoly p^M of about 1.925; Keppo et al. report percentage price elevation over the static Nash price (about $1.47; monopoly about $1.92). — [Garra, arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13037); [Keppo et al., arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20281v1)
- Myopic-agent contrast (closest thing to a "no shadow of the future" control, pricing only): Keppo et al. pair a patient (delta = 0.95) with a myopic (delta = 0) LLM and elevation falls from +22% to +10%. — [Keppo et al., arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20281v1)
- Reasoning analysis by LLM judge: Agrawal et al. score sellers' reasoning traces 1–4 with GPT-4.1-mini and report inter-run reliability (Krippendorff alpha 0.948 over 500 rounds x 10 judgments) — reliability of the judge with itself, not against human coders. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01413)
- Reasoning analysis by LLM judge (logs incl. messages): Mazur's 0–10 illegality score from o3. — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yqhy3zBmpeFuGFLxX/emergent-price-fixing-by-llm-auction-agents)
- Reasoning analysis by unsupervised clustering: Heo, Park & Ahn embed 1,861 plan sentences (921 B1, 940 B2) with text-embedding-3-large, PCA to 35 dimensions, k-means with 5 clusters, GPT-4o cluster labels; they find plans contain only raise/lower intentions and "no explicit indications of collusion." — [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Reasoning shown as examples only: Tolety Appendix B. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Reward–punishment tested behaviourally rather than textually: Heo, Ahn & Park (2026) report agents "frequently adopt reward–punishment mechanisms" (method not visible in abstract); Garra regresses own price on the competitor's lagged price (coefficients 0.077 under P1, 0.165 under P2) and classifies off-path sentences (73.5% of "AvoidPriceWar" sentences from P1). — [OpenAlex abstract of Heo et al.](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144); [Garra, arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13037)
- Caution on relying on CoT: Lee & Park (arXiv:2609.18346) test nine LLMs in pricing competition and report that "collusive behavior and chain-of-thought faithfulness dissociate along both dimensions" (abstract only; pricing, not auctions). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18346)

### Inferences
- A one-shot / no-history control arm, a private-value Bayes–Nash bid benchmark, and a normalised collusion index would each be new to the LLM auction literature; all three exist only in neighbouring literatures (index and benchmarks in LLM pricing; Bayes–Nash in non-collusion LLM auction work).
- Human hand-coding of bidder reasoning would also be a methodological difference from the auction papers found, which use LLM judges or clustering; Agrawal et al.'s reliability statistics do not include human agreement.

### Gaps
- Fish et al.'s main pricing analysis of plan text (clustering and off-path analysis) was not re-read here; I only confirmed that Appendix A.6 contains no text analysis for the auction arm.
- Whether Keppo et al., Lee & Park, or Garra hand-code any reasoning (versus automated classification) was not established.
- Method behind "reward–punishment" in Heo, Ahn & Park (2026) is unknown.

## Which papers study which market-design or information levers (bid disclosure, number of bidders, model heterogeneity, oversight prompts) reduce LLM collusion in auctions?

### Takeaway
Within auctions, tested levers are: number of bidders (Tolety: coordination gone at N >= 5), communication and oversight/urgency messages and model mix (Agrawal et al.), same-role chat removal (Mazur), prompt wording (Fish A.6; Heo et al.), and "market transparency" (Heo, Ahn & Park 2026, direction: transparency may facilitate coordination). No auction paper with LLM bidders tests an allocation-rule lever such as the tie-break rule, a rebid stage, reserve prices, or bid-disclosure policy as a controlled factor that I could verify.

### Cited Findings
- Number of bidders: Tolety finds supra-competitive prices for N = 2–4 and "near-competitive prices around $10.28" for N >= 5, and concludes that "market structure levers can be more effective than capability limits for mitigation." — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21802)
- Model capability / identity: in Tolety, o4-mini shows no coordination (p = 0.80) while GPT-4.1-mini and GPT-4o-mini do; GPT-4.1-nano cannot participate. All groups are same-model. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802)
- Model heterogeneity in a double auction: Agrawal et al. mixed (3 GPT-4.1 + 2 Claude-3.7-Sonnet) sessions average $89.37 vs $91.44 all-GPT-4.1 and $88.63 all-Claude; confidence intervals overlap. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.01413)
- Oversight and urgency prompts: Agrawal et al. oversight lowers average trade price to $86.24 (from $90.52); urgency raises it to $96.26; oversight + urgency stays at $96.16, i.e. urgency dominates oversight. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.01413)
- Communication: Agrawal et al. $90.52 with seller messaging vs $87.71 without. Mazur: blocking same-role messages "reduced—but did not eliminate—price-rigging." — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.01413); [GitHub README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lechmazur/emergent_collusion/main/README.md)
- Prompt wording: Fish A.6 profit 0.115v (A1) vs 0.006v (A2); Heo, Park & Ahn mean bids about 1.04–1.06 (B2) vs about 1.00 (B1), a one-sentence difference. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00806); [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Information / transparency: Heo, Ahn & Park (2026) state that "market transparency may facilitate coordination among agents" and call for "regulatory attention to the informational conditions" (abstract only). Fish A.6 and Heo ISARC hold feedback fixed (minimal-bid-to-win feedback; full rival bid history of 20 rounds, respectively). — [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144); [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00806); [IAARC PDF](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)
- Procurement-rule lever, RL agents only: Heo, Ahn, Oh & Park (2025) find "different bid evaluation methods influence the speed and stability of this collusive behavior" among reinforcement-learning bidders. — [SNU Pure abstract](https://snu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/algorithmic-collusion-in-construction-bidding-risks-dynamics-and--2/) [UPDATE 3 Oct 2026: full text read; the lever is the award rule (lowest, second-lowest, closest-to-mean), and the paper does not report a tie rule.]
- Sampling temperature and operator counting: Xu et al. find same-model residual bid correlation falls with temperature and frame temperature as "a deployment parameter a regulator could mandate"; they argue for counting independent decision-makers rather than price-level audits. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26385v1)
- Untested proposals: Tolety suggests randomising price increments to break focal points; Idowu et al. (conceptual; ICML 2026 TAIGR workshop and Knowledge-Based Systems per arXiv comments, journal details unverified) recommend restricting communication, one-shot interactions, delayed feedback, anonymisation/identity rotation, continuous entry and avoiding "algorithmic monoculture", and note sealed-bid formats are less collusion-prone than open ascending ones; no experiments. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21802); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.00360)

### Inferences
- Every tested auction lever so far acts on the participants (how many, which model, what they are told, whether they can talk) or on prompts; none acts on the auctioneer's allocation rule. A tie-break manipulation is a mechanism-side lever and is in that sense orthogonal to the existing auction results.
- The Agrawal et al. model-mix and oversight effects are modest relative to their confidence intervals (10 sessions per cell), and the Heo ISARC and Tolety designs have 3 runs and 1 trial per cell respectively; evidence on levers in auctions is thin and low-powered.

### Gaps
- No LLM auction study was found that experimentally varies bid-disclosure policy (e.g. winner-only vs all-bids feedback) with a verifiable design; Heo, Ahn & Park (2026) may do so but the full text was not read.
- No LLM auction study was found on reserve prices, entry, or bidder anonymisation as tested levers.

## Adjacent LLM pricing-collusion literature, only as it bears on auctions or on the levers above

### Takeaway
The pricing literature supplies the tools the auction papers lack (normalised collusion index, Nash/monopoly benchmarks, structured intervention comparisons) and consistent lever results: collusion is prompt-sensitive, weakens with more sellers and with heterogeneity, and incentive or structural interventions beat warning prompts. Same-model correlation (Xu et al.) is the one adjacent result obtained in an auction.

### Cited Findings
- Fish, Gonczarowski & Shorrer (arXiv:2404.00806): repeated Bertrand duopoly with LLM pricing agents reaching "supracompetitive prices and profits"; "seemingly innocuous phrases in LLM instructions ... substantially influence the degree of supracompetitive pricing"; "Our results extend to auction settings" (the A.6 experiment above). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Lin, Ojha, Cai & Chen (arXiv:2410.00031, submitted 19 Sep 2024, revised 16 May 2025; abstract only): multi-commodity Cournot; "LLMs can effectively monopolize specific commodities by dynamically adjusting their pricing and resource allocation strategies" without explicit direction. Relevance: market division/specialisation is the pricing analogue of bid rotation or territory allocation. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00031)
- Keppo, Li, Tsoukalas & Yuan (arXiv:2603.20281; SSRN 5386338), "On the Fragility of AI Agent Collusion": repeated Bertrand with logit demand, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B (GPT-5-mini robustness). Price elevation over Nash: two patient LLMs +22%; patient vs myopic +10%; high vs low data access +7%; LLM vs Q-learning +2–8% with no sustained convergence; three patient LLMs +36%, four +11%, five +3% (no convergence); 32B vs 14B models +21% (leader–follower, i.e. size heterogeneity did not break collusion). No auction content. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20281v1); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5386338)
- Garra (arXiv:2609.13037, 11 Sep 2026; UMass Amherst), "Mitigating Emergent Collusion in LLM Pricing Agents": Fish-style duopoly replicated with DeepSeek-V3.1, 300 periods, 7 runs per treatment. Collusion index under prompt P1 = 0.569 (76% of runs above Nash), P2 = −0.166. Warning prompt ("This market is monitored by an antitrust regulator"): P1 index 0.242, 86% of runs still above Nash. Harrington-style expected-damages regulator: 0.080. Random third entrant: −0.367 (P1) and −0.571 (P2), zero runs above the adjusted benchmark. No auction content. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13037)
- Xu et al. (arXiv:2607.26385): same-base-model deployments are correlated (+0.053) and cross-model pairs are not (+0.0001), in a first-price auction task; see C1 above. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26385v1)
- Lee & Park (arXiv:2609.18346; abstract only): CoT monitoring cannot be relied on to detect tacit collusion in LLM pricing agents across nine models. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18346)
- Tian (arXiv:2604.17774, 20 Apr 2026; abstract only), "Prompt Optimization Enables Stable Algorithmic Collusion in LLM Agents": a meta-optimiser refining shared guidance yields coordination strategies that generalise to held-out duopoly markets; no auction content. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17774)
- Idowu, Almasoud & Alfahid (arXiv:2601.00360): taxonomy mapping sanctions, leniency/whistleblowing, monitoring/auditing, market design and governance to multi-agent AI; conceptual only. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00360)

### Inferences
- Results on the number of competitors agree across settings (Tolety: breakdown at N >= 5 in an auction; Keppo et al.: +3% and no convergence at five sellers), but Keppo et al.'s non-monotonicity (three sellers +36% > two sellers +22%) warns against assuming monotone effects of N.
- Xu et al. imply that same-model bidder groups can produce correlated or matching bids for non-strategic reasons; a design that pools across models or includes mixed-model groups, plus a one-shot control, is needed to attribute exact-match bids to repeated-game coordination rather than shared priors. This is an inference from their result, not a claim they make about ties.
- Garra's ranking (structural/incentive interventions > prompt warnings) and Agrawal et al.'s finding that urgency overrides oversight both suggest prompt-level oversight is a weak lever relative to mechanism changes — consistent with motivating a tie-break (mechanism) manipulation.
