# Tie-breaking rules, bid rotation and BAFO/rebid mechanisms in relation to bidder collusion (economics, IO, auction theory, procurement practice)

Notes compiled 2 October 2026. Paper setup these notes serve: LLM bidders, repeated first-price sealed-bid procurement (lowest bid wins), private costs, no communication; tie-break manipulated across (1) random, (2) "least-wins-first", (3) BAFO rebid. Hypothesis (H): least-wins-first lets bidders rotate by submitting identical bids without the execution risk of cover bidding; a rebid reopens competition.

Conventions used below:
- **[T]** theory, **[E]** empirical, **[X]** laboratory experiment, **[L]** legal / policy / regulation.
- **Verification tags.** "Full text read" = I extracted and read the passage myself. "Abstract only" = only the abstract was accessed. "Crossref only" = bibliographic record verified via api.crossref.org but content not accessed. "Snippet" = content comes from a search-result extract and was not checked against the source. Anything tagged Snippet or Crossref-only should be re-checked before it is cited for a substantive claim.
- **"Bearing [inference]"** lines are my own reading of how a result relates to H. They are not claims made by the source.
- Most classic auction papers are written for a *selling* auction (high bid wins, reserve price r). The procurement mirror image is: low bid wins, and the cartel's target is the buyer's ceiling / reserve price.

## 1. Identical bids and collusion: classic evidence and theory; what McAfee & McMillan say about tie-breaking

### Takeaway
The canonical result (McAfee & McMillan 1992) is that a cartel that cannot make side payments can do no better than have every member submit the *same* bid at the reserve, using the auctioneer's random tie-break as the cartel's allocation device, and that this needs no coordination beyond knowing the rule. The same paper says explicitly that the auctioneer can disrupt this by replacing randomisation with a deterministic tie-break that discriminates against some bidders, and that cartels respond by switching to rotating bids. That passage is the closest thing in the literature to a statement of the paper's mechanism, but it points both ways (see Inferences).

### Cited Findings

**McAfee, R. P. & McMillan, J. (1992). "Bidding Rings." *American Economic Review* 82(3): 579–599.** [T] No DOI (pre-DOI AER; Crossref has no record). Full text read. Setting: one-shot first-price sealed-bid selling auction, IPV, n cartel members, public reserve r; "weak cartel" = no side payments. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v82y1992i3p579-99.html); [full-text PDF used](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)
- Abstract-level claim: "weak cartel can do no better than have its members submit identical bids."
- Tie-break assumption the result rests on (p. 583): "Assume that it is common knowledge that, in the event of two or more bidders being tied for the highest bid, the seller awards the good randomly, with equal likelihood to each of the tied bidders."
- Mechanism (p. 584): in the absence of side payments "incentive compatibility requires that the good be awarded stochastically, with equal probability of being awarded to anyone whose value is larger than the minimum price: any attempt to arrange that the highest-value bidder wins generates incentives for the bidders to misstate their valuations. By submitting equal bids, the bidders in effect use the seller as their randomizing device."
- No communication needed (p. 584): "the optimal mechanism can be implemented in a decentralized way: the bidders need not actually report to the mechanism, but simply bid r if and only if their valuations exceed r. In this sense, although the analysis allows the possibility of coordination, the optimal mechanism can be implemented without coordination."
- Condition: the identical-bid result holds when H(v) = [1 − F(v)]/f(v) is decreasing (H′ < 0), which the authors call "the more likely case, as it is satisfied by most common distributions." When H′ ≥ 0 everywhere (e.g. exponential) the scheme "does not improve on noncooperative bidding" (p. 584).
- Correlated devices add nothing (p. 584): "Bidding according to the phases of the moon is an example of a correlated equilibrium. Can the cartel do better than using identical bidding by going to a correlated equilibrium? The answer is no."
- Efficiency cost (p. 584): collusion without transfers lowers the payment to the seller and "the average valuation of the winning bidder is decreased, because the winner is no longer efficiently selected."
- Real-world counterpart (p. 584), quoting Scherer (1970, p. 182): "Each year the federal and state governments receive thousands of sets of identical bids in the sealed bid competitions they sponsor." Footnote 8: "in one sealed-bid tender to a Canadian local government, all nine bids were for $6,009.15 (Green, 1985 p. 177)."
- Rotation as the alternative (pp. 585–586): a cartel may instead use "some device (such as the phases of the moon) to choose which bidder's turn it is to bid"; the designated bidder "bids an amount r unopposed (or with token opposition — 'rival' bids less than r)... This was how the electrical-equipment conspiracy operated." "The total expected profits from this mechanism are the same as from the identical-bidding mechanism, but in practice it requires more coordination, which creates a risk of cartel communications being detected but reduces the evidence given directly from bidding."
- **What they say about tie-breaking as a counter-measure (p. 586), verbatim:** "The second reason for using rotating bids comes from the fact that, since the mechanism of Theorem 1 works by using the seller as a randomizing device, the seller can easily disrupt the mechanism by refusing to randomize. Instead of awarding the item arbitrarily when the bids are tied, the seller could announce a deterministic tie-breaking rule: for example, he could award the item to the smallest firm bidding, or to the bidder whose name comes first in the alphabet. Then it would no longer be in the interest of bidders so discriminated against to remain in the cartel. The cartel members could defeat this ploy by rotating their bids. Mund (1960) cites some instances of bidders for government contracts switching from an identical-bid mechanism to a rotating-bid mechanism after the government authorities became suspicious about the identical bids."
- Other counter-strategies discussed (p. 591): "raising the reserve price, keeping the reserve price secret, and interfering with the cartel's enforcement mechanism."
- Unequal shares are a reason to prefer rotation (p. 586): circuit-breaker contracts in the electrical-equipment conspiracy were allocated "General Electric got 45 percent, Westinghouse got 35 percent, and Allis-Chalmers and Federal Pacific got 10 percent each."
- Bearing [inference]: this is the foundational citation for H. Two points matter. (i) Identical bidding is a no-communication-required scheme, which fits the paper's no-communication LLM setting. (ii) M&M's proposed remedy is a deterministic tie-break that *permanently disadvantages* some bidders so they defect. Least-wins-first is deterministic but does the opposite: it equalises wins, so no bidder is discriminated against and the "refuse to randomise" remedy does not bite. M&M do not analyse such an equalising rule.

**Comanor, W. S. & Schankerman, M. A. (1976). "Identical Bids and Cartel Behavior." *Bell Journal of Economics* 7(1): 281–286. DOI 10.2307/3003203.** [T/E] Abstract only. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/bellje/v7y1976ispringp281-286.html)
- Abstract: "Despite wide acceptance of the view that identical bids suggest the presence of a cartel agreement, there has been little analysis of the role that such bids would play. When this matter is examined, we find that except in certain extreme circumstances, identical bid arrangements are unlikely to be maintained among colluding firms where their number in an industry is small. If this conclusion is correct, the focus of past government policy on the presence of identical bids as suggesting collusive behavior in highly concentrated industries is surely misplaced."
- As summarised by McAfee & McMillan (p. 586): they "found that rotating-bid arrangements were significantly more common in cartels with few members than in cartels with many members, reflecting the extra difficulties of setting up and running a rotating-bid mechanism." — [M&M PDF](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)
- Bearing [inference]: cuts against a naive reading of H. With a small number of bidders (the paper's setting) human cartels historically preferred rotation to identical bids. One reading is that with few firms a random tie-break gives too uneven a realised split; a least-wins-first rule removes exactly that unevenness.

**Mund, V. A. (1960). "Identical Bid Prices." *Journal of Political Economy* 68(2): 150–169. DOI 10.1086/258292.** [E/L] Crossref only; content known to me only through McAfee & McMillan's citation (bidders switching from identical bids to rotation once authorities became suspicious). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/258292)
- Bearing [inference]: historical evidence that identical bidding and rotation are substitutes chosen according to detection risk.

**Executive Order 10936, "Reports of Identical Bids" (John F. Kennedy, 24 April 1961).** [L] — [American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10936-reports-identical-bids)
- Preamble: "identical bidding is harmful to the effective functioning of a system of competitive bids" and "may constitute evidence of the existence of conspiracies to monopolize or restrain trade." Federal agencies had to report to the Attorney General identical bids on procurements or sales "exceeding $10,000 in total amount." (Quotes via a page summary; the operative sections were not read line by line.)

**Executive Order 12430, "Reports of Identical Bids" (Ronald Reagan, 6 July 1983).** [L] Full text read. — [American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-12430-reports-identical-bids)
- Revoked EO 10936 "in order to eliminate an agency reporting requirement which has proved ineffective and which consumes resources that could be employed in a more effective manner to prevent antitrust violations."
- Bearing [inference]: identical-bid reporting was tried for 22 years and abandoned as ineffective, consistent with Comanor & Schankerman's critique and with cartels having moved to rotation / cover bidding.

**Athey, S., Bagwell, K. & Sanchirico, C. (2004). "Collusion and Price Rigidity." *Review of Economic Studies* 71(2): 317–349. DOI 10.1111/0034-6527.00286.** [T] Abstract only. Setting: infinitely repeated Bertrand, public prices, private i.i.d. cost shocks, symmetric perfect public equilibria. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v71y2004i2p317-349.html)
- "A rigid-pricing scheme, where a firm's collusive price is independent of its current cost position, sacrifices efficiency benefits but also diminishes the informational cost." "For a wide range of settings, the optimal symmetric collusive scheme requires the absence of equilibrium-path price wars and a rigid price." If firms are too impatient "the rigid-pricing scheme cannot be enforced."
- Bearing [inference]: the repeated-game analogue of M&M: with private costs and no transfers, the best symmetric scheme is everyone at the same price with demand split by the tie rule. This is the closest theoretical model to the paper's environment (repeated, private i.i.d. costs, no transfers). It implies identical bids should be the natural tacit scheme under *either* random or least-wins-first; the tie rule changes the variance of realised shares, not the expected share.

**Asai, K. & Charoenwong, B. "Ownership Networks and Bid Rigging." SSRN working paper, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.3298152 (2018; Sept 2019 draft read).** [E] Singapore public procurement. A search result indicates a published version in *Review of Corporate Finance Studies* (advance article, DOI 10.1093/rcfs/cfag002); I did not verify that record. — [2019 draft](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/micropaper19/micro1224.pdf)
- Abstract: "identical bidding in the same sealed-bid auction is prevalent and positively correlated with ownership connections among bidders. Moreover, the identical bidding attributable to ownership connections is positively associated with contract price when the lowest bidder wins."
- On the identical-bid vs rotation trade-off (p. 6 of draft): "Compared to identical bidding, bid rotations provide a clear market-sharing rule, but the coordination costs will be higher because the cartel must agree on a division of cartel profits."
- Bearing [inference]: modern evidence that identical bids are still used and are price-raising; the quoted sentence states the trade-off H relies on. A least-wins-first tie rule supplies the "clear market-sharing rule" at identical-bid coordination cost.

**OECD (2009). *Guidelines for Fighting Bid Rigging in Public Procurement*.** [L] Full text read. — [PDF](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2009/02/guidelines-for-fighting-bid-rigging-in-public-procurement_d4a5f7a8/8cfeafbb-en.pdf)
- Design checklist: "Avoid splitting contracts between suppliers with identical bids. Investigate the reasons for the identical bids and, if necessary, consider re-issuing the invitation to tender or award the contract to one supplier only."
- Detection checklist warning sign: "Competitors submit identical tenders or the prices submitted by bidders increase in regular increments."
- Also: "Avoid presenting contracts with identical values that can be easily shared among competitors"; "More predictable procurement schedules and unchanging quantities sold or bought can facilitate collusion."

**OECD (2025). *OECD Guidelines for Fighting Bid Rigging in Public Procurement (2025 Update)*. OECD Publishing, Paris. DOI 10.1787/cbe05a56-en.** [L] Full text read (PDF p. 22). — [PDF](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/09/oecd-guidelines-for-fighting-bid-rigging-in-public-procurement-2025-update_127880ea/cbe05a56-en.pdf)
- "Whenever possible, add criteria to resolve any tie between bidders. Investigate the reasons for any identical bids and, if necessary, consider re-issuing the invitation to tender."
- Red flags include "Identical bids, bids with similar format or metadata" and "Identical pricing when prices were previously different."
- I did not find the 2009 "avoid splitting contracts between suppliers with identical bids" sentence in my text extraction of the 2025 update, although a search summary attributed it to the 2025 text. Treat that as unresolved.
- Bearing [inference]: the OECD position is that the *outcome* of a tie should not reward identical bidders with a share. Least-wins-first is the intertemporal equivalent of splitting the contract among identical bidders, so it is the kind of rule this guidance warns against. Re-issuing the tender is the policy analogue of the rebid condition.

### Inferences
- Established: identical bids at the reserve with a random tie-break are the optimal no-transfer cartel scheme in a one-shot IPV auction (M&M 1992) and rigid identical pricing is optimal among symmetric schemes in the repeated private-cost Bertrand game (Athey, Bagwell & Sanchirico 2004). Both need the tie rule to hand out the contract.
- Inference: the paper's condition (1) is M&M's baseline. Condition (2) is not M&M's "deterministic tie-break" remedy, because M&M's remedy works by making the rule unfair to someone; least-wins-first is a deterministic rule that is fair over time. The paper can cite M&M p. 586 for the general idea that the tie-break rule is a lever on identical-bid collusion and then state that the direction depends on whether the deterministic rule discriminates or equalises.
- Inference: under risk neutrality and patience, expected shares are equal under random and least-wins-first, so standard theory gives no first-order difference in the sustainability of identical bidding. Any difference has to come from (a) variance of realised shares over a finite horizon, (b) the perception by the losing bidder of an unlucky streak as defection or unfairness, (c) salience / focal-point effects. This is worth saying in the paper, because a referee who knows M&M will ask why a random rule is not already sufficient.
- Inference: the historical record (Comanor & Schankerman; Mund via M&M; EO 12430) says small-number human cartels moved *away* from identical bids toward rotation with cover bids, partly because identical bids are conspicuous. The paper's claim that identical bids avoid the "execution risk of cover bidding" is consistent with M&M's remark that rotation "requires more coordination", but the paper should acknowledge that identical bids carry higher detection risk.

## 2. Bid rotation: theory and evidence; do schemes condition on past wins, and does any allocation rule mechanically favour bidders with fewer past wins?

### Takeaway
Theory shows that when bidders observe only who won, the collusive schemes that beat simple rotation are ones that keep score of past wins and make recent winners stand down (Skrzypacz & Hopenhayn's "chips"; Athey & Bagwell's "future market-share favors"), and field evidence from Japan and Ohio shows real cartels allocate by backlog, favour-balance or incumbency. I found no paper that studies an auctioneer-imposed allocation or tie-break rule that mechanically favours the bidder with the fewest past wins; that appears to be an open question.

### Cited Findings

**Skrzypacz, A. & Hopenhayn, H. (2004). "Tacit collusion in repeated auctions." *Journal of Economic Theory* 114(1): 153–169. DOI 10.1016/S0022-0531(03)00128-5.** [T] Full text read. Setting: repeated sealed-bid auctions, IPV, no communication, no side payments; "the only information available to players is the identity of winners in all previous auctions." — [PDF](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/skrzypacz+hopenhayn-journal-of-economic-theory-2004A.pdf)
- Abstract: "Collusion better than bid rotation is still feasible, but full surplus cannot be extracted. This constraint becomes less severe with more players and large cartels can become asymptotically efficient even with very limited monitoring."
- Two-player bound: "The optimal collusive outcome is thus uniformly bounded away from efficient collusion for any discount factor."
- Chips mechanism (Section 3.1): "in the beginning of the game each of the two players gets T chips. After each auction, the winner gives one chip to the loser. If player i runs out of chips, he is supposed to allow player j to win k auctions in a row without competition and for that he receives one chip back."
- They also note the design implication: "It may thus be in the interest of the auction designer to limit the information available to bidders in the repeated game."
- Bearing [inference]: the most relevant theory paper for H. The chips scheme is a win-count ledger that bidders must maintain themselves and enforce by voluntary abstention. A least-wins-first tie-break has the *auctioneer* keep the ledger and do the enforcing, provided bidders tie. It also shows that publishing winner identities (which a least-wins-first rule makes necessary) is itself collusion-facilitating.

**Aoyagi, M. (2003). "Bid rotation and collusion in repeated auctions." *Journal of Economic Theory* 112(1): 79–105. DOI 10.1016/S0022-0531(03)00071-1.** [T] Crossref only; abstract not accessible. Setting (as characterised by later literature): repeated auctions with pre-play communication and no side transfers. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jetheo/v112y2003i1p79-105.html)
- A machine-generated summary on Semantic Scholar states that the paper's dynamic scheme "yields a strictly higher payoff to the bidders than any static collusion scheme which coordinates bids based only on the current reported signals." Treat as unverified.
- Bearing [inference]: establishes that rotation conditioning on history beats static schemes; depends on communication, which the paper's setting rules out.

**Athey, S. & Bagwell, K. (2001). "Optimal Collusion with Private Information." *RAND Journal of Economics* 32(3): 428–465. DOI 10.2307/2696363.** [T] Abstract read (Feb 2001 draft). Setting: infinitely repeated Bertrand, public prices, private i.i.d. cost shocks. — [draft PDF](https://web.mit.edu/athey/www/ocpiwfig.pdf)
- "In the most profitable collusive schemes, firms implement productive efficiency, and high-cost firms are favored with higher expected market share in future periods. If types are discrete, there exists a discount factor strictly less than one above which first-best profits can be attained purely through history-dependent reallocation of market share between equally efficient firms." If side payments are costly, "optimal collusive equilibria involve the use of future market-share favors."
- Modelling note: in this framework, when prices tie the firms themselves choose how to share the market. Chassang & Ortner (2019, p. 8 of the working paper) adopt the same device: "In the case of ties, we follow Athey and Bagwell (2001) and let the bidders jointly determine the allocation." — [Chassang & Ortner WP](https://www.sylvainchassang.org/assets/papers/minimum_prices.pdf)
- Bearing [inference]: in the leading theory, control over who wins a tie *is* the cartel's transfer instrument. A random rule takes that instrument away; least-wins-first gives the cartel a specific, favourable version of it for free (the bidder owed a turn gets it).

**Blume, A. & Heidhues, P. (2008). "Modeling Tacit Collusion in Auctions." *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics* 164(1): 163–184. DOI 10.1628/jite-2008-0012.** [T] Abstract only (Crossref). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1628/jite-2008-0012)
- "We study tacit collusion, which we interpret as collusion without communication about strategies, in repeated auctions in which bidders can only observe past winners and not their bids. Strategies cannot discriminate among initially nameless bidders until they have become named through winning an auction... the lack of communication limits collusive strategies and payoffs among impatient bidders... Sufficiently patient bidders can overcome the attainability constraints imposed by lack of communication."
- Bearing [inference]: without communication, bidders face a symmetry-breaking problem (who goes first?). Identical bids plus an auctioneer tie-break solve that problem externally under both the random and least-wins-first rules.

**Rachmilevitch, S. (2013). "Endogenous bid rotation in repeated auctions." *Journal of Economic Theory* 148(4): 1714–1725. DOI 10.1016/j.jet.2013.04.004.** [T] Abstract only. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jetheo/v148y2013i4p1714-1725.html)
- "I study collusion between two bidders in a general symmetric IPV repeated auction, without communication, side transfers, or public randomization. I construct a collusive scheme, endogenous bid rotation, that generates a payoff larger than the bid rotation payoff."
- Bearing [inference]: rotation can arise endogenously with no communication and no public randomisation device; it is a theoretically legitimate tacit outcome in the paper's setting.

**Marshall, R. C. & Marx, L. M. (2007). "Bidder collusion." *Journal of Economic Theory* 133(1): 374–402. DOI 10.1016/j.jet.2005.12.004.** [T] Working-paper text skimmed. — [PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~marx/bio/papers/biddercollusion.pdf)
- Notes that in repeated settings "repeated play provides the opportunity for intertemporal payoff transfers through a bid rotation scheme," and (footnote 30) that with a discrete bid increment one would "expect to see ties occasionally" under non-cooperative bidding with close values, "something that would not be expected under efficient collusion."
- Bearing [inference]: with a coarse bid grid, some exact ties are competitive. The paper's design should distinguish accidental ties from collusive ties (e.g. ties at or near the reserve versus ties near cost).

**Ishii, R. (2009). "Favor exchange in collusion: Empirical study of repeated procurement auctions in Japan." *International Journal of Industrial Organization* 27(2): 137–144. DOI 10.1016/j.ijindorg.2008.05.006.** [E] Abstract only. Setting: public-works consulting auctions, Naha, Japan. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/indorg/v27y2009i2p137-144.html)
- "It is known that bid rigging in public-work auctions in Japan often takes the form of exchanging favors. In such a scheme, the winner is designated based on favors he has given to other ring members. By explicitly modeling 'favor' as an explanatory variable, this paper analyzes data from the public-works auctions for consulting works in Naha, Japan, to confirm that such a collusion scheme is in operation."
- Bearing [inference]: field evidence that dango rings select the winner by a running balance of past concessions, i.e. something close to "fewest recent wins goes next", maintained by the ring. Least-wins-first puts the same ledger in the auctioneer's hands.

**Kawai, K., Nakabayashi, J., Ortner, J. & Chassang, S. (2023). "Using Bid Rotation and Incumbency to Detect Collusion: A Regression Discontinuity Approach." *Review of Economic Studies* 90(1): 376–403. DOI 10.1093/restud/rdac013.** (NBER WP 29625, Jan 2022, read in full text.) [E] Ohio school milk (Porter & Zona 1999 sample) and Japanese municipal construction auctions. — [NBER WP](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29625/w29625.pdf)
- Abstract: "Cartels participating in procurement auctions frequently use bid rotation or prioritize incumbents to allocate contracts. However, establishing a link between observed allocation patterns and firm conduct has been difficult: there are cost-based competitive explanations for such patterns. We show that by focusing on auctions in which the winning and losing bids are very close, it is possible to distinguish allocation patterns reflecting cost differences across firms from patterns reflecting non-competitive environments."
- Method: backlog is measured from "how many auctions each bidder has recently won" (90-day and 180-day windows). Under competition, close winners and close losers should have the same backlog; "If close winners have consistently lower levels of backlog than close losers, this is evidence of collusive bid rotation." In the Japanese sample (roughly 11,000 auctions let by 16 Tohoku municipalities in the baseline sample) "Close winners have a significantly lower backlog than close losers."
- Competitive confound stated in the paper: "Bid rotation can arise under competition if marginal costs increase with backlog."
- The model assumes "Ties are broken with uniform probability."
- Author summary of the Japanese result: winners had lower recent workloads than losers, consistent with "bidders take turns winning auctions." — [Kawai, NBER Reporter 2025 no. 2](https://www.nber.org/reporter/2025number2/reporter/collusion-public-procurement)
- Bearing [inference]: (i) confirms that real rotation conditions on recent wins. (ii) Supplies a detection logic the paper could borrow: under least-wins-first, the winner of a tied auction has the lowest win count by construction, so a backlog-discontinuity screen would flag every tied auction. In other words the rule produces, mechanically, the pattern that this literature treats as a signature of collusion.

**Porter, R. H. & Zona, J. D. (1993). "Detection of Bid Rigging in Procurement Auctions." *Journal of Political Economy* 101(3): 518–538. DOI 10.1086/261885;** and **Porter, R. H. & Zona, J. D. (1999). "Ohio School Milk Markets: An Analysis of Bidding." *RAND Journal of Economics* 30(2): 263–288. DOI 10.2307/2556080.** [E] Crossref only for both. Long Island highway paving (cover bids) and Ohio school milk (incumbency-based allocation). Via Kawai et al.: in Cincinnati-area milk auctions the colluding dairies designated incumbents as winners, and marginal winners were far more likely than marginal losers to be incumbents. — [Kawai, NBER Reporter](https://www.nber.org/reporter/2025number2/reporter/collusion-public-procurement)
- Bearing [inference]: the classic evidence that phony/cover bids leave statistical traces; this is the "execution risk of cover bidding" the paper refers to.

**Pesendorfer, M. (2000). "A Study of Collusion in First-Price Auctions." *Review of Economic Studies* 67(3): 381–411. DOI 10.1111/1467-937X.00136.** [E/T] Snippet (abstract as reported by search). School milk, Florida and Texas, 1980s. — [author PDF](https://econ.lse.ac.uk/staff/mpesend/papers/firstpriceauctions.pdf)
- "One cartel divides the market among members, while the other cartel also uses side payments to compensate members"; both arrangements are "almost optimal, provided the number of contracts is sufficiently large."
- Bearing [inference]: with many contracts, market division without transfers gets close to the optimum; many rounds make rotation-style schemes nearly as good as transfers.

**Athey, S., Levin, J. & Seira, E. (2011). "Comparing Open and Sealed Bid Auctions: Evidence from Timber Auctions." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 126(1): 207–257. DOI 10.1093/qje/qjq001.** [E] Abstract only. US Forest Service timber. — [NBER WP 14590](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14590)
- "sealed bid auctions attract more small bidders, shift the allocation towards these bidders, and can also generate higher revenue." The paper uses the format comparison to assess bidder competitiveness (the collusion discussion concerns open auctions; I did not read that section).
- Bearing [inference]: background support for sealed bidding being less collusion-prone than open bidding; not about tie rules.

**Chassang, S., Kawai, K., Nakabayashi, J. & Ortner, J. (2022). "Robust Screens for Noncompetitive Bidding in Procurement Auctions." *Econometrica* 90(1): 315–346. DOI 10.3982/ECTA17155.** [E/T] Abstract via search. — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2022/01/01/robust-screens-noncompetitive-bidding-procurement-auctions)
- "winning bids tend to be isolated, and there is a missing mass of close losing bids"; extreme forms are "inconsistent with competitive behavior under arbitrary information structures."
- Bearing [inference]: cover-bidding cartels keep losing bids safely *away* from the winner to avoid accidental wins. Identical bidding is the opposite pattern (mass at zero gap). The two schemes have opposite observable signatures.

**OECD (2009), definition of bid rotation.** [L] — [PDF](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2009/02/guidelines-for-fighting-bid-rigging-in-public-procurement_d4a5f7a8/8cfeafbb-en.pdf)
- "In bid-rotation schemes, conspiring firms continue to bid, but they agree to take turns being the winning (i.e., lowest qualifying) bidder... conspirators might choose to allocate approximately equal monetary values from a certain group of contracts to each firm or to allocate volumes that correspond to the size of each company." Also: "cover bidding may be used in conjunction with a bid-rotation scheme."

### Inferences
- Established: (a) with only winner identities observable and no communication, history-dependent schemes that make past winners yield beat simple rotation (Skrzypacz & Hopenhayn 2004); (b) real cartels allocate by backlog or favour-balance (Ishii 2009; Kawai et al. 2023); (c) rotation normally has to be implemented with cover bids, which are detectable (Porter & Zona; Chassang et al. 2022).
- Inference: least-wins-first is an auctioneer-run version of the chips / favour-ledger schemes. Under it, a tacit cartel needs only one piece of coordination (bid the same number) and gets a deterministic, auditable rotation with no need for anyone to submit a deliberately losing bid. That is the precise sense in which it removes "execution risk": no bidder has to guess how far above the designated winner to bid, and no bidder has to trust that the designated winner bids low enough.
- Inference: I found no theoretical or empirical paper on an allocation rule that mechanically favours bidders with fewer past wins. The nearest items are (i) Skrzypacz & Hopenhayn's and Athey & Bagwell's cartel-run equivalents and (ii) the procurement "equitable distribution" rules in Section 3. The paper can reasonably present this as a gap it addresses, with the caveat that my search was not exhaustive.
- Inference: a competitive explanation for rotation-like outcomes exists (capacity constraints / backlog-dependent costs). In the paper's design costs are private and presumably i.i.d., so this confound is absent, which is worth stating.

### Gaps
- Aoyagi (2003): abstract and text not accessed; characterisation relies on citing papers and a machine-generated summary.
- Porter & Zona (1993, 1999), Pesendorfer (2000) and Athey, Levin & Seira (2011) were not read in full; numbers from those papers are not reported here.
- I did not review Marshall & Marx (2012), *The Economics of Collusion: Cartels and Bidding Rings* (MIT Press), or Kovacic, Marshall, Marx & Raiff (2006), "Bidding rings and the design of anti-collusive measures for auctions and procurements," in *Handbook of Procurement* (CUP), pp. 381–411, DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511492556.016 (Crossref only). Either may discuss tie-breaking; I cannot say.
- Hendricks & Porter's handbook treatment of collusion in auctions was not reviewed.

## 3. Tie-break rules in procurement practice: lots, rebid, rotation, fewest previous awards

### Takeaway
Real tie-break rules for equal low bids fall into four families: preference criteria then drawing lots (US FAR, Japan, Philippines, Maryland); a rebid / BAFO among the tied bidders (Italy, Brazil, UN, West Virginia); "keep awarding to the previous winner while bids stay identical" or "any manner that will discourage tie bids" (Hawaii, Utah 2006, Louisiana), which is an explicitly anti-collusive design; and splitting the award. **I found no regulation that breaks a tied price bid in favour of the bidder with the fewest previous awards.** "Equitable distribution of work" rules do exist, but in a different place: work allocation among pre-qualified panel members, micro-purchases, and qualifications-based selection of architects and engineers. The paper's uncited sentence is therefore only partly supported and should be reworded.

### Inferences
- The paper currently says least-wins-first "mirrors a rule used in some public-sector vendor-panel procurement systems, adopted as an anti-favouritism measure." What the sources do not support: that any system uses fewest-previous-awards as the tie-break for equal *price bids* in a sealed-bid competition. I found none. The closest sealed-bid tie rules either randomise, re-open bidding, or (Hawaii, Utah 2006) deliberately favour the previous winner so that identical bidding yields no rotation.
- Suggested rewording (my suggestion, not a sourced claim): "Least-wins-first is modelled on 'equitable distribution of work' provisions found in panel, micro-purchase and qualifications-based procurement rules (e.g. FAR 13.203(a)(1); Fla. Stat. § 287.055(4)(b); DFARS PGI 236.602-1), transplanted here to the resolution of tied price bids; we are not aware of a jurisdiction that uses it as a sealed-bid tie-break." This is defensible and also makes the experiment more interesting, because the equitable-distribution rules were not designed with collusion in mind.
- The Hawaii / Utah / Louisiana family is useful to the paper as a foil. Their logic is M&M's "refuse to randomise": if identical bids always go to the same firm, the others gain nothing from tying. That is the regulatory mirror image of least-wins-first and could be cited as evidence that practitioners understand tie rules as a collusion lever. I am inferring the anti-collusive intent from the wording ("in any manner that will discourage tie bids"; ban on "dividing business among tied bidders"; reporting to the attorney general); I did not find an official statement of purpose.
- The Italian, Brazilian, UN and West Virginia rules show that condition (3), a rebid restricted to the tied bidders, is established practice and in Italy is nearly a century old.

## 4. Has anyone argued that a tie-break rule can facilitate or deter collusion?

### Takeaway
Yes, but thinly: McAfee & McMillan (1992) argue a deterministic discriminatory tie-break deters identical-bid collusion; Puzzello (2008) shows experimentally that a *sharing* tie rule produces significantly more perfect collusion than a coin-toss rule in posted-price duopoly; OECD guidance and several US state rules treat tie resolution as an anti-collusion instrument. I found no theoretical or empirical paper that analyses a win-history-dependent tie-break rule.

### Cited Findings

- **McAfee & McMillan (1992), p. 586** [T]: the seller "can easily disrupt the mechanism by refusing to randomize" via "a deterministic tie-breaking rule"; cartels respond "by rotating their bids." (Full quote in Section 1.) — [PDF](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)

- **Puzzello, D. (2008). "Tie-breaking rules and divisibility in experimental duopoly markets." *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 67(1): 164–179. DOI 10.1016/j.jebo.2006.09.002.** [X] Abstract only. Posted-price duopoly with market power; treatments: tie rule (share vs random coin toss) × price-grid fineness. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jeborg/v67y2008i1p164-179.html)
  - "We find that the incidence of perfect collusion is significantly higher under the sharing tie-breaking rule than under the random (coin-toss) one, especially when the price space is less divisible."
  - Bearing [inference]: the nearest human-subject precedent for the paper's manipulation, and it supports H's direction. A sharing rule gives each tied seller a certain half; a coin toss gives the same expectation with risk. Least-wins-first is the sequential-auction counterpart of sharing (certain equal split over time). It also flags a design moderator: effects were strongest with a coarse price grid, so the bid grid in the LLM experiment matters.

- **Maskin, E. & Riley, J. (2000). "Equilibrium in Sealed High Bid Auctions." *Review of Economic Studies* 67(3): 439–454. DOI 10.1111/1467-937X.00138.** [T] Snippet (extract from the paper as reported by search) plus Crossref. — [author PDF](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/maskin/files/equilibrium_in_sealed_high_bid_auctions.pdf)
  - With discrete types, equilibrium may fail to exist "under the standard tie-breaking rule (in which the winner is selected at random)"; existence is restored when "the tying high bidders compete for the item in a second-round Vickrey auction."
  - Bearing [inference]: the canonical theory paper in which ties are resolved by a second-round auction among the tied bidders. It is a technical device for existence, not an anti-collusion argument, but it is the theoretical ancestor of a rebid tie-break and shows that a rebid makes the tie itself payoff-irrelevant in competitive equilibrium.

- **Jackson, M. O., Simon, L. K., Swinkels, J. M. & Zame, W. R. (2002). "Communication and Equilibrium in Discontinuous Games of Incomplete Information." *Econometrica* 70(5): 1711–1740. DOI 10.1111/1468-0262.00351.** [T] Crossref only; content from my background knowledge, not accessed in this session: equilibrium existence in auctions generally requires *endogenous* tie-breaking (sharing) rules that may depend on bidders' reported private information. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00351)
  - Bearing [inference]: establishes that tie-break rules are a substantive modelling choice in auction theory; not a collusion result.

- **Athey & Bagwell (2001); Chassang, S. & Ortner, J. (2019). "Collusion in Auctions with Constrained Bids: Theory and Evidence from Public Procurement." *Journal of Political Economy* 127(5): 2269–2300. DOI 10.1086/701812.** [T/E] Working-paper text read for the tie passage; abstract via search. — [WP](https://www.sylvainchassang.org/assets/papers/minimum_prices.pdf)
  - Model: "In the case of ties, we follow Athey and Bagwell (2001) and let the bidders jointly determine the allocation."
  - Result: "bidding constraints weaken cartels by limiting the scope for punishment"; introducing minimum prices in Japanese cities (Ibaraki prefecture, 2007–2016) lowered the right tail of winning bids.
  - Bearing [inference]: (i) theory treats tie allocation as something the cartel controls; an auctioneer rule that fixes tie allocation changes the cartel's toolkit. (ii) A price floor at which bids pile up and are resolved by lottery did not strengthen cartels in this evidence; it weakened them. That is a caution against assuming that more ties automatically means more collusion.

- **Hoernig, S. H. (2007). "Bertrand Games and Sharing Rules." *Economic Theory* 31(3): 573–585. DOI 10.1007/s00199-006-0112-8;** and **Baye, M. R. & Morgan, J. (2002). "Winner-take-all price competition." *Economic Theory* 19(2): 271–282. DOI 10.1007/PL00004212.** [T] Crossref only; content via search snippet: under a "winner-take-all" sharing rule a single randomly selected firm serves all demand at a price tie, versus equal sharing under the symmetric rule, and the equilibrium set of the one-shot Bertrand game depends on which rule applies. — [Hoernig, ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24057384_Bertrand_Games_and_Sharing_Rules)
  - Bearing [inference]: the Bertrand rationing-rule literature confirms tie rules can change equilibrium sets in static price games. I did not find a repeated-game paper comparing collusion sustainability across tie rules.

- **Sherstyuk, K. (1999). "Collusion Without Conspiracy: An Experimental Study of One-Sided Auctions." *Experimental Economics* 2(1): 59–75. DOI 10.1023/A:1009929832148.** [X] Abstract only (Crossref). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1009929832148)
  - "In the sealed bid experiments the outcomes converged to the competitive equilibrium. In the oral auctions, collusive outcomes emerged and were sustained with bidders using bid matching strategies... Therefore, the strict bid improvement rule becomes critical for breaking collusion." (In the oral auctions bidders could match the standing bid and the winner was drawn at random among the highest bidders, per the search description of the design.)
  - Bearing [inference]: evidence that *allowing ties plus random allocation* is collusion-facilitating in an open format, where matching is observable and punishable, and that the same subjects did not collude tacitly under sealed bids. Relevant to both sides of H: tie-allowing rules matter, but tacit identical bidding under sealed bids is hard for humans.

- **OECD (2009; 2025)** [L]: do not split among identical bidders; add criteria to resolve ties; investigate; consider re-tendering (quotes in Section 1). — [2025 PDF](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/09/oecd-guidelines-for-fighting-bid-rigging-in-public-procurement-2025-update_127880ea/cbe05a56-en.pdf)

- **Hawaii § 3-122-34; Utah S.B. 220 (2006); Louisiana LAC 34:V.529** [L]: tie rules framed to "discourage tie bids" (quotes in Section 3). — [Louisiana text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/louisiana/La-Admin-Code-tit-34-SS-V-529)

- **Anton, J. J. & Yao, D. A. (1992). "Coordination in Split Award Auctions." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 107(2): 681–707. DOI 10.2307/2118486.** [T] Crossref only; not read. Listed because split-award formats are the standard reference for implicit coordination when a contract can be divided between bidders. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2118486)

### Inferences
- Established: tie rules affect equilibrium existence and structure in static auction and Bertrand theory (Maskin & Riley; Jackson et al.; Hoernig; Baye & Morgan), a discriminatory deterministic tie rule is proposed as a cartel deterrent (M&M), and a sharing tie rule raised collusion relative to a coin toss in a posted-price experiment (Puzzello).
- Inference: the paper's three conditions map onto this literature as follows. Random = M&M / Baye–Morgan "winner-take-all" baseline. Least-wins-first = intertemporal sharing rule (Puzzello's "share" treatment, Anton–Yao split awards, the practice OECD 2009 warns against). BAFO rebid = Maskin–Riley second-round tie-break, OECD "re-issue the tender".
- Inference: a risk-neutral, patient bidder is indifferent between random and least-wins-first in expectation, so the standard repeated-game sustainability condition for identical bidding should be about the same under both. The rebid changes the stage game: a tie no longer delivers a 1/n chance at the collusive price but triggers a second auction in which each tied bidder knows the others matched and has an incentive to shade. Predicted ordering of collusion from this reasoning: least-wins-first ≥ random > rebid, with the first inequality driven by risk, fairness perception and focal-point effects, not by expected payoffs. This is my reasoning, not a published result.
- Inference: I found nobody who has studied a tie-break that conditions on win history. Subject to the limits of this search, the paper's condition (2) looks novel.

### Gaps
- No repeated-game theory paper found that compares collusion sustainability across tie-break rules in auctions. One may exist in the Bertrand-sharing-rule literature; I did not find it.
- Jackson et al. (2002), Hoernig (2007), Baye & Morgan (2002) and Anton & Yao (1992) were not read; do not attribute specific results to them without checking.
- I did not check Marshall & Marx (2012) or Kovacic et al. (2006) for tie-break recommendations.
- The search surfaced, but I did not read, a 2022 working paper relevant to algorithmic bidders: Banchio, M. & Skrzypacz, A., "Artificial Intelligence and Auction Design" (SSRN DOI 10.2139/ssrn.4033000), and an arXiv paper, "Algorithmic Collusion in Auctions: Evidence from Controlled Laboratory Experiments" (arXiv 2306.09437), whose search extract says tacit collusion in repeated auctions "can take the form of symmetrically suppressed bids or asymmetric bid rotation" and that ties were resolved uniformly at random. These belong to the algorithmic-collusion strand and should be checked by whoever covers it.

## 5. BAFO / rebid / multi-round sealed bids and collusion

### Takeaway
Auction theory treats a final sealed-bid stage as pro-competitive (Klemperer's Anglo-Dutch auction; Maskin & Riley's Vickrey tie-break; Perry, Wolfstetter & Zamir's two-stage sealed bid), and several procurement codes use a rebid among tied bidders. The main empirical study of rebids (Kawai & Nakabayashi 2022) shows that explicit cartels are *not* disrupted by them: in Japanese national procurement the first-round low bidder stayed lowest in the rebid about 97% of the time, even when the first-round margin was near zero. A rebid therefore helps only to the extent that the cartel cannot coordinate who should win it.

### Cited Findings

- Published abstract: "Our data set contains information on about 42,000 auctions whose award amount sums to about $40 billion. - Key fact (WP): "96.70% of cases, the lowest bidder in the first round is still the lowest bidder in the second round." - Anecdote reproduced from Porter (1994): a 1983 New York DOT resurfacing job was re-auctioned twice with the same bidders; the same firm was low each time and its bid *rose* by 20% and then a further 10%.
- Bearing [inference]: the strongest caution for the "rebid reopens competition" half of H. With pre-agreed designated winners, a rebid is a second chance to execute the same plan, and in the Japanese format the buyer even announces the lowest first-round bid. In the paper's setting there is no communication and tied bidders have no designated winner, so the mechanism that made Japanese rebids harmless to cartels is absent. The paper should say that, and should note that rebids are useful to *detect* collusion (persistence of rank across rounds).

**Ji, L. & Li, T. (2008). "Multi-round procurement auctions with secret reserve prices: theory and evidence." *Journal of Applied Econometrics* 23(7): 897–923. **Klemperer, P. (2002). "What Really Matters in Auction Design." *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 16(1): 169–189. DOI 10.1257/0895330027166.** [T/policy] Working-paper text read. — [PDF](https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/Users/Klemperer/designnew.pdf)
- Anglo-Dutch auction: ascending stage "until all but two bidders have dropped out. The two remaining bidders are then each required to make a best and final sealed-bid offer that is not lower than the current asking price, and the winner pays his bid."

- It "eliminates the final stage of the ascending auction, when just one excess bidder remains, in which phase an ascending auction is particularly vulnerable to collusive and predatory behavior."

- Bearing [inference]: the standard authority for the claim that a final sealed "best and final" round is pro-competitive: it removes the ability to observe and retaliate. Direct support for the design logic of condition (3), with the caveat that Klemperer's comparison is against ascending auctions, not against a single sealed-bid round.

- Bearing [inference]: a sealed first-price round invites defection from a ring; a sealed rebid among tied bidders is such a round.

### Inferences
- Established: (a) a sealed final round removes observation and immediate retaliation and so is less collusion-prone than open formats (Klemperer 2002; Hinloopen et al. 2020; Sherstyuk 1999); (b) explicit cartels with a designated winner pass through rebids almost untouched (Kawai & Nakabayashi 2022).
- Inference on whether a rebid helps: it depends on what makes the tie collusive. If bidders tie tacitly at a focal price with no designated winner (the paper's setting), a rebid turns the tie into a one-shot first-price auction among bidders who have just learned that the others matched; each has a strict incentive to shade slightly, and the tie no longer pays. If bidders can agree in advance who should win the rebid, the rebid changes nothing except to add a second round in which the others must bid high, i.e. it reintroduces cover bidding.
- Inference on gaming: cartels can game a rebid in at least three ways suggested by these sources. (1) Re-tie in the rebid, if the rules then fall back to lots (Italy: lots apply if tied bidders "non vogliano migliorare l'offerta"; UN: public drawing if BAFOs are "without result"). The paper needs to specify what happens on a second tie; that fallback rule becomes the effective tie-break. (2) Use the first round as a signal of who is "owed" and let that bidder shade in the rebid. (3) Avoid exact ties altogether and rotate with cover bids, which is the response M&M and Mund document. The paper's design can observe (1) and (3) directly.
- Inference: a rebid also has a competitive cost the paper might mention. If bidders anticipate a rebid they may bid less aggressively in round one, as in the multi-round declining-bid pattern of Ji & Li. That is not collusion but it does raise first-round bids.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that models or tests a rebid triggered specifically by an exact tie in a repeated procurement auction. The theoretical tie-break-by-second-auction (Maskin & Riley) is static and non-collusive.
- I found no empirical estimate of the price effect of BAFO stages in US federal procurement and nothing on BAFO in FCC or Treasury auctions; those leads from the brief did not produce sources.
- Perry, Wolfstetter & Zamir (2000) and Ji & Li (2008) were not read in full.
- The 96.70% persistence figure is from the 2014 working paper; the exact figure in the 2022 JPE version was not checked (the author's 2025 summary says "more than 95 percent").

## 6. Laboratory experiments with human subjects on tie-breaking, identical bids or bid rotation in repeated auctions

### Takeaway
Human subjects rarely reach tacit collusion in sealed-bid first-price auctions without communication, but do collude when the institution allows bid matching with random allocation (open auctions) or when communication is allowed, and one posted-price experiment shows a sharing tie rule raises perfect collusion relative to a coin toss. I found no human-subject experiment that varies the tie-break rule (random vs history-dependent vs rebid) in a repeated sealed-bid auction.

### Inferences
- Established: in human laboratories, tacit collusion in sealed-bid first-price auctions without communication is rare; identical prices appeared only with communication (Davis & Wilson 2002); collusion via matching bids appears when ties are allowed and observable (Sherstyuk 1999); a sharing tie rule raises perfect collusion relative to a coin toss in posted-price duopoly (Puzzello 2008).
- Inference: H predicts something humans mostly do not do (tacit identical bidding under sealed bids). If the LLM bidders do it, particularly under least-wins-first, the human benchmark makes the finding more striking, and the paper should say so. If they do not, the paper is consistent with the human evidence.
- Inference: the experimental literature supplies three design controls the paper should state explicitly: group size (Dufwenberg & Gneezy), bid-grid coarseness (Puzzello), and what feedback bidders receive after each round (Akai et al.; Skrzypacz & Hopenhayn's remark on limiting information).
- Inference: because rotation-like patterns arise without collusion (Davis & Wilson) and backlog-like patterns can be competitive (Kawai et al.), the paper's outcome measure for collusion should be the price level relative to the competitive benchmark together with the tie rate, not the evenness of the win distribution.

### Gaps
- No human-subject experiment found that manipulates the tie-break rule in a repeated sealed-bid auction, and none with a win-history-dependent tie rule or a tie-triggered rebid. Absence of evidence from a bounded search; a targeted search of experimental-economics working papers could still turn one up.
- Several experimental papers above are cited from abstracts or search extracts only (tagged individually). Design details that matter for comparison, in particular each experiment's own tie-break rule, group size and feedback, were not verified except where quoted.
- Kagel & Levin's handbook surveys of auction experiments (1995; 2016 update) were located but not read; they would be the place to confirm the general statement that tacit collusion is rare in sealed-bid laboratory auctions.
