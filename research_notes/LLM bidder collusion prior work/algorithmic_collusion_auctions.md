## Full-text update, 3 October 2026

**Heo, Ahn, Oh & Park (2025), "Algorithmic Collusion in Construction Bidding: Risks, Dynamics, and Regulatory Implications", J. Management in Engineering 41(5):04025045 — [FULL] (was [ABSTRACT]).** Read in full from a PDF supplied by the user.
- Setup (pp. 3-6): multiagent Q-learning, tabular (TQL) and deep (DQN) agents. Common cost: bidders' estimates are taken to be close to the engineer's estimate (WSDOT data, 1,244 projects, IQR outlier removal); actual cost drawn after bidding with (bid minus cost) over cost normal with mean 3.5% and sd 3.0%. Action: markup between 0.7 and 1.2 on an 11-point grid. State: bounded memory of rivals' past bids. Full transparency (bidder identities, prices, winner). Reward: (bid minus cost) over cost if the agent wins, 0 if it loses. Q-learning parameters: learning rate 0.1, discount 0.9, exploration decay 0.99 to a floor of 0.01. Convergence declared when every Q-value changes by less than 1e-6. 100 sessions per condition.
- Main experiment (pp. 6-8): two bidders, lowest bid wins; TQL against TQL, DQN against DQN, and "re-paired" versions where converged agents meet a new converged opponent with exploration off. Both TQL and DQN pairs ended at the highest allowed bid for both; durations in the figures: about 700,000 steps (TQL), 5,000 (DQN), 1,500 (ReP-TQL), 300 (ReP-DQN). ReP-DQN reached the cap after about 150 rounds, which the authors equate with a contractor's annual number of bids, in all 100 sessions.
- Award rules (pp. 7-8): four DQN agents, 100 sessions each, under lowest bid, second-lowest bid (winner paid the second-lowest bid) and closest-to-mean. All reached the cap eventually; mean-bid fastest and steadiest, second-lowest had temporary dips, lowest-bid settled slightly below the cap.
- Sample size (p. 9): variance-stability criterion on the final winning markup with threshold 0.01 gives minimum sessions of 23 (TQL), 96 (ReP-TQL), 83 (DQN), 45 (ReP-DQN).
- Tie handling: the paper says the lowest bid wins and does not say how ties are resolved or how rewards are set for tied bidders, although the reported end state (both agents at the cap on an 11-point grid) is an exact tie.
- Overlap / difference with OUR SETUP: procurement, lowest bid wins, repeated, no communication, award-rule manipulation. Differs: Q-learning not LLMs, common cost, discrete grid, tie rule unstated and not varied, no one-shot control, no Bayes-Nash benchmark, same algorithm on both sides. A second-price-style rule did not prevent collusion here, unlike Banchio & Skrzypacz's stateless learners.


**Heo, Park & Ahn (2024), "Potential AI-Driven Algorithmic Collusion and Influential Factors in Construction Bidding", J. Computing in Civil Engineering 38(4):04024016 — [FULL] (was [ABSTRACT]).** Read in full from a PDF supplied by the user.
- Setup (pp. 4-8): Q-learning bidders. All bidders share the same cost estimate (the engineer's estimate); the actual cost is drawn after bidding, with (bid minus cost) over cost distributed normal with mean 3.5% and standard deviation 3.0%. Action: a markup-competitiveness ratio (bid over estimate) chosen from 10 equally spaced points between 0.7 and 1.2. State: rivals' bids from the previous period (memory of one). All bids become public; the lowest ratio wins. Two reward functions: win-maximising and profit-maximising. Typical parameters: learning rate 0.1, exploration 0.1 decaying at 0.999.
- Validation (Table 2, p. 6): one learner against historical bids from 1,244 Washington State DOT projects won 77.5% of the time under the win objective and 57.6% under the profit objective, against a human mean ratio of 0.943.
- Findings (pp. 8-9, Table 3): with two bidders, win-maximising agents settled near 1.05; profit-maximising agents rose to 1.198, just under the 1.2 cap, and stayed there. Raising the number of bidders from 2 to 5 lowered the mean ratio from 1.198 to 1.093 (all profit-maximising). In 900 runs over learning rates 0.01 to 0.3 and exploration rates 0.1 to 0.9, most stayed above 1.06. Pairing the same two agents for thousands of episodes raised markups. Convergence needed hundreds of thousands of iterations (p. 10).
- Tie handling: the paper says the lowest ratio wins and does not say how exact ties are broken, even though a 10-point grid with 2 to 5 bidders must produce many ties. Another case where the tie rule is not reported.
- Overlap / difference with OUR SETUP: procurement, lowest bid wins, repeated, no communication, number of bidders varied. Differs: Q-learning not LLMs, common cost estimate (not private costs redrawn per round), discrete 10-point grid, tie rule unstated and not varied, no one-shot control, results are in markups against a cap, not against a Bayes-Nash benchmark. The profit-maximising result of sitting at the cap resembles the reserve-price prediction of Athey, Bagwell & Sanchirico (2004).


## Q1. What do Banchio & Skrzypacz and Banchio & Mantegazza find about first-price vs second-price auctions and feedback, and what follow-ups exist?

### Takeaway
Banchio & Skrzypacz (2022) find that two stateless Q-learners with a fixed common value collude in repeated first-price auctions (mean revenue 0.24 vs. a static-Nash 0.95) but not in second-price auctions, and that revealing the minimum-bid-to-win (which permits counterfactual "synchronous" updating) restores near-competitive bidding; Banchio & Mantegazza explain this as "spontaneous coupling" of asynchronous learners' estimates. Follow-ups show the result is fragile: it depends on optimistic initialisation, a high discount factor, symmetric algorithms and complete information, it disappears for mean-based no-regret learners with three or more top-value bidders, and in the one Bayesian (values redrawn each round) study bandit learners converge to the Bayes-Nash equilibrium.

### Cited Findings

#### 1. Banchio & Skrzypacz — "Artificial Intelligence and Auction Design" [FULL, arXiv v1 of 12 Feb 2022]
- Citation: Martino Banchio, Andrzej Skrzypacz. Working paper arXiv:2202.05947 (econ.TH), SSRN 4033000. Published only as a 2-page extended abstract: Proceedings of the 23rd ACM Conference on Economics and Computation (EC '22), pp. 30–31, DOI 10.1145/3490486.3538244. I found no full journal version. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3490486.3538244); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3490486.3538244)
- Setup: forward auction, infinitely repeated; 2 bidders (extensions: 3 bidders; 2 bidders + a non-strategic fringe bid ~U[0,1]); both value the object at v = 1, constant over time (complete information, no private types). Family of "α-auctions" (winner pays a convex combination of winning and losing bid), with first-price (FPA) and second-price (SPA) as the extremes. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Bid grid: 19 equally spaced bids 0.05, 0.10, …, 0.95; the top bid is deliberately kept strictly below value so that the static Nash equilibrium (both bid 0.95) is strict. Bid increment = 0.05. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Algorithm: tabular, **stateless** Q-learning (no memory of past bids; one Q-value per bid), ε-greedy with ε_t = 0.025·exp(−0.0002·t), γ = 0.99, α = 0.05, optimistic initialisation, asynchronous updating in the baseline. 1,000 runs × 1,000,000 periods; convergence = argmax of Q unchanged for the last 1,000 iterations; non-converged runs discarded. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Feedback in baseline: the auctioneer "reports solely whether they won or not" (bidder sees own payoff only). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Result 1: SPA converges to the static Nash equilibrium (revenue 0.95, no dispersion); FPA converges to much lower bids — average revenue per auction 0.24, with large dispersion across runs. Robust to never-decaying exploration (ε = 0.001 constant, 100 million rounds) and to 10M/100M-period runs. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- The collusive outcome is on the diagonal: "the algorithms do not converge on pairs of bids outside of the diagonal"; with persistent exploration "the two players spend most of the time on the diagonal (with equal bids), but do not settle in one place forever. Instead they move up and down" (stochastic cycles among several low equal-bid pairs). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Mechanism: in FPA the best deviation from a common bid is exactly one bid increment up, so after a break-up bidders re-coordinate at nearby low bids; in SPA any higher bid is equally profitable, so there is only an upward force. Tests: (Result 2) share of runs reaching static Nash rises monotonically as the payment rule moves from first- to second-price; (Result 3) restricting exploration to adjacent bids does not change the pattern; initialising at collusion (0.4, 0.4) — FPA stays collusive, SPA reverts; (Result 4) adding an artificial downward push in exploration produces below-Nash bids in both formats. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Feedback (Result 5): if the auctioneer reveals the highest competing bid / minimum bid to win, the Q-learner can update all bids counterfactually ("synchronous" updating); FPA then yields revenue "close to the static Nash equilibrium", with Edgeworth-cycle-like paths. Footnote: "Competition remains stuck in (0.9, 0.9) because of the discretization of the bid space. Finer discretizations approximate competition with higher and higher precision." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Google: the paper ties Result 5 to Google Ad Manager's 2019 switch from second- to first-price auctions, at which point Google began giving buyers "the minimum bid price to win after the auction closes". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Why not full collusion at 0.05: (Result 6) adding non-participation (negative) bids makes coordination on the lowest bid more frequent; (Result 7) a reserve price of 0.2 truncates the distribution and skews bids upward. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Number of bidders (Result 8): with 3 bidders collusion "is harder to sustain" at γ = 0.99 but "is restored" at γ = 0.999; SPA stays at static Nash. Fringe (Result 9): FPA bids rise, mass concentrates at 0.6–0.65 (optimal collusive bid against the fringe would be 0.5). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Benchmarks: static (one-shot) Nash revenue; fraction of runs converging to static Nash; heatmaps of converged bid pairs. Footnote 19 defines tacit collusion as "equilibria with revenues smaller than in the repetition of the static Nash equilibrium". No Δ-type index. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Authors' own caveats: open questions are asymmetric values/algorithms, time-varying valuations, and algorithms with memory; they warn that with memory, extra information "could backfire by facilitating instead of discouraging tacit collusion (for example, in the form of bid rotation)". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Overlap with our setup: repeated first-price, sealed bid, no communication, rule-based auctioneer, discrete bids, feedback as a design lever, collusion realised as **identical bids resolved by the tie-break**. Differences: forward not procurement; fixed common value (no private information, no redraw); Q-learning not LLMs; 2 bidders; tie-break rule never varied; 10^6 rounds rather than tens.
- A short non-technical companion exists: Banchio & Skrzypacz, "Market Design for AI Algorithms", ACM SIGecom Exchanges 20(2):62–68, Dec 2022, DOI 10.1145/3699804.3699809 [BIBLIO — page could not be fetched]. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3699804.3699809)

#### 2. Banchio & Mantegazza — "Artificial Intelligence and Spontaneous Collusion" / "Adaptive Algorithms and Collusion via Coupling" [PARTIAL, arXiv v5 of Sep 2023]
- Citation: Martino Banchio, Giacomo Mantegazza. arXiv:2202.05946 (v1 Feb 2022 – v5 Sep 2023, 61 pp.); earlier SSRN title "Games of Artificial Intelligence: A Continuous-Time Approach" (DOI 10.2139/ssrn.4032999). Published as a one-page abstract "Adaptive Algorithms and Collusion via Coupling", EC '23, p. 208, DOI 10.1145/3580507.3597726. Working paper; I found no journal version. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05946); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3580507.3597726)
- Content: a continuous-time (fluid) approximation of stateless reinforcement learners. "Spontaneous coupling" = an endogenous statistical linkage in the algorithms' estimates that lets them "periodically coordinate on actions that are more profitable than static Nash equilibria", without monitoring or reward-punishment strategies. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05946)
- Feedback: coupling requires non-uniform learning rates across actions (asynchronous updating, where only the played action is updated). With synchronous updating / counterfactual feedback the coupling disappears and learners reach competitive play; the paper proposes "learning-robust" mechanism design in which the designer supplies counterfactual feedback (a menu / price signal), and identifies Google's "minimum bid to win" as exactly such a menu description for a single-item auction. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05946)
- Auction application: a keyword-auction model (separate second-price auctions with reserve) where two Q-learners choose which keywords to bid on; they learn not to bid on the rival's branded keyword, i.e. market division, sustained by coupling. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05946)
- It summarises Banchio & Skrzypacz as finding "that additional feedback in first-price auctions restores competition". — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05946)
- Overlap: mechanism for tacit coordination without memory or communication; feedback as the lever. Differences: mostly Prisoner's Dilemma / Bertrand / keyword-selection games, not first-price procurement bidding; no private costs; not about ties.

#### 3. Bichler, Gupta, (Mathews,) Oberlechner — display-ad auction revenue with learning agents [SUMMARISED, arXiv v2 of May 2024]
- Citation: arXiv:2312.00243. v2 title "Low Revenue in Display Ad Auctions: Algorithmic Collusion vs. Non-Quasilinear Preferences" (authors Bichler, Gupta, Mathews, Oberlechner). Published as Martin Bichler, Alok Gupta, Matthias Oberlechner, "Revenue in First- and Second-Price Display Advertising Auctions: Understanding Markets with Learning Agents", Information Systems Research, online 17 Aug 2026, DOI 10.1287/isre.2025.2160 (Crossref lists three authors; volume/pages not yet assigned). — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/isre.2025.2160)
- Setup A (complete information, replication of Banchio–Skrzypacz): 2 bidders, v = 1, discrete bids from 0.05 to 0.95, T = 5×10^5 iterations, 10 runs; Q-learning (α = 0.05, γ ∈ {0.25, 0.99}, optimistic vs zero initialisation, decaying vs fixed ε), Exp3, ε-greedy, UCB1, Thompson sampling; bandit feedback (own payoff/win only). — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
- Setup B (incomplete information): N = 2 or 3, values drawn i.i.d. U[0,1] **each round**, 21 discrete bids for the bandit learners (64 grid points for the SODA equilibrium solver), T = 10^7. — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
- Result 1: the Banchio–Skrzypacz collusion is reproduced but "rel[ies] on the parameters of the Q-values. We find evidence that optimistic initialization enables collusive outcomes while lower initializations or lower discount rates lead to higher prices closer to the equilibrium." — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
- Result 2: with heterogeneous algorithms (Q-learning vs Exp3) "agents bid significantly higher compared to the symmetric case with identical Q-learners"; Exp3 vs Exp3 converges to Nash. "collusion is not robust even in a market with only symmetric Q-learning agents but different hyperparameters." — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
- Bayesian setting: no systematic collusion with Exp3; agents learn the Bayes-Nash equilibrium (BNE). The paper invokes Ahunbay & Bichler's uniqueness result for the Bayesian coarse correlated equilibrium of the first-price auction as the reason no-regret learners must converge to BNE. — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
- Alternative explanation of low first-price revenue: ROI- or ROS-maximising (non-quasilinear) bidders have lower equilibrium revenue in first-price than second-price auctions; "The difference in revenue shrinks with increasing levels of competition." (Summary reported FP ≈ 0.70 vs SP ≈ 0.82 for ROI bidders with N = 2 — unverified number.) — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
- Metrics: revenue vs. BNE revenue; relative utility loss ℓ = 1 − u(s)/u(best response) with target ℓ < 10^-3; median bids per 2,000-iteration window. — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
- Overlap: the Bayesian treatment (values redrawn each round, sealed first-price, bandit feedback) is the closest learning-algorithm analogue to our information structure, and it finds **no** collusion. Differences: forward auction; uniform values; no memory; ties random and not studied.
- Caveat: the summariser described the complete-information grid both as "{0.05, 0.10, …, 0.95}" and as "10 discrete bids" (the former implies 19); check the paper.

#### 4. Kolumbus & Nisan — "Auctions between Regret-Minimizing Agents" [PARTIAL]
- Citation: Yoav Kolumbus, Noam Nisan. Proceedings of the ACM Web Conference 2022 (WWW '22), pp. 100–111, DOI 10.1145/3485447.3512055; arXiv:2110.11855. Published. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3485447.3512055)
- Setup: repeated single-item first- and second-price auctions and generalised first/second-price (ad-slot) auctions; two agents with fixed values v ≥ w; bids on a discrete ε-grid; multiplicative weights / FTPL and general mean-based no-regret algorithms; "if agents place equal bids, ties are broken uniformly at random." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855)
- Second-price (Theorem 1): with multiplicative-weights agents the high-value player always wins but "pays an average price that is strictly less than the second price"; in the v = 1, w = 0.5 simulation the average price is about 0.27. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855)
- First-price (Theorem 2): for mean-based agents, "if the dynamics converge to any single distribution, then the high player wins with probability approaching 1 and pays an average price that converges to the second price (up to O(ε))". Convergence in the symmetric case is "very slow, perhaps the slowest that we have seen". So for these learners first-price is the *more* competitive format — the opposite ranking to Banchio–Skrzypacz. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855)
- First-price auctions do have low-revenue coarse correlated equilibria on identical bids, e.g. equal weight on (0,0), (0.46,0.46), (0.64,0.64), (0.73,0.73) for v = w = 1, and some (non-mean-based) regret-minimisers could converge to them. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855)
- Generalised first-price auction, v = w = 1: bids stay nearly identical ("both agents bid nearly the same value") and cycle; agents' utility ≈ 0.52 vs 0.5 at Nash — "a weak form of implicit collusion". In the meta-game where users misreport values to their agents, "severe implicit collusion occurs, where the users capture 89% of the total welfare, leaving only 11% to the auctioneer (in contrast to 33% to the auctioneer in the Nash equilibrium...)". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855)
- Overlap: repeated sealed-bid, discrete grid, no communication. Differences: fixed values, forward auction, no-regret algorithms, focus on value-reporting meta-game.

#### 5. Deng, Hu, Lin, Zheng — "Nash Convergence of Mean-Based Learning Algorithms in First-Price Auctions" [PARTIAL, arXiv v5 of Aug 2025]
- Citation: Xiaotie Deng, Xinyan Hu, Tao Lin, Weiqiang Zheng. Proceedings of the ACM Web Conference 2022, pp. 141–150, DOI 10.1145/3485447.3512059; arXiv:2110.03906 (v5, Aug 2025, extended). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03906); [Crossref search record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3485447.3512059)
- Setup: N ≥ 2 bidders with fixed integer values, integer bids {0,…,V}, repeated first-price auction, mean-based algorithms (MWU, FTPL, Exp3, ε_t-greedy). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03906)
- Result depends on the number of bidders sharing the highest value: ≥ 3 → converges almost surely to Nash in time-average and last-iterate; exactly 2 → time-average convergence but not necessarily last-iterate; exactly 1 → may not converge. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03906)
- With exactly two top bidders there are two equilibria (both bid v₁−1 or both bid v₁−2); in simulations with v₁ = v₂ = 4, ε_t-greedy converged to the v₁−1 equilibrium in 774 of 1000 runs, while MWU always converged to v₁−1. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03906)

- Overlap: discrete first-price, role of number of bidders. Differences: theory; fixed values; no collusion framing.

#### 6. Rawat — "Designing Auctions when Algorithms Learn to Bid" (v2 title: "Algorithmic Collusion in Auctions: Evidence from Controlled Laboratory Experiments") [SUMMARISED v2 and v3; ABSTRACT v1]
- Citation: Pranjal Rawat. arXiv:2306.09437; v1 15 Jun 2023, v2 19 Jan 2025, v3 20 Mar 2026. Working paper; no journal reference on arXiv. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09437)
- v1 abstract: 427 trials with Q-learning bidders in up to 250,000 auctions; first-price auctions "vulnerable to coordinated bid suppression" with winning bids about 20% below values, second-price aligns bids with values; recommends switching from first- to second-price. — [arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09437v1)
- v2 design: factorial experiments varying format (first/second price), bidders {2, 4, 6}, reserve price, learning rate, discount factor γ ∈ {0.0,…,0.99}, ε-greedy vs Boltzmann exploration, asynchronous vs synchronous updating, information feedback (previous winning bid and/or median of others' bids included in the state), constant vs affiliated values, Q-learning vs LinUCB contextual bandits. Bid grid typically {0, 0.1, …, 1.0}. — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2)
- v2 results: first-price lowers revenue by 0.0945 (constant values), 0.1185 (affiliated values), 0.2140 (LinUCB), all p < 0.0001; more bidders dampen the first-price loss for Q-learning but worsen it for bandits; Boltzmann exploration and synchronous updates mitigate first-price losses; including the previous winning bid in the state has no significant effect on the first-price revenue gap (p = 0.888). — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2)
- v3 results (re-framed): "structural market parameters dominate algorithmic design choices"; number of bidders has effects of about 11–29% of grand-mean revenue across experiments; the format effect reverses by algorithm class (negligible for Q-learning, second-price better for contextual bandits, first-price better under budget-constrained pacing); "no single auction format is universally superior when bidders are algorithms". — [arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v3)
- Metrics: late-round revenue, seller regret = 1 − revenue, time to convergence, price volatility, no-sale rate, **winner entropy** (Shannon entropy of winner identity). — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2)
- Overlap: explicitly factorial over format, bidder count, feedback, with affiliated/private signals. Differences: forward auction; RL/bandit agents; the conclusions shifted substantially between versions, so cite the version.

#### 8. Brânzei, Derakhshan, Golrezaei, Han — "Learning and Collusion in Multi-unit Auctions" [SUMMARISED]

#### 9. Further no-regret / equilibrium-learning items (second-hand or abstract only)

## Q2. How are ties handled, does bid-grid coarseness matter, and does any paper report that the tie-break rule or discretisation changes collusion, or that algorithms coordinate on identical bids / bid rotation?

### Takeaway
**No paper I found manipulates the tie-break rule as a treatment**; every simulation study breaks ties uniformly at random (or by a fixed label order in theory papers) and treats this as an implementation detail. Coordination on *identical* bids is nonetheless the central collusive pattern in Banchio–Skrzypacz (and appears in Kolumbus–Nisan's generalised first-price dynamics), grid discreteness is known to create extra low-price equilibria and to bound how competitive outcomes can get, and learned bid *rotation* is discussed theoretically but not reported as an emergent outcome in any of these simulations.

### Cited Findings
- Tie rule by paper:
  - Banchio & Skrzypacz: not stated as a formal rule in the model section of v1; implied 50/50 (bidders at equal bids "start winning on average half of the time"; Appendix A: "win with probability 1/2"). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
  - Kolumbus & Nisan: "ties are broken uniformly at random". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855)
  - Deng et al.: "If there are more than one highest bidders, the good is allocated to one of them uniformly at random." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03906)
  - Bichler et al.: "Ties are broken randomly". — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
  - Rawat: "Ties for the top bid are resolved by uniformly random selection among all tying bidders." — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2)
  - Decarolis et al.: in the Q-learning implementation "if m bidders submit the same bids, each of them wins the slot with a probability 1/m"; in the theoretical model ties are "broken according to bidders' labels". — [IGIER WP 708](https://igier.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/media/publication/708_0.pdf)
  - Brânzei et al.: lexicographic order of player names [SUMMARISED]. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.17402v2)
  - Sadoune et al. (procurement, see Q5): tied low bidders share the contract in proportion to market power, which in the homogeneous case is "mathematically equivalent to a random selection mechanism for breaking ties" [SUMMARISED]. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03521)
  - Calvano et al.: differentiated-products logit demand, so exact price ties do not determine a winner. — [2019 WP](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Identical bids as the collusive outcome: in Banchio–Skrzypacz first-price runs, converged outcomes lie only on the diagonal (equal bids), and with persistent exploration the bidders "spend most of the time on the diagonal (with equal bids)" cycling among low equal-bid pairs; the explanation given is that an off-diagonal loser earns zero and would deviate. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Identical bids under no-regret learning: in Kolumbus–Nisan's symmetric generalised first-price simulation "both agents bid nearly the same value and thus only a single bid-line is visible"; with asymmetric values the empirical distribution is "nearly diagonal". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855)
- Grid/tie-induced equilibrium multiplicity: with two equal top-value bidders, a discrete grid and random tie-breaking, Deng et al. have two Nash equilibria (both bid v₁−1 or both bid v₁−2), and ε-greedy reaches the higher-bid one in only 774 of 1000 runs (the paper says both equilibria are obtained across runs; I did not read the count for the lower one); Bichler et al. similarly note "with two agents and a random tie-breaking rule, the NE is to bid b₁=b₂=0.95 or b₁=b₂=0.90". — [Deng et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03906); [Bichler et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
- Same phenomenon in pricing: Banchio & Mantegazza note that in a discretised Bertrand game there are two Nash equilibria and "The multiplicity is a consequence of the discretization of the space in equally spaced prices." — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05946)
- Grid in Banchio–Skrzypacz: the bid increment (0.05) is central to the mechanism ("the optimal deviation is just by one bid increment"); the critical discount factors for collusive equilibria depend on the number of grid points m (γ*_FPA = (m−2)/(2m−3) < γ*_SPA = m/(2m−1), both → 1/2 as m grows); under synchronous updating "Finer discretizations approximate competition with higher and higher precision." They do not report simulations with a varied grid. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Grid in Calvano et al.: raising the number of feasible prices from 15 to 50 or 100 lowers the average profit gain "slightly, but with m = 100 it is still a substantial 70%" (vs 85% baseline); they conjecture this is because more actions need more exploration. — [2019 WP](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Grid in Rawat: v2/v3 state that changing grid granularity did not materially alter results, with no systematic variation reported [SUMMARISED]; a search-result snippet for the 2023 version says payment rules matter more with "coarse bid spaces" [snippet only, unverified]. — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2)
- Grid in theory: Ipsen & Skoulakis bound the revenue shortfall of no-swap-regret bidders in a first-price auction by Θ(1/k) for k discrete bids [ABSTRACT]. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06085)
- Bid rotation: Banchio–Skrzypacz analyse bid-rotation equilibria in Appendix A (rotation needs γ ≥ ≈0.62 in first-price as m → ∞ but is sustainable for γ → 0 in second-price) and stress that their stateless learners "are not designed to learn conditional strategies like the bid rotation scheme"; they flag rotation as a risk if bidders with memory are given more feedback. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Market division rather than rotation: Banchio & Mantegazza's keyword-auction application has advertisers learn "not to bid on their competitor's favorite keyword, thus implicitly splitting the market". — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05946)
- Rawat tracks "winner entropy" (Shannon entropy of the winner-identity distribution), the only metric in this literature that speaks to how wins are shared across bidders; in v2, adding the median of others' bids to a LinUCB context reduces winner entropy under first-price (p ≈ 0.036) [SUMMARISED]. — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2)
- Deng et al. cite Chen & Peng (2023), "Complexity of Equilibria in First-Price Auctions under General Tie-Breaking Rules", a computational-complexity result rather than a learning/collusion study [cited-by only; not retrieved]. — [Deng et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03906)

### Inferences
- Under random tie-breaking, tying at a low bid is how two symmetric learners split the collusive surplus in Banchio–Skrzypacz. A tie-break rule that removes the expected 50% share from an exact match (e.g. a best-and-final rebid) or that allocates ties deterministically by win history (least-wins-first, which mechanically implements rotation) changes exactly the payoff that makes the diagonal absorbing. That the tie rule is a design lever for algorithmic collusion appears to be untested in the learning-algorithm literature — a clean novelty claim, subject to the search limits below.
- Because discrete grids plus random ties generate additional equal-bid equilibria just below the competitive bid (Deng et al.; Bichler et al.), some "below-Nash" outcomes on coarse grids are static equilibria, not collusion. Any collusion index in our setting should be computed against the equilibrium of the *discretised* game under the specific tie rule.
- The least-wins-first rule has no analogue in this literature; the nearest concept is bid rotation, which appears only as theory (Banchio–Skrzypacz Appendix A, citing McAfee & McMillan 1992 and Skrzypacz & Hopenhayn 2004).

### Gaps
- "None found" is based on targeted searches (queries combining tie-breaking, algorithmic collusion, auction, identical bids, discretisation) and on reading the tie-handling passages of the papers above; it is not an exhaustive systematic review. A paper that varies the tie rule in an appendix could have been missed.
- I did not verify the tie rule in Heo et al. (construction bidding) or Tian (procurement poster); only abstracts were available.
- No simulation paper I read reports emergent bid rotation among learning algorithms; I cannot rule out such a result in the electricity-market agent-based literature, which I only skimmed via search snippets.

## Q3. How do information-disclosure rules and the number of bidders affect algorithmic collusion in auctions?

### Takeaway
The effect of disclosure depends on how algorithms use it: information that enables counterfactual (full-feedback) updating — e.g. minimum-bid-to-win — removes collusion among stateless Q-learners, while information used as a conditioning state or about the environment has null or pro-collusive effects in other studies. More bidders reduce but do not always eliminate collusion, and patience can substitute for concentration.

### Cited Findings
- Winner-only / own-payoff feedback → collusion in first-price; revealing the highest rival bid (minimum bid to win) with synchronous updating → revenue close to static Nash (Banchio–Skrzypacz Result 5). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- General principle: coupling arises when learning rates differ across actions (only the played action is updated); "providing counterfactual feedback may avoid spontaneous coupling's occurrence" (Banchio & Mantegazza). — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05946)
- Same pattern in pricing: synchronous learners compete down to cost, asynchronous learners collude (Asker–Fershtman–Pakes, as described by Banchio–Skrzypacz). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Null effect when disclosure enters the state: in Rawat v2, adding the previous winning bid to the Q-learner's state does not significantly change the first-price revenue gap (p = 0.888), nor does the median of others' bids (p = 0.489); synchronous updating does curtail first-price losses [SUMMARISED]. — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2)
- Pro-collusive direction: Decarolis et al. find "the auctioneer revenues tend to decline as more complete information is available to the advertiser bidding algorithms" (information here = knowledge of auction rules via the learner type and informative Q-initialisation, not bid disclosure). — [IGIER WP 708](https://igier.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/media/publication/708_0.pdf)
- Pro-collusive direction in procurement: Tian's poster abstract reports that "disclosing past winning bids or anchoring reserves to historical prices, can inadvertently amplify collusive tendencies" among DQN bidders in second-price procurement auctions [ABSTRACT]. — [WTAMU repository](https://wtamu-ir.tdl.org/items/47040bd7-cc58-4403-b5b8-5f3aeb9b56db)
- Warning in Banchio–Skrzypacz: with memory-equipped algorithms, "providing information could backfire by facilitating instead of discouraging tacit collusion (for example, in the form of bid rotation)". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Full-information vs bandit feedback is a modelled treatment in Brânzei et al. (all bids revealed vs only the clearing price and own allocation), with regret bounds O(√(TK³ log T)) vs O(min{(T³K⁷ log T)^{1/4}, KT}) [SUMMARISED; the summary's statement that bandit feedback limits collusion should be checked against the paper]. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.17402v2)
- Empirical (not a simulation; bidders share a third-party autobidder): Eschenbaum, "Shared Bidding Algorithms and Competition: Evidence from Electricity Markets", arXiv:2607.13002, 14 Jul 2026, working paper [ABSTRACT]. In the Australian National Electricity Market, bids from batteries using the same provider co-move, "more strongly after a disclosure reform made the scarcity state easier to observe"; estimated conduct parameter close to one where a provider's share of near-margin battery capacity exceeds roughly 30%; cost to consumers an annualised $5.5 million. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13002)
- Number of bidders:
  - Banchio–Skrzypacz: with 3 bidders collusion is harder at γ = 0.99 and restored at γ = 0.999; a random fringe bidder pushes first-price bids to 0.6–0.65. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
  - Calvano et al. (pricing): Δ falls from 85% (n = 2) to 64% (n = 3) and 56% (n = 4). — [2019 WP](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
  - Deng et al.: with ≥ 3 bidders sharing the top value, mean-based learners converge to Nash almost surely; with 2, only in time-average. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03906)
  - Rawat v3: number of bidders is "the strongest driver of revenue" (effects ≈ 11–29% of grand mean) [SUMMARISED]. — [arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v3)
  - Bichler et al.: the first-/second-price revenue gap for ROI bidders "shrinks with increasing levels of competition" (N = 2 → 3). — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
  - Sadoune et al. (procurement): joint collusive-action frequency for UCB falls from 58% (2 homogeneous players) to 43% (5 homogeneous) and 8% (5 heterogeneous); other algorithms fall from 3–17% to 1–2% [SUMMARISED]. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03521)
  - Tian (poster abstract) reports DQN bidders overbid "especially under high competition" yet also that "limiting bidder numbers ... significantly reduce[s] overbidding" — the direction is unusual and I could not check it beyond the abstract. — [WTAMU repository](https://wtamu-ir.tdl.org/items/47040bd7-cc58-4403-b5b8-5f3aeb9b56db)

### Inferences
- "Winner only / winning bid / all bids" does not map onto a monotone collusion ranking for learning algorithms. The useful distinction is (a) disclosure that lets a bidder evaluate counterfactual bids against the last opponent profile (pro-competitive for stateless learners) versus (b) disclosure that enlarges the state on which strategies can condition (neutral to pro-collusive). LLM bidders with the full history in context are closer to (b).
- No learning-algorithm study I found runs a clean three-arm disclosure treatment (winner only vs winning bid vs all bids) in a first-price auction with private values.

### Gaps
- No verified result on "all bids revealed" vs "winning bid only" for learning bidders in single-unit first-price auctions.
- Tian's result is a poster abstract with no accessible paper; treat as unverified.

## Q4. Which benchmarks and indices quantify collusion in auctions, and does anyone use a memoryless / one-shot control?

### Takeaway
The auction papers mostly report raw revenue or bids against the static (one-shot) Nash or Bayes-Nash benchmark rather than a normalised index; the Calvano et al. profit-gain index Δ is the standard in pricing and is easily transplanted. Memoryless/myopic controls exist in the pricing literature (Calvano et al. find Δ < 5% with no memory), but in auctions the headline collusion result is itself obtained with memoryless learners, so "memoryless" is not a clean no-collusion control for RL.

### Cited Findings
- Calvano et al. index: Δ ≡ (π − π^N)/(π^M − π^N), with π^N the static Bertrand-Nash profit and π^M the monopoly (full-collusion) profit; "Δ = 0 corresponds to the competitive outcome and Δ = 1 to the perfectly collusive outcome"; chosen because it "can be compared directly across different economic settings". — [2019 WP](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Calvano et al. controls: "collusion is impossible when k = 0 (the algorithms have no memory), and it cannot arise in equilibrium when δ = 0"; "Turning to the case of memoryless algorithms, we find profit gains of less than 5%", attributed to discretisation; as δ falls the profit gain drops "from over 80% to a modest 16%" at δ = 0.35 (then rises slightly at lower δ because δ also changes the effective learning rate). They criticise earlier work that found supra-competitive prices with myopic, memoryless agents as evidence of failed learning rather than collusion. — [2019 WP](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Banchio–Skrzypacz: benchmark is static Nash revenue (0.95) vs realised revenue (0.24 in FPA), plus share of runs converging to static Nash; their learners are stateless, yet collude. They note that for stateless learners "Nash equilibrium theory would predict that collusion should not be possible in either of the formats." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)
- Banchio & Mantegazza argue the stateless outcome is still collusion (sustained by coupling, not punishment). — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05946)
- Xu & Zhao, "Memoryless Algorithmic Collusion: Sure to Fail, Slow to Fall", arXiv:2409.01147 (v1 Sep 2024, v2 Aug 2026), working paper [ABSTRACT]: in Bertrand-style games "memoryless Q-learning algorithms should adapt to Nash Equilibrium given sufficient explorations in the long-run", but time to convergence grows super-exponentially at high discount factors. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.01147)
- Discount-factor control in auctions: Bichler et al. find Q-learning with γ = 0.25 or zero initialisation converges near the Nash bid, while γ = 0.99 with optimistic initialisation colludes [SUMMARISED]. Rawat varies γ from 0.0 to 0.99 as a factor [SUMMARISED]. — [Bichler et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2); [Rawat v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2)
- Bayesian benchmark: Bichler et al. compare learned bidding with the BNE computed numerically (SODA) and use relative utility loss ℓ = 1 − u_i(s)/u_i(best response) < 10^-3 as the equilibrium test. — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2)
- Rawat: seller regret = 1 − realised revenue per round (values normalised to 1), late-round revenue, time to convergence (rolling revenue within ±5% of final mean), price volatility, no-sale rate, winner entropy; v3 adds liquid welfare and price of anarchy; explicitly no one-shot control [SUMMARISED]. — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2); [arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v3)
- Kolumbus–Nisan: split of welfare between auctioneer and bidders vs. the Nash split (e.g. 11% to auctioneer vs 33% at Nash). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855)
- Sadoune et al.: frequency of the joint "collusive price" action at the final episode, in a game with a binary fair/collusive action; stateless bandits serve as the memoryless comparison to stateful deep RL, and "There is no correlation between algorithmic sophistication and collusive potential" [SUMMARISED]. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03521)
- Decarolis et al.: auctioneer revenue and allocative efficiency relative to equilibrium/competitive benchmarks in GSP and VCG. — [IGIER WP 708](https://igier.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/media/publication/708_0.pdf)
- Terminology caution: a search-result excerpt of recent work states that the literature uses "algorithmic collusion" to denote any algorithmic revenue shortfall relative to competitive benchmarks [snippet only; source paper not identified with certainty]. Calvano et al. instead insist on strategy-based evidence (punishment after deviation). — [Rawat v2 (search hit)](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09437v2); [2019 WP](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a procurement auction the natural analogue is Δ = (π − π^BNE)/(π^M − π^BNE) on bidder profit, or equivalently procurement cost relative to the BNE cost and to the reserve/monopoly cost, with π^BNE computed for the discretised game under the specific tie rule (see Q2).
- A one-shot / no-history control for LLM bidders is the counterpart of Calvano et al.'s k = 0 test and separates repeated-game coordination from level effects (e.g. generic bid shading or grid artefacts). The RL-auction literature offers no ready-made equivalent because its learners are already stateless and learning is itself the repeated interaction.
- Outcome-based indices alone do not distinguish collusion from failure to learn; Calvano et al.'s deviation/impulse-response test is the stricter standard, and none of the auction papers above runs it.

### Gaps
- I found no auction paper that reports the Calvano-style Δ index explicitly; this is an absence in what I read, not a verified universal negative.
- AER-published values may differ slightly from the 2019 working paper quoted here; the 85/64/56% figures match what search results attribute to the published version, but I did not open the AER text.

## Q5. Is there work on algorithmic collusion in procurement / reverse auctions or public tenders specifically?

### Takeaway
Very little, and none in a top economics or CS venue: one arXiv working paper on a stylised "minimum price" public-procurement game, two construction-management journal papers with RL bidders, and one unpublished conference poster on DQN bidders in second-price procurement. None features private costs redrawn each round under first-price rules with a tie-break treatment.

### Cited Findings
- Sadoune, Joanis, Lodi — "Algorithmic Collusion And The Minimum Price Markov Game", arXiv:2407.03521 (v1 Jul 2024, v3 Mar 2025), working paper [SUMMARISED]. Lowest-bid-wins first-price procurement modelled as a repeated Markov game with a **binary** action (fair price vs collusive price 30% higher); 2 or 5 players, homogeneous or heterogeneous "market power"; complete information; agents observe action frequencies and average rewards; tested ε-greedy, Thompson sampling, UCB (stateless), D3QN and MAPPO (stateful); 100 training auctions × 100 replications. Final joint collusive-action frequency: UCB 58%/55% (2 players homogeneous/heterogeneous), 43% (5 homogeneous), 8% (5 heterogeneous); D3QN 16–17% → 2%; MAPPO 10–11% → 1%; TS 4% → 1%; ε-greedy 3% → 1%. Conclusion: "The minimum price rule is generally resilient to non-engineered tacit coordination among rational actors". (The summary mentions six methods but lists five.) — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.03521); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03521)
  - Overlap: public procurement, lowest price wins, repeated, no communication, 2 vs 5 bidders. Differences: two-action game rather than a bid grid; no private cost draws; ties shared/random and not varied; RL not LLM.
- Heo, Park, Ahn — "Potential AI-Driven Algorithmic Collusion and Influential Factors in Construction Bidding", Journal of Computing in Civil Engineering 38(4):04024016, July 2024, DOI 10.1061/JCCEE5.CPENG-5683 [SUPERSEDED, now FULL TEXT, see update block at top; was ABSTRACT; bibliographic details from the SNU repository listing, not Crossref]. AI bidding agents in repeated simulated construction bidding "tend to develop cooperative strategies over time, leading to higher bids"; collusion is facilitated by frequent interaction and by profit-maximising objectives, and hindered by objectives that maximise the number of wins. — [ASCE](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/abs/10.1061/JCCEE5.CPENG-5683); [SNU repository](https://s-space.snu.ac.kr/handle/10371/203344)
- Heo, Ahn, Oh, Park — "Algorithmic Collusion in Construction Bidding: Risks, Dynamics, and Regulatory Implications", Journal of Management in Engineering 41(5), article 04025045, Sept 2025, DOI 10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-6704 [SUPERSEDED, now FULL TEXT, see update block at top; was ABSTRACT; Crossref-verified]. RL-based simulation under various market conditions and **bid evaluation methods**: "algorithmic bidders gradually can converge to collusive strategies, consistently leading to supracompetitive prices"; "they exhibited collusive coordination even when matched with a new opponent"; "different bid evaluation methods influence the speed and stability of this collusive behavior"; risk highest with "limited competition and price-focused evaluations". — [SNU Pure](https://snu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/algorithmic-collusion-in-construction-bidding-risks-dynamics-and--2/); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-6704)
  - Overlap: procurement (lowest-price and other award rules), repeated, RL bidders, award-rule manipulation. Differences: algorithm class, cost structure, grid and tie handling unknown to me (abstract only).
- Tian, Jingyi — "AI-Bidding in Procurement", research poster, West Texas A&M University 2026 Faculty and Student Research Poster Session [ABSTRACT; not peer-reviewed, no paper found]. Independent DQN bidders in **second-price** procurement auctions with private costs "systematically overbid relative to their private costs"; attributed to experience replay over-representing high-payoff overbids; 2×2 design (competition intensity × reserve price); capping reserves and limiting bidder numbers reduce overbidding; disclosing past winning bids amplifies it. — [WTAMU repository](https://wtamu-ir.tdl.org/items/47040bd7-cc58-4403-b5b8-5f3aeb9b56db)
- Adjacent supply-side auctions (electricity): Eschenbaum (2026, arXiv:2607.13002) is empirical evidence on shared autobidding software in the Australian electricity market [ABSTRACT]; Brânzei et al. motivate their uniform-price analysis with electricity and emission-permit auctions [SUMMARISED]; an unreviewed GitHub project reports that independent Q-learners in a uniform-price electricity auction reach high prices across 50-seed sweeps but that its behavioural tests do not establish collusion ("high prices yes, collusion not proven") [repository description only]. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13002); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.17402v2); [GitHub](https://github.com/pradeep221b/algorithmic-collusion-auction)
- Out of scope but surfaced by the searches (LLM bidders; presumably covered by another researcher): Tolety, "Tacit Bidder-Side Collusion: Artificial Intelligence in Dynamic Auctions", arXiv:2511.21802 (Nov 2025) — LLM bidders in repeated Dutch auctions with platform-posted payouts, supra-competitive prices in small markets that vanish as bidder numbers grow [ABSTRACT]; and an ASCE Journal of Management in Engineering article "Tacit Collusion by LLM Agents in Construction Bidding: Evidence from a Simulated Bidding Environment" (DOI 10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144) [title only, from search results]. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21802); [ASCE](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144)

### Inferences
- A first-price procurement auction with fixed common cost is strategically a mirror image of the Banchio–Skrzypacz forward auction (and, as Deng et al. note, of Bertrand competition with fixed costs), so their results transfer in principle; the genuinely different ingredient in our setup is private costs redrawn each round, where the learning literature points toward BNE convergence rather than collusion.
- Procurement-specific work so far varies award/evaluation rules (Heo et al.) and reserve/disclosure (Tian) but, as far as I could determine, not the tie-break rule.

### Gaps
- I could not access full texts of either Heo et al. paper (ASCE returned 403), so their algorithm, number of bidders, cost structure, grid and tie handling are unknown to me.
- I found no algorithmic-collusion simulation of public tenders in economics journals (Econometrica, AER, RAND, JET, GEB) or NBER working papers; the searches were keyword-based and may miss papers framed as "bid rigging" or "agent-based" rather than "algorithmic collusion".
- The older agent-based electricity-market literature on tacit collusion among learning generators (pre-2015) surfaced only as search snippets; I did not verify any individual citation and therefore list none.
