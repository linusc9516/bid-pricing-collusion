# Statistical screens and metrics for detecting / quantifying bidder collusion (procurement auctions and simulated-agent studies), as of October 2026

Reading conventions for these notes.

- Paper metrics referred to throughout: **(i)** collusion index = (mean winning bid − Bayes-Nash winning bid) / (reserve − Bayes-Nash winning bid), unclipped; **(ii)** index minus the same index in a matched one-shot (no-history) control; **(iii)** share of rounds won by the lowest-cost bidder; **(iv)** win-count chi-square vs uniform, and repeat-win rate; **(v)** gap between losing bids and the winning bid (cover-bid clustering); **(vi)** rate of exactly tied lowest bids (early vs late) and price level of ties; **(vii)** rebid price minus original tied bid.
- Verification tags: **[FT]** = I read the full text (PDF text extraction, targeted passages); **[FT-WP]** = I read a working-paper / preprint version, not the published version, so published numbers may differ; **[ABS]** = abstract or search snippet only; **[2nd]** = known only through another source's description. Bibliographic details marked "Crossref-verified" were checked against api.crossref.org on 2026-10-02.
- Statements about how a source bears on metrics (i)–(vii) are my assessments and are placed under "Inferences", not "Cited Findings".

## 1. Variance / distribution screens: what are the standard screens, thresholds and reported accuracy?

### Takeaway
The standard bid-distribution screens are a small, well-defined family (coefficient of variation, spread, kurtosis; percentage difference, relative/normalised distance, skewness; a KS-uniformity statistic) computed per tender from bids alone; collusion with a designated winner plus cover bids is expected to lower CV and raise the gap between the two lowest bids relative to the spacing of losing bids. Thresholds are case-calibrated rather than theoretical (COMCO: CV ≤ ~0.06 and RD > 1 as "conspicuous"), and reported out-of-sample accuracy is 84–95% when trained and tested on one known cartel but 70–84% across 73 cartels in 7 countries, with documented false negatives when a cartel allocates on cost and mimics competition.

### Cited Findings

#### Abrantes-Metz, Froeb, Geweke & Taylor (2006), "A variance screen for collusion", IJIO 24(3):467–486, doi:10.1016/j.ijindorg.2005.10.003 (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: FTC Bureau of Economics WP 275, March 2005]
- Screen: coefficient of variation of price over time, CV = standard deviation / mean, computed per seller; look for geographic clusters of sellers with unusually low CV and high mean — [FTC WP 275](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/variance-screen-collusion/wp275_0.pdf)
- Motivating evidence: around the collapse of a frozen-perch bid-rigging conspiracy (sales to the US Defense Personnel Support Center), mean price fell 16.2% (3.544 → 2.970 $/lb), the standard deviation rose 263% (0.078 → 0.283) and CV rose 332% (0.022 → 0.095); the cost CV rose only 41.8% (0.158 → 0.224) — [FTC WP 275](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/variance-screen-collusion/wp275_0.pdf)
- Application: daily prices at 263 Louisville retail gasoline stations, 1996–2002, with MCMC/Gibbs imputation of missing observations; no suspicious low-variance clusters were found — [FTC WP 275](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/variance-screen-collusion/wp275_0.pdf)
- Stated theoretical basis: a variance screen is justified "if it is costly to coordinate price changes or if the cartel must solve an agency problem as in Athey, Bagwell, and Sanchirico (2004)" — [FTC WP 275](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/variance-screen-collusion/wp275_0.pdf)
- Data needed: a price (or winning-bid) time series per seller plus, ideally, a cost series to rule out cost-driven variance changes — [FTC WP 275](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/variance-screen-collusion/wp275_0.pdf)
- Known ambiguity of the variance marker: Bolotova, Connor & Miller (2008, IJIO 26(6):1290–1307, doi:10.1016/j.ijindorg.2007.12.008) found price variance lower during the lysine conspiracy but higher during the citric acid conspiracy [ABS] — [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:indorg:v:26:y:2008:i:6:p:1290-1307)

#### Imhof, Karagök & Rutz (2018), "Screening for bid rigging — does it work?", J. Competition Law & Economics 14(2):235–261, doi:10.1093/joclec/nhy006 (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: CRESE WP 2017-09]
- Screens: (a) per-tender CV of bids; (b) relative distance RD = (b2 − b1) / (standard deviation of the losing bids), the "cover-bidding screen"; (c) a bid-rotation / firm-interaction analysis on the subset of conspicuous tenders; (d) geographic analysis — [CRESE WP 2017-09](https://crese.univ-fcomte.fr/uploads/wp/WP-2017-09.pdf)
- Thresholds: "A RD larger than 1 points to a conspicuous contract"; for CV "there is no theoretical argument for a specific level", so benchmarks are taken from past cases — Ticino road-construction cartel: CV 0.03 on average during the cartel, "almost no rigged tenders with CV values higher than 0.05", rising to 0.098 after breakdown; the looser Aargau cartel (partial collusion, 17 firms, ~100 rigged contracts): average CV 0.06. Working rule: "tenders with a CV above 0.06 and a RD below 1 are inconspicuous and vice versa" — [CRESE WP 2017-09](https://crese.univ-fcomte.fr/uploads/wp/WP-2017-09.pdf)
- Data: official bid-opening records (bids and bidder identities only) for 282 road-construction tenders, 2004–2010, 138 firms, ~1,500 bids, CHF 216 million; no prior information about collusion — [CRESE WP 2017-09](https://crese.univ-fcomte.fr/uploads/wp/WP-2017-09.pdf)
- Sensitivity to procedure: mean CV was 0.081 in open procedures vs 0.058 in invitation procedures, i.e. the raw CV differs across auction formats absent any collusion finding; CV and RD were not significantly correlated (Spearman −0.15 and −0.16) — [CRESE WP 2017-09](https://crese.univ-fcomte.fr/uploads/wp/WP-2017-09.pdf)
- Result: the initial screen flagged 80 conspicuous contracts; COMCO opened an investigation in 2013 and sanctioned firms in 2016 (the WP says "eight firms were sanctioned"; Harrington & Imhof 2022 say the screen delivered evidence "for opening an investigation against six firms") — [CRESE WP 2017-09](https://crese.univ-fcomte.fr/uploads/wp/WP-2017-09.pdf); [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)
- Stated limits: "Irregular and selective bid-rigging agreements between firms loosely connected ... are, however, extremely hard – if not impossible – to identify with a screen"; the method needs "regular interaction between stable groups" — [CRESE WP 2017-09](https://crese.univ-fcomte.fr/uploads/wp/WP-2017-09.pdf)

#### Wallimann, Imhof & Huber (2023), "A machine learning approach for flagging incomplete bid-rigging cartels", Computational Economics 62(4):1669–1720, doi:10.1007/s10614-022-10315-w (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: arXiv:2004.05629v1] — source of exact screen formulas
- For tender t with n_t bids, mean b̄_t, standard deviation s_t, ordered bids b_1t ≤ b_2t ≤ …: — [arXiv:2004.05629](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05629)
  - CV_t = s_t / b̄_t
  - KURTO_t = [n_t(n_t+1) / ((n_t−1)(n_t−2)(n_t−3))] Σ_i ((b_it − b̄_t)/s_t)^4 − 3(n_t−1)^3 / ((n_t−2)(n_t−3)) — as printed in the arXiv text (the last term's exponent is printed as 3; the standard sample excess-kurtosis formula has (n_t−1)^2, so check before use); computed only for tenders with ≥ 4 bids
  - SPD_t = (b_max,t − b_min,t) / b_min,t
  - DIFFP_t = (b_2t − b_1t) / b_1t; absolute difference D_t = b_2t − b_1t
  - RD_t = (b_2t − b_1t) / s_losing bids,t
  - RDNOR_t = (b_2t − b_1t) / [ Σ_{i=1}^{n_t−1} (b_{i+1,t} − b_it) / (n_t − 1) ] (difference of two lowest bids over the mean difference between all adjacent bids)
  - RDALT_t = (b_2t − b_1t) / [ Σ_{i=2}^{n_t−1} (b_{i+1,t} − b_it) / (n_t − 2) ] (denominator uses adjacent losing bids only)
  - SKEW_t = [n_t / ((n_t−1)(n_t−2))] Σ_i ((b_it − b̄_t)/s_t)^3
  - KS_t = max(D+_t, D−_t), with D+_t = max_i (x_it − i_t/(n_t+1)), D−_t = max_i (i_t/(n_t+1) − x_it), x_it = bid divided by the tender's bid standard deviation (tests closeness to a uniform distribution)
- Three classes: variance screens (CV, KURTO, SPD), asymmetry / "cover-bidding" screens (DIFFP, SKEW, RD, RDNOR, RDALT), uniformity (KS) — [arXiv:2004.05629](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05629)
- Robustification for incomplete cartels: compute each screen on every subgroup of 3 or 4 bids within a tender and use the mean, median, min and max across subgroups as predictors — [arXiv:2004.05629](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05629)
- Reported gain: with five competitive bids added to collusive tenders, correct classification rises from 76% to 86%; on two Swiss incomplete-cartel investigations the gain is 3 to 7.5 percentage points [2nd, as summarised by Harrington & Imhof] — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)

#### Huber & Imhof (2019), "Machine learning with screens for detecting bid-rigging cartels", IJIO 65:277–301, doi:10.1016/j.ijindorg.2019.04.002 (Crossref-verified) [2nd: via Harrington & Imhof 2022]
- Lasso and an ensemble method applied to 584 Swiss tenders "correctly classify 84% of the tenders, as either collusive or competitive"; the two most informative markers are the normalised distance and the coefficient of variation — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)
- Direction of effects: "bid manipulation among cartel participants increases the distance between the first and second lowest bids in a tender and reduces the distance between losing bids" — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)
- Caveat stated by the survey: the data involve cartels that encompass all bidders, which makes the pattern easier to recognise — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)

#### Imhof & Wallimann (2021), "Detecting bid-rigging coalitions in different countries and auction formats", Int. Review of Law and Economics 68:106016, doi:10.1016/j.irle.2021.106016 (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: arXiv:2105.00337]
- Coalition-based screens: for every triple of firms that bid together in ≥ 3 tenders, compute the nine tender-level screens on the three bids and summarise by mean/median/min/max (36 features) — [arXiv:2105.00337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.00337)
- Evaluation protocol: balanced 50/50 samples, 75/25 train/test split, repeated 100 times, average correct classification rate — [arXiv:2105.00337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.00337)
- Okinawa cartel (207 collusive, 1,793 competitive coalitions): correct classification 92.3% (lasso), 94.9% (random forest), 93.9% (super learner), 91.9% (SVM); most important predictors are the median CV, the spread and the KS statistic — [arXiv:2105.00337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.00337)
- Asymmetry screens perform poorly on small coalitions because "the bid of the designated winner and thus the distance between the winning bid and the second lowest bid from the cartel is not systematically considered" — [arXiv:2105.00337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.00337)
- Levels of the screen are not portable: mean of coalition-median CV is 3.38 (collusive) vs 6.80 (competitive) in Switzerland, 10.13 vs 30.73 in Italy, 1.06 vs 3.19 in Japan — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)

#### Imhof (2019), "Detecting bid-rigging cartels with descriptive statistics", JCLE 15(4):427–467, doi:10.1093/joclec/nhz019 (Crossref-verified) [ABS]
- The (Ticino) cartel lowered bid variance as shown by the CV and kurtosis; cover bidding produced asymmetry shown by the relative distance, skewness and percentage difference between the two lowest bids — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcle/article-abstract/15/4/427/5730310)

#### Huber, Imhof & Ishii (2022), "Transnational machine learning with screens for flagging bid-rigging cartels", JRSS-A 185(3):1074–1114, doi:10.1111/rssa.12811 (Crossref-verified) [2nd]
- In the Okinawa application the error rate for models using screens only is about eleven percentage points, as reported by Imhof & Wallimann — [arXiv:2105.00337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.00337)

#### Harrington & Imhof (2022), "Cartel screening and machine learning", Stanford Computational Antitrust, Vol. II, starting p. 133 [FT]
- Taxonomy: collusive markers ("a pattern in the data more consistent with collusion than competition"), structural breaks, and anomalies ("a pattern in the data that is inexplicable or inconsistent with competition") — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)
- Worked example of the cover-bid pattern: in one rigged tender the difference between first and second bids (36,800) far exceeds the differences between adjacent losing bids (3,561 to 6,141), giving RD = 4.97 and normalised distance 3.33 — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)
- Decision guidance: more than 50% of tenders classified collusive in a region/group "would call for further examination"; 20–50% "tightened scrutiny" — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)
- Structural-break example with a reserve: in 139 Japanese paving tenders (Ishii 2008 WP) the winning bid was 91–95% of the maximum allowed bid in 123 tenders and equal to the minimum bid in the other 16 — periodic switching between collusion and competition — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)

#### Harrington (2008), "Detecting cartels", in P. Buccirossi (ed.), Handbook of Antitrust Economics, MIT Press [FT-WP; page range not verified]
- Four empirical questions: (A) is behaviour inconsistent with competition? (B) is there a structural break? (C) does the behaviour of suspected colluders differ from that of competitive firms? (D) does a collusive model fit better than a competitive one? "Methods A and B are generally first-stage methods in that they do not provide evidence of collusion but rather evidence that doesn't sit well with a competitive model" — [Harrington WP](https://www.competitionpolicyinternational.com/assets/Uploads/Detecting-Cartels.pdf)
- Warning on levels vs changes: "Screening for abrupt changes in price (or price-cost margins) is then likely to be a more effective screen - in picking up fewer false positives - than looking for high price-cost margins" — [Harrington WP](https://www.competitionpolicyinternational.com/assets/Uploads/Detecting-Cartels.pdf)
- Disclaimer on markers: "evidence supporting collusion need not imply evidence against competition"; "At best, collusive markers can serve to screen industries" — [Harrington WP](https://www.competitionpolicyinternational.com/assets/Uploads/Detecting-Cartels.pdf)

#### Fazekas, Tóth, Wachs & Abdou (2026), "Public procurement cartels: A large-sample testing of screens using machine learning", IJIO 104:103228, doi:10.1016/j.ijindorg.2025.103228 [FT, open access]
- 73 proven cartels from 7 European countries, 2004–2021; a random forest over many price and bidding-pattern indicators "achieves 70–84 % prediction accuracy ... (accuracy is 97 % when trained and tested on a single cartel case, typical of the literature)" — [Fazekas et al. 2026](https://www.govtransparency.eu/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Public_procurement_cartels_A-large-sample-testing-of-screens-using-machine-learning.pdf)
- External-validity problem: "different cartel strategies give opposing signals" — [Fazekas et al. 2026](https://www.govtransparency.eu/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Public_procurement_cartels_A-large-sample-testing-of-screens-using-machine-learning.pdf)
- "Relative price" indicator = final contract price / initial estimate (reference price), used as a competition proxy; affected by many non-collusive factors — [Fazekas et al. 2026](https://www.govtransparency.eu/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Public_procurement_cartels_A-large-sample-testing-of-screens-using-machine-learning.pdf)

#### Imhof, Madiès & Huber (2026), "Swimming in dark water: when cartels mimic competition", arXiv:2606.30470 [FT, preprint]
- The Ticino road-construction cartel (1999–2005), a weak cartel without side payments, "implemented a cost-based allocation mechanism that closely approximated the first-best collusive outcome"; allocation criteria in its written "convention" were backlog, distance, specialisation and discussion; members had to prepare cover bids — [arXiv:2606.30470](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30470)
- Because bids were aligned with cost structure, Bajari–Ye conditional-independence/exchangeability tests "generated a high rate of false negatives ... the cartel was able to pass as competitive while being fully collusive"; estimated overcharge at least 45% — [arXiv:2606.30470](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30470)

- OECD (2022), "Data screening tools for competition investigations" [FT]: screens are classified as structural vs behavioural; screens "carry an inherent risk of false positives or false negatives: they provide only economic evidence that is often ambiguous"; false positives arise from omitted variables — [OECD 2022](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/10/data-screening-tools-for-competition-investigations_03cc9a27/4c5bbb9d-en.pdf)

### Inferences
- Bearing on paper metrics. The screen literature **supports (v)** in spirit but **suggests reformulating it**: the validated cover-bid statistics are ratios that separate two things — the gap between the lowest and second-lowest bid, and the spacing among losing bids (RD, RDNOR, RDALT, DIFFP) — plus dispersion of all bids (CV, SPD). A single "gap between losing bids and the winning bid" conflates a large winner–runner-up gap (the collusive signature in this literature) with tight clustering of losers. Reporting DIFFP, RD/RDNOR and the CV of losing bids per round would let the paper cite Imhof et al. (2018) and Wallimann et al. (2023) directly.
- With few bidders some screens are undefined or degenerate: KURTO needs ≥ 4 bids and RD needs ≥ 3 bids (two losing bids give a two-point standard deviation). DIFFP and SPD work for any n ≥ 2.
- Thresholds (CV ≈ 0.03–0.06, RD > 1) are calibrated on Swiss road construction and should not be imported as cut-offs for a simulation: the same authors show screen levels differ by a factor of ~10 across countries and by procedure. In a simulation the correct reference is the screen's distribution under the known Bayes-Nash bid function applied to the realised costs (and under the one-shot control), not a field threshold.
- **(i)** corresponds to the field "relative price" marker (winning bid relative to reserve / engineer's estimate) but is better identified because the competitive benchmark is computed rather than assumed. Harrington's caution that levels generate more false positives than changes argues for leading with the difference-based metric **(ii)**.
- **(iii)** is *not* a clean competitive marker: the Ticino evidence shows a real cartel allocating contracts by cost. High allocative efficiency together with a high index (i) is exactly the "cartel mimicking competition" pattern, so (iii) should be interpreted jointly with (i), not as evidence against collusion on its own.
- The 97% vs 70–84% contrast (Fazekas et al.) is a warning against claiming that any single screen "detects" collusion; the paper's battery of mutually reinforcing metrics mirrors the COMCO approach.

## 2. Structural / regression-discontinuity approaches: how do rotation- and incumbency-based tests work, and what do they imply when costs are known to the analyst?

### Takeaway
The structural literature tests necessary conditions of competitive bidding — bids must respond to costs (Porter–Zona), be conditionally independent and exchangeable given costs (Bajari–Ye), leave no profitable small deviation (Chassang et al.), and make winning "as-if random" among nearly tied bidders (Kawai et al.) — precisely because field analysts cannot observe costs. In a simulation with known costs each of these becomes a direct, more powerful test, and Kawai & Nakabayashi's rebid analysis is the closest published precedent for a rebid-based metric.

### Cited Findings

#### Porter & Zona (1993), "Detection of bid rigging in procurement auctions", JPE 101(3):518–538, doi:10.1086/261885 (Crossref-verified) [ABS of NBER WP 4013; mechanism also described in Kawai et al.]
- Long Island highway paving, early 1980s: ring meetings designated "a serious bidder, and its bid, and the other firms then frequently submitted phony higher bids"; bids and rank of non-cartel firms were related to cost measures such as backlog, whereas "the rank distribution of higher cartel bids was unrelated to similar cost measures, and differed from the distribution of the low cartel bid" — [NBER WP 4013](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4013)
- Detection is possible "because of limited participation in the collusive scheme" (a competitive comparison group exists) — [NBER WP 4013](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4013)
- Characterisation by Kawai et al.: Porter & Zona "focus on the lack of incentives among losing cartel bidders to bid in ways that reflect their true costs. Hence, their primary focus is on losing bidders" — [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)

#### Porter & Zona (1999), "Ohio school milk markets: an analysis of bidding", RAND J. Econ. 30(2):263–288, doi:10.2307/2556080 (Crossref-verified) [ABS/2nd]
- Compares a group of Cincinnati dairies with a control group; behaviour is consistent with collusion; estimated average price effect about 6.5% — [EconPapers](http://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:rje:randje:v:30:y:1999:i:summer:p:263-288)
- Anomaly used: some suppliers "submitted lower bids on school milk tenders for districts that were farther away from their plants (and thus had higher transportation costs)" — collusion near home, competition farther away — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf)
- The cartel allocated by incumbency: firms agreed "not to undercut the bid of the incumbent firm that had served a given school district in the previous year" — [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)

#### Bajari & Ye (2003), "Deciding between competition and collusion", REStat 85(4):971–989, doi:10.1162/003465303772815871 (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: July 2002 version]
- Competitive bidding with asymmetric bidders implies (A1) conditional independence — after controlling for publicly observed cost information, bids are independent — and (A3) exchangeability — permuting bidders' cost characteristics permutes their bid distributions (costs alone, not identities, determine bids) — [2002 WP](https://users.nber.org/~confer/2002/si2002/bajari.pdf)
- Implementation: reduced-form bid regressions on cost shifters with auction fixed effects; test H0: ρ_ij = 0 on residual correlations for each pair of firms (Fisher test); test exchangeability by an F-test of equal coefficients across firms — [2002 WP](https://users.nber.org/~confer/2002/si2002/bajari.pdf)
- Data/results: 138 Midwest seal-coat auctions; of 23 pairs with ≥ 4 simultaneous bids, conditional independence was rejected at 5% for four pairs; exchangeability was rejected for the 11 main firms pooled and for one pair — [2002 WP](https://users.nber.org/~confer/2002/si2002/bajari.pdf)
- Stated limitations: correct functional form is required; omitted cost variables can produce false rejections; and "if a sophisticated cartel is operating in this market ... the cartel could satisfy assumptions A1-A5 by generating phony bids in a clever fashion" — [2002 WP](https://users.nber.org/~confer/2002/si2002/bajari.pdf)
- Field performance: Imhof (2017) found the tests "produce too many false negatives" on the Ticino cartel, whereas screens performed well [2nd] — [arXiv:2105.00337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.00337)

#### Chassang, Kawai, Nakabayashi & Ortner (2022), "Robust screens for noncompetitive bidding in procurement auctions", Econometrica 90(1):315–346, doi:10.3982/ECTA17155 (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: NBER WP 25654, "Data driven regulation: theory and application to missing bids", 2019]
- Statistic: for every (bidder, auction) pair, Δ = (own bid − lowest bid among the bidder's opponents) / reserve price; Δ < 0 means the bidder won — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)
- Finding: in ~78,000 Japanese national auctions (2001–2006) and ~1,500 city auctions (2007–2014) "winning bids tend to be isolated. There is a missing mass of close losing bids" around Δ = 0 — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)
- Theory: with counterfactual demand D_i(b|h) = Prob(lowest opposing bid > b | h), any competitive equilibrium requires ∂log D_i(b|h)/∂log b ≤ −1 at the bid placed (firms must not gain from raising bids). With missing bids the elasticity is ≈ 0, so the pattern "is inconsistent with competitive behavior under arbitrary information structures" — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)
- "Safe tests" = tests "passed with probability one by competitive bidders, but need not be passed by non-competitive ones"; confidence sets for demand are obtained by bootstrap in the empirical analysis — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)
- Conditioning sets must be "adapted" to bidders' information (e.g., an industry or location), not selected on outcomes such as the margin of victory — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)
- Validation: histories with bids close to the reserve price fail the tests more often than histories with low bids; histories before a collusion investigation fail more often than histories after — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)
- Ties: the model assumes "Ties are broken randomly"; in city-level data "there is a surprisingly large mass of auctions such that the lowest and second lowest bids are tied or extremely close", so a small downward deviation (ρ = −0.0005) "increases a bidders likelihood of winning by a non-vanishing amount, while reducing profits by a negligible amount, provided margins are not zero. The corresponding histories cannot be competitive" — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)
- Scope restriction: this paper "rule[s] out intertemporal linkages between actions and payoffs" (e.g., backlog-dependent costs) — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)

#### Kawai, Nakabayashi, Ortner & Chassang (2023), "Using bid rotation and incumbency to detect collusion: a regression discontinuity approach", REStud 90(1):376–403, doi:10.1093/restud/rdac013 (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: NBER WP 29625]
- Problem: "Bid rotation can arise under competition if marginal costs increase with backlog. Incumbency advantage can be explained by cost asymmetries ... or by learning-by-doing", so unconditional rotation/incumbency patterns "would yield false positives" — [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)
- Running variable: Δ_i,t = b_i,t − (lowest competing bid), with bids normalised by the reserve price; Δ < 0 wins. Test statistic: β = lim_{ε↓0} E[x_i,t | Δ_i,t = ε] − lim_{ε↑0} E[x_i,t | Δ_i,t = ε], H0: β = 0, where x is a pre-determined covariate — [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)
- Covariates: (a) backlog — raw k-day backlog x^Bk_i,t = Σ_{τ in the k days before t} r_τ · 1{i won τ} for k ∈ {90, 180} (r = reserve price), and a within-firm standardised version (subtract the firm mean, divide by the firm standard deviation); (b) incumbency = bidder won the same buyer's auction in the previous year. Expected sign: β > 0 for backlog under rotation; β < 0 for incumbency under incumbent priority — [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)
- Why it identifies non-competition: if residual demand D_i(b|h) is strictly decreasing and continuously differentiable, Prob(i wins | h, |b_i − lowest competing bid| ≤ ε) → 1/2 for every history h (Lemma 1), so "Winning and losing are 'as-if-random' conditional on close bids" and covariates must be balanced across marginal winners and losers (Corollary 1), even if they are correlated with costs — [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)
- Estimation: local linear regression, triangular kernel, coverage-error-rate-optimal bandwidth with bias correction (Calonico, Cattaneo & Titiunik 2014), pooling bidders and auctions; standard errors clustered by school district (Ohio) or by auction (Japan) — [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)
- Validation on a known cartel (Ohio school milk, 1980–1990, 3,754 auctions): raw incumbent win rates are similar in collusive (83–86%) and control (81%) auctions, but the RD estimate of the incumbency gap is −0.312 (p = 0.077; −0.379 excluding breakdown years, significant at 5%; bandwidth 0.004) among auctions with only defendant bidders, versus −0.031 (not significant) in control auctions with > 10 times the sample — [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)
- Japanese municipal auctions (Tohoku): marginal losers have 0.136 SD higher 90-day standardised backlog (SE 0.038; bandwidth 0.020; N = 59,367) and are 18.4 percentage points less likely to be incumbents (SE 0.078; N = 2,517); effects are concentrated in bids above the municipality's median winning bid — [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)
- Power: the test "has power only against cartels that allocate projects based on a rotation scheme" (stated in the companion application) and cartels can adapt, e.g. by making the designated winner bid well below the runner-up — but that "would generate isolated winning bids similar to the pattern documented in Chassang et al." — [NBER WP 30271](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30271); [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)
- Application to five jurisdictions (Kawai, Nakabayashi & Shimamoto 2022, NBER WP 30271) [FT]: discontinuity in backlog found for Indonesia — non-competitive share "at least about 5% for all E-procurement auctions and about 3% for all auctions" — and no rejection for Georgia, Mongolia, Malta or California — [NBER WP 30271](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30271)

#### Kawai & Nakabayashi (2022), "Detecting large-scale collusion in procurement auctions", JPE 130(5):1364–1411, doi:10.1086/718913 (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: authors' version dated 1 June 2021]
- Setting: ~42,000 Japanese national construction auctions (April 2003–December 2006, ~$40 billion) with a secret reserve price; if no bid meets the reserve there is an immediate reauction (rebid) — [authors' version](http://www.keikawai.com/detection_revised.pdf)
- Idea: preallocation "leads to persistence in the identity of the lowest bidder across the initial auction and the reauctions"; the designated losers "avoid undercutting the bid of the designated winner in the initial auction as well as in the reauctions" — [authors' version](http://www.keikawai.com/detection_revised.pdf)
- Pattern 1: the second-lowest first-round bidder i(2) "loses to i(1) in the reauction more than 95% of the time"; "there are hundreds of auctions in which i(2) is outbid by i(1) in the reauction by a tiny margin, but almost none in which i(1) is outbid by i(2)". Using later rebids to bound costs, bidders who lost the initial auction by < 1% of the reserve could have raised profits by > 1.7 million yen by cutting their rebid 2% — [authors' version](http://www.keikawai.com/detection_revised.pdf)
- Pattern 2: the distribution of the round-2 bid difference between i(1) and i(2) has a kink at zero; under a benchmark with idiosyncratic bid shocks the probability that i(2) outbids i(1) "must converge to 0.5" as the difference approaches zero — [authors' version](http://www.keikawai.com/detection_revised.pdf)
- Pattern 3: bidders that marginally outbid i(1) in the rebid come from past auctions with significantly lower winning bids than bidders marginally outbid by i(1) — [authors' version](http://www.keikawai.com/detection_revised.pdf)
- Results: competition rejected for 1,066 firms (613 of the 1,000 largest), which won 15,583 auctions = 37.1% of the sample; validated on four cartels prosecuted by the JFTC (online appendix) — [authors' version](http://www.keikawai.com/detection_revised.pdf)
- Two competitive benchmarks are used: one-shot expected-profit maximisation with rational expectations, and a model with small idiosyncratic bid shocks independent of rivals' bids ("Absence of any such idiosyncratic errors suggests coordinated bidding") — [authors' version](http://www.keikawai.com/detection_revised.pdf)
- Link to missing bids: if the designated winner names "a round number above which designated losers should bid", the result is a small missing mass of almost-tied bids — [authors' version](http://www.keikawai.com/detection_revised.pdf)

#### Conley & Decarolis (2016), "Detecting bidders groups in collusive auctions", AEJ: Microeconomics 8(2):1–38, doi:10.1257/mic.20130254 (Crossref-verified) [ABS]
- Setting is average-bid auctions ("contracts are awarded to the bid closest to a trimmed average bid"), which "create incentives to coordinate bids to manipulate the bid distribution"; tests detect coordinated entry and bidding and "perform well in a validation dataset where a court case makes coordination observable" — [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mic.20130254)

#### Ishii (2009), "Favor exchange in collusion: empirical study of repeated procurement auctions in Japan", IJIO 27(2):137–144, doi:10.1016/j.ijindorg.2008.05.006 (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: Osaka University DP 07-15, "Detecting collusion through exchange of favors in repeated procurement auctions"]
- Data: 175 consulting-works auctions in Naha, Okinawa; 1,748 bids by 136 firms; winning price within 5% of the reserve in 72% of auctions; in 11 of 175 the winning price was below 80% of the reserve — [Osaka DP 07-15](https://ideas.repec.org/p/osk/wpaper/0715.html)
- Metric: a pairwise "score" x_ijt = net favours firm i has given firm j to date; when i wins an auction in which j also bid, x_ij falls by the favour value v_t and x_ji rises by v_t; scores start at zero. Test: winning probability is positively related to the bidder's score against the other participants — a formal rotation / "balance sheet" test — [Osaka DP 07-15](https://ideas.repec.org/p/osk/wpaper/0715.html)
- Institutional evidence: ring members in Okinawa and Takarazuka kept a "balance sheet" of auction dates, winning prices and winners — [Osaka DP 07-15](https://ideas.repec.org/p/osk/wpaper/0715.html)

#### Related theory on rotation and efficiency (abstract-level)
- McAfee & McMillan (1992), "Bidding rings", AER 82(3):579–599 [FT]: without side payments, "incentive compatibility requires that the good be awarded stochastically, with equal probability of being awarded to anyone whose value is larger than the minimum price: any attempt to arrange that the highest-value bidder wins generates incentives for the bidders to misstate their valuations" — [McAfee & McMillan 1992](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)
- Skrzypacz & Hopenhayn (2004), "Tacit collusion in repeated auctions", JET 114(1):153–169, doi:10.1016/S0022-0531(03)00128-5 [ABS]: with IPV and limited public monitoring and no communication, "collusion better than bid rotation is still feasible, but full surplus cannot be extracted" — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jetheo/v114y2004i1p153-169.html)
- Pesendorfer (2000), "A study of collusion in first-price auctions", REStud 67(3):381–411, doi:10.1111/1467-937X.00136 [ABS]: school-milk cartels in Florida (side payments) and Texas (market division, no side payments); both arrangements are "almost optimal, provided the number of contracts is sufficiently large" — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:restud:v:67:y:2000:i:3:p:381-411.)
- Ortner, Chassang, Kawai & Nakabayashi, "Screening adaptive cartels", NBER WP 30219 [ABS]: asymptotically safe tests "do not create new collusive equilibria, and do not hurt competitive industries" — [NBER WP 30219](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30219)

### Inferences
- What known costs change. Every structural test above is a workaround for unobserved costs. When the analyst knows each bidder's cost each round: (a) the Porter–Zona test becomes a direct regression of each bid on own cost — under the symmetric risk-neutral BNE with uniform costs the slope is (n−1)/n and the intercept c_max/n; "phony"/cover bids show up as losing bids with weak cost pass-through; (b) Bajari–Ye conditional independence becomes a test that residuals b_it − b_BNE(c_it) are uncorrelated across bidders within a round (and exchangeability is a test that the fitted bid function does not depend on bidder identity); (c) the Chassang et al. inequality can be evaluated exactly, since the profit from a small upward or downward deviation can be computed from realised rival bids and the true cost; (d) the Kawai et al. RD can be replaced by a sharper design that conditions on *costs* being close (or on the cost rank) rather than on bids being close.
- **(iii)** is the simulation analogue of these tests' core idea (allocation should track cost). It **is supported** by McAfee–McMillan (a weak cartel without side payments cannot allocate efficiently, so efficiency below the competitive level is diagnostic) but **qualified** by Pesendorfer and by Imhof–Madiès–Huber (some real cartels achieve near-efficient allocation). Reading: low (iii) with high (i) points to rotation / random allocation; high (iii) with high (i) points to an efficient ring or to uniform overpricing by all bidders.
- **(iv)**: with i.i.d. private costs redrawn each round and a symmetric equilibrium, the winner is the lowest-cost bidder, so win counts are multinomial with equal probabilities and the probability that the previous winner wins again is 1/n. Rotation produces win counts that are *more even* than multinomial and a repeat-win rate *below* 1/n; incumbency priority produces a repeat-win rate above 1/n. An upper-tail chi-square against uniform therefore has no power against rotation; the paper should use a two-sided dispersion test (or report the variance ratio) and test the repeat-win rate against 1/n in both directions. A closer analogue of Kawai et al. / Ishii is a regression of the win indicator on lagged wins (backlog) *controlling for own cost and cost rank* — under competition the lagged-win coefficient is zero because costs are i.i.d. This avoids the false-positive problem that motivated the RD design.
- The Kawai et al. RD cannot be applied literally to a setting with exact ties and a discrete grid: its Lemma 1 needs smooth residual demand, and the tie-break rule (least-wins-first) mechanically creates a backlog discontinuity among tied bidders. So a backlog difference between marginal winners and losers under the least-wins-first treatment is a property of the mechanism, not evidence of collusion; it is only interpretable under the random tie-break.
- **(vii)**: Kawai & Nakabayashi **support** using rebids as evidence, but their statistics are about *rank persistence* and *who undercuts whom* in the rebid, not about the price change. They suggest adding to (vii): the probability that the rebid is again tied; the probability that each tied bidder cuts; the size of the cut relative to the remaining margin (rebid − cost); and whether the deviation "cut by one grid step" was available and unprofitable or simply not taken. With known costs the "could have profited by cutting x%" calculation they do with cost bounds can be done exactly.
- **(v)**: the Chassang et al. Δ statistic (own bid minus lowest rival bid, over reserve) pooled over bidder-rounds is a ready-made, citable version of (v); a hole in its density just above zero is the collusive signature. In the paper's setting it should be compared with the density implied by the BNE applied to realised costs.

### Gaps
- Published Econometrica and REStud versions were not read; the theoretical statements quoted are from the NBER working papers and assumptions may have been tightened in publication.
- I did not find a published test that uses rotation or incumbency in an environment with exact ties and a deterministic tie-break rule; the implication drawn above for the least-wins-first treatment is my own.
- Porter & Zona (1993, 1999) full texts were not read (the NBER scan of the 1999 paper did not extract); the rank-based multinomial-logit specification is not reproduced here for that reason.
- Conley & Decarolis's test statistics are specific to average-bid auctions and were not read in detail; relevance to a lowest-bid-wins auction is limited.

## 3. Identical or near-identical bids, and cover bids clustered just above the winning bid

### Takeaway
Identical bids are a classic red flag with a firm theoretical basis — a cartel without side payments optimally has every member bid the same price at the reserve and lets the buyer's tie-break allocate — but a weak empirical screen on its own (a US DOJ identical-bids unit found no conspiracy in six years). For cover bids the modern evidence is that designated-winner schemes produce *both* an isolated winning bid (a missing mass of near-ties) and clustering among the remaining bids, so "many ties" and "no near-ties" are signatures of two different collusive schemes.

### Cited Findings

#### McAfee & McMillan (1992), "Bidding rings", AER 82(3):579–599 [FT]
- Result: "The weak cartel can do no better than have its members submit identical bids" — with no transfers and H'(v) < 0 (H = (1−F)/f), optimal collusion has "every bidder who values the item at more than the minimum price submitting the same bid, namely, the minimum price" — [McAfee & McMillan 1992](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)
- Role of the tie-break: "By submitting equal bids, the bidders in effect use the seller as their randomizing device"; random tie-breaking, "employed in most government procurement auctions, is not a best response against the existence of a cartel" — [McAfee & McMillan 1992](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)
- No communication needed: "the optimal mechanism can be implemented without coordination" — bidders "simply bid r if and only if their valuations exceed r" — [McAfee & McMillan 1992](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)
- Empirical prevalence cited there: Scherer (1970): "Each year the federal and state governments receive thousands of sets of identical bids"; other documentation in Mund (1960), Cook (1963), Comanor & Schankerman (1976); one example of nine identical bids of $6,009.15 — [McAfee & McMillan 1992](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)
- The price r can be the announced reserve or "the cartel members' shared perception of how low a bid they can get away with" (the paper is written for a selling auction; in procurement the analogue is the maximum acceptable price) — [McAfee & McMillan 1992](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)

#### Mund (1960), "Identical bid prices", JPE 68(2):150–169, doi:10.1086/258292; Comanor & Schankerman (1976), "Identical bids and cartel behavior", Bell J. Econ. 7(1):281–286, doi:10.2307/3003203 (both Crossref-verified) [ABS/2nd — not read]
- Comanor & Schankerman: despite the accepted view that identical bids suggest a cartel, "identical bid arrangements are unlikely to be maintained among colluding firms where their number in an industry is small" (search-result abstract) — [Crossref record](https://doi.org/10.2307/3003203)

#### Abrantes-Metz et al. (2006) on the failure of an identical-bids screen [FT-WP]
- "In the 1970's ... the U.S. Department of Justice formed an 'identical bids' unit that investigated government procurement auctions in which identical bids were submitted. In six years, the unit failed to uncover a single conspiracy" (attributed in a footnote to Frederick Warren-Boulton) — [FTC WP 275](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/variance-screen-collusion/wp275_0.pdf)

#### Harrington (2008) — identical bids plus a deterministic tie-break as market allocation [FT-WP]
- Oklahoma Highway Department asphalt auctions 1954–65 (Funderburk 1974): during suspected collusion "bids were identical and, beginning in 1957, were constant at 10.25 cents/gallon. With identical bids, the OHD awarded the contract to the nearest firm to the job site ... which, it was argued, acted as a market allocation scheme"; the same suppliers bid about 6 cents/gallon in other states — [Harrington WP](https://www.competitionpolicyinternational.com/assets/Uploads/Detecting-Cartels.pdf)
- Structural-break use: the Department "only started receiving identical bids at procurement auctions some time after the Asphalt Refiners Association was formed" — [Harrington WP](https://www.competitionpolicyinternational.com/assets/Uploads/Detecting-Cartels.pdf)

#### Chassang et al. (2022) — ties and near-ties [FT-WP]
- A mass of exactly or approximately tied lowest bids is non-competitive when margins are positive because a tiny undercut raises the win probability discretely (see Section 2); conversely, the national data show a *missing* mass of near-ties — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)

#### Clark, Coviello & De Leverano (2025), "Complementary bidding and the collusive arrangement: evidence from an antitrust investigation", AEJ: Microeconomics 17(2):66–93, doi:10.1257/mic.20210450 [ABS for the published version; FT-WP: Queen's Economics Department WP 1446]
- "Clustered bids and a missing mass of nearly tied bids have both been proposed as markers of collusion. We present causal empirical evidence from an actual procurement cartel that bidding involves both clustering and a gap around the winning bid" — [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mic.20210450)
- Design: difference-in-differences comparing Montreal asphalt auctions before/after a police investigation with Quebec City — [Queen's WP 1446](https://www.econ.queensu.ca/research/working-papers/1446)
- Measures (WP): clustering = standard deviation of bid differences Δ_i,a (own bid minus lowest rival bid, $ per ton): Montreal 2.62 → 5.79 after the investigation vs Quebec City 3.05 → 3.78 (difference-in-differences 2.43). Isolation = Pr(Δ = 0) / Pr(0 < Δ ≤ median pre-period Montreal difference of $0.5): Montreal 0.11 → 1.80, Quebec City 0.00 → 0.33 — [Queen's WP 1446](https://www.econ.queensu.ca/research/working-papers/1446)
- Winning bid in Montreal fell by $8 per ton after the investigation while it rose $6 in Quebec City — [Queen's WP 1446](https://www.econ.queensu.ca/research/working-papers/1446)

#### Kawai, Nakabayashi & Ortner (2021), "The value of privacy in cartels: an analysis of the inner workings of a bidding ring", NBER WP 28539 [ABS]
- "the designated winner of the cartel can attain higher payoffs by randomizing its bid and keeping it secret from other bidders when defection is a concern" — a reason for cover bids to sit a noticeable distance above the winning bid — [NBER WP 28539](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28539)

#### Experimental and simulation evidence on tie rules
- Sherstyuk (2002), as summarised by Kagel & Levin: in ascending auctions with repeated play and no communication, under a weak bid-improvement rule (matching a bid suffices, "tied bids settled randomly") prices converge toward the reserve and "Collusion largely results from bid matching"; under a strict improvement rule "collusion tends to take the form of a bid rotation rule" [2nd] — [Kagel & Levin draft](https://www.asc.ohio-state.edu/kagel.4/HEE-Vol2/Auction%20survey_all_1_31_15.pdf)
- Banchio & Skrzypacz (2022): Q-learning bidders in repeated first-price auctions collude because of "the incentive in first-price auctions to outbid opponents by just one bid increment. This facilitates re-coordination on low bids after a phase of experimentation" (bid grid step 0.05) — [arXiv:2202.05947](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05947)
- Fish, Gonczarowski & Shorrer (v6, 2026), auction appendix: ties are broken uniformly at random; with rounding to cents "there are additional equilibria involving bids of v and v − 1¢" — [arXiv:2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Athey, Bagwell & Sanchirico (2004), "Collusion and price rigidity", REStud 71(2):317–349, doi:10.1111/0034-6527.00286 [ABS]: with private i.i.d. cost shocks, "the optimal symmetric collusive scheme requires the absence of equilibrium-path price wars and a rigid price" for a wide range of settings (pooling on one price sacrifices allocative efficiency) — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v71y2004i2p317-349.html)
- Chassang & Ortner (2019), "Collusion in auctions with constrained bids", JPE 127(5):2269–2300, doi:10.1086/701812 [ABS]: "introducing minimum prices can lower the winning-bid distribution" under collusion because bid constraints limit punishments — [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/collusion-in-auctions-with-constrained-bids-theory-and-evidence-f)

### Inferences
- **(vi) is well supported** theoretically: McAfee–McMillan predict identical bids *at the reserve* with random allocation as the optimal no-transfer scheme, implementable without communication; Athey–Bagwell–Sanchirico predict a rigid common price; Chassang et al. show ties at positive margins violate competitive best response. The paper's choice to report the *price level* of tied bids is therefore the right companion statistic: ties at or near the reserve are the McAfee–McMillan scheme, ties near cost are Bertrand-like focal bidding. I would add the tied bidders' margin (tie price minus own cost) and the forgone gain from undercutting by one grid step, which is the Chassang et al. deviation computed exactly.
- **(vi) is contradicted as a stand-alone screen** by the DOJ identical-bids experience and by Comanor & Schankerman. The defensible claim is comparative: tie rate relative to the one-shot control and relative to the tie rate implied by applying the BNE (rounded to the grid) to the realised costs — both of which the simulation can compute.
- The tie-break manipulation maps onto documented cases: random tie-break = McAfee–McMillan's "seller as randomizing device"; a deterministic rule (least-wins-first) = an allocation device operated by the buyer, like Oklahoma's nearest-firm rule, which converts identical bidding into rotation at no coordination cost. This gives the paper a field precedent for the hypothesis that a deterministic, "fair" tie-break makes identical bidding more attractive. Under that treatment, (iv) will show rotation mechanically whenever ties are frequent, so (iv) should be reported separately for tied and non-tied rounds.
- **(v)**: Clark et al. imply two numbers, not one — dispersion of bid differences (clustering) and the mass at/near zero (isolation). In a tacit, communication-free setting there is no designated winner announcing a bid, so the "missing bids" signature may be absent even if prices are supra-competitive; absence of a gap is not evidence of competition.
- Early-vs-late comparison in (vi) corresponds to the structural-break approach (identical bids appearing after a coordination device arises) and is supported by Harrington's Oklahoma example.

### Gaps
- Mund (1960) and Comanor & Schankerman (1976) were not read; only bibliographic details were verified.
- I found no systematic estimate of the false-positive rate of identical-bid screens in modern e-procurement data, and no empirical paper measuring tie rates under alternative tie-break rules in procurement.
- The published AEJ: Micro version of Clark et al. (which the abstract says contains "an easy-to-implement screen") was not read; the measures above are from the working paper and may differ from the final screen.
- "Bid roundness" (Ishii 2014, "Bid roundness under collusion in Japanese procurement auctions", Review of Industrial Organization 44:241–254) appeared in search results as a related marker but was not read. It matters because LLMs may favour round numbers, which would inflate tie rates for non-strategic reasons.

## 4. Collusion indices in simulation studies; is b(c) = c + (c_max − c)/n the right benchmark?

### Takeaway
The standard index is Calvano et al.'s normalised profit gain Δ = (π − π_N)/(π_M − π_N); LLM papers reuse it (sometimes on prices rather than profits) and report it unclipped, including negative values, while Fish et al. and most LLM-auction papers report raw prices/bids against Nash and monopoly benchmarks without an index. The risk-neutral symmetric BNE is the conventional benchmark, but it is known that humans and most non-reasoning LLMs bid more aggressively than it in first-price auctions, and that discretisation and tie rules can change the equilibrium set — so a nonzero (often negative) index is expected even without repeated-game effects.

### Cited Findings

#### Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò & Pastorello (2020), "Artificial intelligence, algorithmic pricing, and collusion", AER 110(10):3267–3297, doi:10.1257/aer.20190623 (Crossref-verified) [FT-WP: SSRN 3304991 version hosted by the FTC]
- Definition: "average profit gain Δ ≡ (π − π^N) / (π^M − π^N), where π^N is the per-firm profit in the Bertrand-Nash static equilibrium, π^M is the profit under full collusion (monopoly), and π is the profit upon convergence. Thus, Δ = 0 corresponds to the competitive outcome and Δ = 1 to the perfectly collusive outcome"; chosen because "this index can be compared directly across different economic settings" — [SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Level is not the definition of collusion: following Harrington (2017), collusion is "a situation in which firms use a reward-punishment scheme to coordinate their behavior for the purpose of producing a supra-competitive outcome ... what is crucial is not the level of profits as such but the way the supra-competitive result is achieved" — [SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Magnitudes: Δ ranges from 70% to 90% over the baseline grid of learning parameters — [SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Discretisation leaves a residual: for memoryless algorithms Δ is "less than 5%. These are almost negligible and do not vanish altogether simply because our discretization of the strategy space implies that the one-shot Bertrand equilibrium can at best be approximated" — [SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Caveat on bounds with asymmetry: the less efficient firm "may actually get less profit under joint profit maximization than in the Bertrand-Nash equilibrium. This is why the level of the individual profit gains Δ_i given asymmetry needs to be interpreted with caution" — [SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)

#### The benchmark itself
- Risk-neutral BNE for first-price sealed bid with n bidders and i.i.d. uniform private values: b*(v) = ((n−1)/n)·v — [Shah et al., arXiv:2507.09083](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09083)
- Humans: "persistent reports of significant bidding above the risk neutral Nash equilibrium (RNNE) benchmark in FPSB auctions, initial explanations of which focused on risk aversion"; the risk-aversion explanation is contested (alternatives: regret, probability miscalculation, "confusion"); deviations are hump-shaped in the valuation — [Kagel & Levin draft](https://www.asc.ohio-state.edu/kagel.4/HEE-Vol2/Auction%20survey_all_1_31_15.pdf)
- LLMs: in first-price IPV auctions "humans and the non-reasoning large models all predominantly overbid relative to the equilibrium shading rule" — overbids are 67.5% of deviating human bids and 85.9%, 97.8%, 89.9% for GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Haiku, Gemini 2.0 Flash; Llama-3-8B mostly underbids (82%); GPT-5-mini bids within tolerance of the shading rule in every case — [Shah et al., arXiv:2507.09083](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09083)
- Discreteness: for uniformly distributed values on a grid, "the first-price auction with two bidders and an even number of values has a symmetric equilibrium that closely resembles its continuous counterpart", but in other cases introducing discreteness removes or changes equilibria; two tie rules are analysed (fair random tie-breaking, and no winning through a tie) and the results "do not substantially hinge on which tie-breaking rule we assume" — [Rasooly & Gavidia-Calderon, arXiv:2006.03016](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03016)
- Reserve / maximum price as the collusive anchor: the weak-cartel optimum is for all members to bid the minimum price r (selling auction), which may be the announced reserve or the bidders' perception of what they can get away with — [McAfee & McMillan 1992](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)
- Field analogue of a reserve-anchored price measure: winning bid as a percentage of the maximum allowed bid (91–95% under collusion in Ishii's paving data; winning price within 5% of the reserve in 72% of Naha auctions) — [Harrington & Imhof 2022](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/harrington-imhof-2022.pdf); [Osaka DP 07-15](https://ideas.repec.org/p/osk/wpaper/0715.html)

### Inferences
- **(i) is a legitimate auction analogue of Calvano's Δ**, with two differences that the paper should state. First, it is price-based (like Garra's index) rather than profit-based; the buyer's harm is measured directly, but a price index of 1 is not "maximum joint bidder profit" unless the lowest-cost bidder wins — random allocation at the reserve gives the ring less than efficient allocation at the reserve. A companion profit-based Δ = (realised joint profit − BNE joint profit)/((reserve − lowest cost) − BNE joint profit) would be directly comparable with Calvano and Lee & Park and would combine (i) and (iii) in one number. Second, the upper anchor (reserve) is the McAfee–McMillan weak-cartel optimum, which is a natural justification to cite.
- **Unclipped reporting has precedent** at both ends: Garra and Lee & Park report negative Δ; Fish et al. report prices above monopoly. It is also the informative choice here, because the cited evidence predicts *negative* values absent collusion: in procurement the mirror image of "bidding above RNNE" is bidding below the BNE price, so human-like or non-reasoning-LLM bidders should give index < 0 in the one-shot control. Clipping at zero would hide that and bias (ii).
- Benchmark formula. Mirroring b*(v) = ((n−1)/n)v to procurement with costs i.i.d. uniform on [c_min, c_max] and a non-binding reserve (reserve ≥ c_max) gives b(c) = c + (c_max − c)/n, and by revenue equivalence the expected winning bid equals the expected second-lowest cost, c_min + 2(c_max − c_min)/(n+1). These are standard textbook derivations that I worked out rather than quoted from a source; they should be checked against an auction-theory text before publication. Caveats that follow from the cited sources:
  - *Reserve*: if the public reserve r is below c_max, the formula changes (bidders with c > r do not bid and the bid function is shaded toward r). My derivation gives b(c) = c + [(c_max − c)^n − (c_max − r)^n] / [n (c_max − c)^(n−1)] for c ≤ r, which reduces to the simple formula when r = c_max; unsourced, verify. If the reserve is above c_max the simple formula holds but the index denominator then includes a region no equilibrium bid would ever reach.
  - *Discrete grid and ties*: Rasooly & Gavidia-Calderon and Fish's footnote show the equilibrium set on a grid can differ from the continuous one and can include asymmetric or tie-involving equilibria; Calvano attribute a residual Δ < 5% to discretisation alone. The paper should either compute the grid equilibrium numerically or report how much index the rounding of the continuous BNE to the grid produces mechanically, and use that as the noise floor.
  - *Risk aversion / bounded rationality*: the benchmark is risk-neutral; the experimental literature's persistent departures from it are in the direction that produces a negative index in procurement. Hence (i) alone cannot separate collusion from a level shift in bidding style — the role of (ii).
  - *Realised vs expected benchmark*: because costs are known, the Bayes-Nash winning bid should be computed round by round from realised costs (b of the realised lowest cost), not as the unconditional expectation; this removes sampling noise in costs from the index (a paired comparison), an option unavailable in field data as Bichler et al. note.
- Xu et al. imply a blind spot for (i) and (ii): coordination through correlated bid residuals (e.g., two copies of the same model choosing the same focal bid) can leave each bidder's marginal bid distribution competitive. Metric **(vi)** (ties) and a within-round residual-correlation statistic (the Bajari–Ye test with known costs) are the natural complements.
- Rawat's vocabulary ("bid suppression" rather than "collusion" for outcome gaps) and winner entropy are useful: winner entropy is an alternative to the chi-square in **(iv)**.

### Gaps
- I did not find any paper that defines exactly the paper's index (winning bid normalised between Bayes-Nash and the reserve) for a private-cost procurement auction with LLM bidders; the nearest are Garra's price index and Fish's raw bid/value comparison.
- The published AER text of Calvano et al. was not accessed (AEA site returned a non-PDF); quotations are from the SSRN/FTC working-paper version.
- The procurement BNE formula with a binding reserve, and the CRRA-risk-aversion bid function (Cox, Smith & Walker 1988, J. Risk and Uncertainty 1:61–99, doi:10.1007/BF00055565 — Crossref-verified, not read) are not sourced from a text I read.
- Heo, Ahn & Park (2026) is the closest published LLM-procurement study but I could only read its abstract; how it measures "supracompetitive" and "reward–punishment" is unverified. Two related ASCE papers by the same group ("Algorithmic collusion in construction bidding", "Potential AI-driven algorithmic collusion and influential factors in construction bidding") appeared in search results and were not read.
- No source was found on equilibrium bidding under a least-wins-first tie-break or a best-and-final-offer rebid among tied bidders; the appropriate benchmark for those treatments is therefore not established in the literature I reviewed.

## 5. Controls for separating collusion from overbidding / bounded rationality

### Takeaway
The literature treats a supra-competitive level as insufficient: Calvano et al. require (a) the gain to vanish where collusion is impossible (no memory, or no weight on the future) and (b) a deviation-and-punishment response; Fish et al. use a lagged-price regression and a counterfactual "implantation" of reasoning text; experimentalists use random rematching to strip out repeated-game effects. A matched no-history control is therefore the accepted first step, but on its own it shows that repetition matters, not that a reward-punishment scheme is in use.

### Cited Findings
- Memory is necessary: "memoryless agents are unable to punish rivals' past defections and thus cannot genuinely collude" — [Calvano et al., SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Interpretation of gains in impossible-collusion settings: earlier studies found supra-competitive prices with δ = 0 or no memory, which "suggests that what [they] actually find is a failure to learn to play Nash"; "The key question is whether the high prices are the result of the algorithms' failure to learn the static Bertrand-Nash equilibrium or of genuine collusion" — [Calvano et al., SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Competitive-environment control: "collusion is impossible when k = 0 (the algorithms have no memory), and it cannot arise in equilibrium when δ = 0"; in their runs Δ falls from over 80% to 16% as δ falls to 0.35, and is below 5% for memoryless algorithms. They also report a small non-monotonicity at very low δ caused by δ changing the effective learning rate — a warning that the control can alter more than the strategic horizon — [Calvano et al., SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Impulse-response test: "upon convergence we step in and manually override one agent's choice, forcing it to defect", for one or several periods, to the static best response or smaller cuts; the average response over 1,000 sessions shows punishment of finite duration and gradual return; a punishment-intensity index correlates positively with Δ across the parameter grid — [Calvano et al., SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
- Fish et al. on-path regression: p^t_{i,r} = α_{i,r} + γ p^{t−1}_{i,r} + δ p^{t−1}_{−i,r} + ε, firm–run fixed effects, periods 101–300, disjoint pairs of periods, alternating which firm is "self", robust standard errors. P1: self 0.484 (0.102), competitor 0.103 (0.046), N = 2,100, R² = 0.209; P2: self 0.280 (0.083), competitor 0.022 (0.013), N = 2,100, R² = 0.081. A positive competitor coefficient is read as "suggesting a reward-punishment scheme" and the own-lag as stickiness — [arXiv:2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Fish et al. counterfactual ("implantation"): for each of three price-war-avoidance sentences, each of the 42 duopoly runs, each agent and each of periods 2–13, the simulation is reset and only the agent's plans/insights text is replaced, then prices are compared (two-sided Welch's t-test) — [arXiv:2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Fish et al. memory ablation in monopoly: clearing plans and insights between rounds reduced convergence to the monopoly price from 12/12 to 6/12 runs — evidence that removing state degrades competence, not only strategic memory — [arXiv:2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Keppo et al.: agents first "recover the one-shot monopoly optimum and the static Bertrand–Nash equilibrium with high precision (within 5% of theoretical benchmarks)"; "patient agents converge to supra-competitive pricing while myopic agents price competitively, with outcomes varying monotonically in the specified discount factor" — the two-myopic condition serves as the baseline — [arXiv:2603.20281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20281)
- Experimental design precedent: Dufwenberg & Gneezy (2002, JEBO 48(4):431–444, doi:10.1016/S0167-2681(01)00235-9) [FT] note that letting a fixed group interact repeatedly introduces "opportunities for cooperation of the kind studied in the theory of repeated games", and therefore use random matching over 10 periods "to allow for experience while avoiding repeated game effects". Their game: two players each choose an integer bid from 2 to 100, the lowest bid wins and is paid its bid (ties split) — interpretable as two firms requesting a price for a government task; unique Nash equilibrium is 2 — [Dufwenberg & Gneezy 2002](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/information-disclosure.pdf)
- Their result: even with random matching, "When bidders are informed about the losing bids in previous periods, prices are higher than the theoretical prediction. However, when this information is not revealed the bidding becomes more competitive"; the authors propose signalling through losing bids. Sessions: 3 feedback treatments × 2 sessions × 12 bidders; first-period average bids were 31.5–42.6 against an equilibrium of 2 — [Dufwenberg & Gneezy 2002](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/information-disclosure.pdf)
- Human baseline for the level: bidding above RNNE in first-price auctions is persistent, and Kagel & Levin flag that small fixed groups "might generate super-game effects that will contaminate the data (whereas the models employed commonly assume one-shot games)" — [Kagel & Levin draft](https://www.asc.ohio-state.edu/kagel.4/HEE-Vol2/Auction%20survey_all_1_31_15.pdf)
- Limits of tacit collusion in the lab: collusion "is a difficult topic to study in the laboratory since it is almost impossible to effectively introduce side payments, and experimental sessions have a natural end point which is likely to induce some unraveling"; since 1995 they identify a single single-unit auction collusion study (Hu, Offerman & Onderstal) — [Kagel & Levin draft](https://www.asc.ohio-state.edu/kagel.4/HEE-Vol2/Auction%20survey_all_1_31_15.pdf)
- Cartel stability by format: bidding rings are less stable in first-price sealed-bid than in English auctions, in both re-matching and fixed-matching conditions (Hinloopen, Onderstal & Treuren 2020, IJIO 71:102642, doi:10.1016/j.ijindorg.2020.102642) [FT-WP abstract and design] — [Tinbergen DP 19-009](https://papers.tinbergen.nl/19009.pdf)
- Non-collusive mechanisms that mimic the outcome: budget-constrained pacing produces collusion-like bid suppression (Rawat); learning agents may converge to equilibrium with revenue differences arising from objectives such as ROI (Bichler et al.) — [arXiv:2306.09437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09437); [arXiv:2312.00243](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00243)
- A data-only audit alternative: Hartline, Long & Zhang (2024), "Regulation of algorithmic collusion", define "plausible algorithmic non-collusion" as passing a statistical test on the data the algorithm collects (good algorithms can pass; algorithms that have colluded on supra-competitive prices cannot) [ABS] — [arXiv:2401.15794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15794)

### Inferences
- **(ii) is directly supported** by Calvano et al. §5.6.1 (memoryless / δ = 0 environments), Keppo et al. (myopic baseline) and Dufwenberg & Gneezy (random matching): subtracting the one-shot index removes the level effect of bounded rationality, risk attitude and grid artefacts that (i) cannot. It is the single most defensible metric in the set for a claim about *repeated-interaction* effects.
- What (ii) does not establish. (a) By the Harrington/Calvano definition, collusion requires a reward-punishment scheme; (ii) > 0 shows that history raises prices, which is necessary, not sufficient. A forced-deviation test (override one bidder's bid to the one-step undercut in a late round and trace rivals' subsequent bids) or the Fish-style lag regression adapted to bids would close this gap, and is cheap in simulation. (b) Dufwenberg & Gneezy show that *feedback about losing bids*, even without fixed partners, raises prices; a "no-history" control removes both the repeated relationship and the feedback, so (ii) bundles the two. A third arm (history shown, opponents re-drawn/anonymised each round) would separate them. (c) Fish's monopoly ablation and Calvano's δ-paradox show that removing memory can also reduce competence; the control should verify that one-shot bidders respond sensibly to their cost (slope of bid on cost near (n−1)/n).
- The one-shot control is not guaranteed to be "competitive": Xu et al.'s same-model coupling and McAfee–McMillan's communication-free identical-bid scheme both imply that homogeneous LLM bidders could coordinate on focal bids without any history. So a positive one-shot index or one-shot tie rate is itself a finding (monoculture coordination), and (ii) is a lower bound on total supra-competitive pricing.
- Human-subject comparisons (Kagel & Levin; Shah et al.) support stating an expectation of index < 0 in the control; if the control index is instead positive, the paper should say that its LLM bidders shade less aggressively than humans rather than attribute it to collusion.
- **(vii)** can serve as a built-in deviation test: the BAFO rebid gives a tied bidder a sanctioned opportunity to undercut. Small or zero cuts despite positive margins, repeated re-ties, and smaller cuts late in a session than early are the rebid analogue of "no profitable deviation taken".

### Gaps
- I found no LLM-auction paper that implements a forced-deviation (impulse-response) test on bidders; Fish et al.'s lag regression and implantation are for pricing, and Heo et al.'s "reward–punishment" claim could not be inspected.
- I did not find an explicit one-shot / no-history control in the LLM-collusion papers I read other than Keppo et al.'s myopic-agent condition and Fish's monopoly memory ablation; Garra reports none.
- Evidence on whether LLMs in a single-shot first-price *procurement* auction bid above or below the risk-neutral BNE was not found; Shah et al. study selling auctions.

## 6. Statistical practice for small numbers of simulated sessions; sample sizes in comparable work

### Takeaway
RL studies use about 1,000 sessions per cell, but LLM-agent studies use 3–21 runs per condition and treat the run (session) as the unit of analysis, comparing run-level means with Welch t-tests, rank tests or bootstrap intervals; multiple-comparison corrections are rare (found in three recent papers). Experimental economics and deep-RL methodology both recommend session/run-level inference with interval estimates.

### Cited Findings
- Sample sizes and tests reported by comparable papers (all [FT] unless noted):
  - Calvano et al.: 1,000 sessions per parameter set, mean and standard error across sessions; up to one billion periods per session — [SSRN/FTC version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)
  - Banchio & Skrzypacz: "1,000 experiments that each involve 1,000,000 auctions" — [arXiv:2202.05947](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05947)
  - Rawat: full/fractional factorial designs replicated across 8 seeds per cell (e.g., 2^6 = 64 cells × 8 = 512 runs); HC3 standard errors, Holm–Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg corrections, wild bootstrap p-values; "Inference is over realizations of the stochastic simulation, not over a population of real markets" — [arXiv:2306.09437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09437)
  - Fish et al.: 21 runs of 300 periods per prompt prefix (seven per α ∈ {1, 3.2, 10}); outcomes averaged over periods 251–300; two-sided Welch's t-tests "using a single firm from each run"; auction appendix 12 runs per prefix; monopoly screening 3 runs per model; I found no multiple-comparison correction in the text — [arXiv:2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
  - Garra: 21 run-level averages per prompt for the replication, 7 runs per condition for interventions; two-sided Welch's t-tests on run-level averages; Kruskal–Wallis across α as robustness — [arXiv:2609.13037](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13037)
  - Lee & Park: three 300-round runs per prompt condition per model (six per model; 48 runs for N = 3); Δ reported as mean ± SD across runs; two-sided Welch's t-tests with Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom; "Where multiple comparisons arise, we note the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold" — [arXiv:2609.18346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18346)
  - Keppo et al.: 10 independent runs per condition, 10 conditions, up to 1,000 periods per run (~2,235 compute hours); one-sided Welch's t-tests — [arXiv:2603.20281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20281)
  - Agrawal et al.: 10 trading sessions per condition, 30 rounds; means with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals — [arXiv:2507.01413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01413)
  - Tolety: each trial = 40 sequential auctions for N = 1,…,7 drivers; temperature 0.2; Mann–Whitney U tests comparing small vs large groups (e.g., U = 177, p < 0.001, r = 0.84); number of independent trials per configuration not found in the text I searched — [arXiv:2511.21802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21802)
  - Lin et al.: "several experiments" of 50 rounds; within-run circular block bootstrap (block size 7, 10,000 resamples) to allow for autocorrelation — [arXiv:2410.00031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00031)
  - Shah et al.: "more than 1,000 auctions with more than 5,000 GPT-4o bidder participants"; bootstrap bands (B = 2000) for reconstructed human benchmarks — [arXiv:2507.09083](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09083)
  - Xu et al.: 95% interval "clustered by developer"; a Bonferroni battery of marginal tests — [arXiv:2607.26385](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26385)
  - Human experiments: Dufwenberg & Gneezy — 2 sessions × 12 subjects per treatment, Mann–Whitney tests; Hinloopen et al. — 144 subjects in fixed matching groups of nine giving "in total 16 statistically independent observations" — [Dufwenberg & Gneezy 2002](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/information-disclosure.pdf); [Tinbergen DP 19-009](https://papers.tinbergen.nl/19009.pdf)
- Session effects: "observations across subjects of a given session might exhibit more correlation than observations across subjects in different sessions"; standard solutions "are at times inadequate" (Fréchette 2012, Experimental Economics 15(3):485–498, doi:10.1007/s10683-011-9309-1; Crossref-verified) [ABS] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10683-011-9309-1)
- Kagel & Levin describe the two competing practices — pooling with subject random effects vs treating each matching group/session as one independent observation — as "An unresolved methodological issue" — [Kagel & Levin draft](https://www.asc.ohio-state.edu/kagel.4/HEE-Vol2/Auction%20survey_all_1_31_15.pdf)
- Few-run evaluation in RL: Agarwal et al. (2021, NeurIPS), "Deep reinforcement learning at the edge of the statistical precipice": point estimates from "a small number of runs" ignore statistical uncertainty; "As a practical solution that is easily applicable with 3-10 runs per task", report "interval estimates via stratified bootstrap confidence intervals", performance profiles, and robust aggregates such as the interquartile mean — [arXiv:2108.13264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264)
- Screens literature: classification accuracy is averaged over 100 random 75/25 train/test splits on balanced samples — [arXiv:2105.00337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.00337); Chassang et al. use bootstrap confidence sets; Kawai et al. cluster by auction or by buyer — [NBER WP 25654](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654); [NBER WP 29625](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29625)

### Inferences
- The defensible unit of analysis for every metric (i)–(vii) is the session (run): rounds within a session share history, so round-level tests overstate precision. Compute each metric once per session and compare conditions with (a) Welch's t-test or a permutation/rank test on session-level values and (b) a percentile bootstrap over sessions for intervals — this matches Fish, Garra, Keppo, Agrawal and Agarwal et al. For within-session diagnostics (early vs late tie rate, lag regressions) use the session as the cluster or a block bootstrap as in Lin et al.
- Typical LLM-study sample sizes are 7–21 sessions per cell; with a design crossing three tie-break rules × history/no-history × several models, a matched-pair structure (same cost draws and seeds across treatment and control) will buy more power than extra sessions, and makes (ii) a paired difference.
- Multiple comparisons: with seven metrics, several models and three tie-break rules, the paper would be ahead of most of this literature by pre-designating confirmatory contrasts and applying Holm (family-wise) or Benjamini–Hochberg (exploratory) corrections, as Rawat does; only Lee & Park, Rawat and Xu et al. mention any correction.
- For (iv), a chi-square on win counts pooled over sessions treats rounds as independent and conflates between-session heterogeneity with within-session rotation; compute the dispersion statistic per session and test the distribution of session-level statistics, or use a permutation test that shuffles winners within session.
- Sampling temperature and model version are part of the "seed": Xu et al. find same-model coupling falls as temperature rises, and Tolety uses 0.2; the paper should report temperature and treat sessions at different temperatures as different conditions.

### Gaps
- I found no paper giving a power analysis or recommended minimum number of sessions specifically for LLM-agent market simulations.
- Fréchette (2012) was read at abstract level only; its specific recommendations (e.g., when session-clustered standard errors are adequate with few sessions) are not reproduced.
- Tolety's and Lin et al.'s exact numbers of independent runs were not found in the extracted text.
- Whether resampling LLM calls with a fixed prompt yields independent draws (given provider-side caching or deterministic decoding) is not addressed in the sources I read.

## 7. Using model reasoning / chain-of-thought as evidence

### Takeaway
Prior LLM-collusion work analyses reasoning text by keyword-plus-embedding classification and clustering (Fish et al.), LLM-judge scoring (Agrawal et al.), lexical analysis (Struski et al.) or stated-vs-behavioural graph comparison (Lee & Park), and the careful papers pair text with a behavioural or causal check. The faithfulness literature, and one 2026 paper on pricing agents specifically, say reasoning traces can corroborate but cannot establish or rule out collusion.

### Cited Findings
- Fish et al. — classification: sentences from agents' plans mentioning price wars (239 after filtering) are embedded with OpenAI text-embedding-3-large (3,072 dimensions) and assigned by cosine similarity to an "AvoidPriceWar" or "StartPriceWar" reference vector (each the average of four reference-sentence embeddings); 221 of 239 are closer to AvoidPriceWar (60.4% from P1 runs) — [arXiv:2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Fish et al. — clustering: 88,419 plan sentences → embeddings → PCA to 20 dimensions → k-means with 20 clusters (each 2.5%–9.1% of sentences) → GPT-4o summarises the 10 sentences nearest each centre; cluster prevalence is compared across prompts (the cluster most over-represented under P2 concerns undercutting) — [arXiv:2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Fish et al. — stated caveats: "it is not clear whether our pricing agents interpret these sentences like a human would. For this reason, we conduct an additional experiment" (implantation); "We are careful to validate our analysis as several papers have discussed the dangers of naïvely taking AI's stated explanations at face value (Arcuschin et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Motwani et al., 2024; Turpin et al., 2023)"; a validation of the price-war classifier is in their Appendix F — [arXiv:2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Fish et al. also note a stated-vs-revealed mismatch: asked directly, GPT-4 says it will not help collude, which they relate to sycophancy — [arXiv:2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)
- Garra: replicates the descriptive text analysis with E5-large-v2 embeddings (1,610 price-war sentences; 73.5% of AvoidPriceWar sentences from P1, 69.7% of StartPriceWar from P2) but does not run implantation, so "our off-path results should be interpreted as descriptive evidence about the agents' reasoning" — [arXiv:2609.13037](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13037)
- Agrawal et al.: an LLM evaluator (GPT-4.1-mini, citing Zheng et al. 2023) scores each seller's planning chain-of-thought each round from 1 (no coordination) to 4 (high coordination), covering tacit and overt coordination; seller-to-seller messages are excluded "to isolate how much collusive intent is detectable from reasoning alone"; scores are reported alongside price and profit metrics. Under oversight, sellers send one-character signals ("~", "↓") and one writes that it wants to gauge others "without signaling collusion or price fixing" — [arXiv:2507.01413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01413)
- Tolety: qualitative excerpts of the model's stated "reason" field, with the disclaimer that the situation labels "are ours, not the model's" — [arXiv:2511.21802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21802)
- Struski et al. (2026), "Competitive market behavior of LLMs", arXiv:2609.02580 [ABS]: lexical analysis of CoT traces in double auctions; executing a trade "is associated with a shift from strategic considerations toward urgency" — [arXiv:2609.02580](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02580)
- Lee & Park (2026): extract a stated causal graph (or inter-firm attention network) from CoT and compare it with a behavioural causal graph estimated from prices (Granger/PCMCI+); across nine LLMs "collusive behavior and chain-of-thought (CoT) faithfulness dissociate ... the most collusive model accurately reports cooperative intent yet reasons structurally unfaithfully, while the most structurally faithful model sustains supra-Nash pricing"; conclusion: "CoT monitoring alone cannot serve as a standalone safeguard against algorithmic collusion". Example: Sonnet 4.5 states attention to all rivals but shows a single Granger-causal link — [arXiv:2609.18346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18346)
- Faithfulness literature:
  - Turpin, Michael, Perez & Bowman (2023): CoT explanations "can systematically misrepresent the true reason for a model's prediction"; models fail to mention biasing features; accuracy drops by as much as 36% on 13 BIG-Bench Hard tasks (GPT-3.5, Claude 1.0) — [arXiv:2305.04388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)
  - Lanham et al. (2023): reliance on the CoT varies widely across tasks; "As models become larger and more capable, they produce less faithful reasoning on most tasks we study" — [arXiv:2307.13702](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702)
  - Chen et al. (2025), "Reasoning models don't always say what they think": models reveal use of a hint in at least 1% of cases where they use it, "but the reveal rate is often below 20%"; CoT monitoring "is not sufficient to rule [undesired behaviours] out" — [arXiv:2505.05410](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410)
  - Arcuschin et al. (2025): unfaithful CoT also occurs "on naturally worded, non-adversarial prompts" (implicit post-hoc rationalisation; rates up to 13% for production models, lower for frontier and thinking models); CoT "is not a complete account of the internal process" — [arXiv:2503.08679](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08679)
  - Korbak et al. (2025), "Chain of thought monitorability": CoT monitoring "is imperfect and allows some misbehavior to go unnoticed" but "shows promise"; monitorability "may be fragile" — [arXiv:2507.11473](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11473)

### Inferences
- Reasoning traces should be presented as corroborating evidence of *mechanism*, never as the primary outcome and never as proof of absence. The pattern in the best prior work is: (1) a pre-specified coding scheme or classifier with reported validation; (2) an association with behaviour (e.g., rounds whose reasoning is coded "match rival / avoid undercutting" have higher tie rates or smaller rebid cuts); and ideally (3) a causal intervention on the text (Fish's implantation). Garra's wording — "descriptive evidence about the agents' reasoning" — is the right register if step (3) is not done.
- For the paper's setting, natural codes are: intent to match a rival's previous bid; explicit aim to tie and rely on the tie-break; "taking turns"/rotation language; fear of a price war; reference to the reserve as a focal point; and, for (vii), the stated reason for not cutting in the rebid. If an LLM judge is used (Agrawal et al.), report inter-rater agreement against a human-coded subsample and judge-model identity; if embeddings are used (Fish et al.), report the reference sentences.
- Lee & Park's dissociation result and Chen et al.'s low reveal rates **caution against** two inferences: "no collusive language, therefore no collusion" and "collusive language, therefore the language caused the bids". Both directions need the behavioural metrics (i)–(vii).
- Reasoning visibility differs by model type: hidden reasoning tokens of reasoning models are not the same object as a requested "explain your bid" field; the paper should state which it analysed. Prompting for an explanation can also change behaviour (Fish's plans-and-insights ablation changed performance), so the no-reasoning-field condition is a useful robustness check.
- Agrawal et al.'s observation that agents under oversight shift to terse signals suggests that telling bidders their reasoning is monitored would itself be a treatment.

### Gaps
- I did not find a published inter-rater reliability figure (human vs LLM-judge) for coding collusive intent in any of the LLM-collusion papers read; Fish et al.'s Appendix F validation was not inspected in detail.
- No paper was found that codes reasoning traces of LLM *bidders in procurement auctions* for tie-seeking or rotation intent; Heo et al. (2026) may, but only its abstract was accessible.
- Motwani et al. (2024, "Secret collusion among generative AI agents"), cited by Fish et al. and Agrawal et al. on steganographic collusion, was not read.
- The faithfulness papers evaluate question-answering and reasoning tasks, not strategic market behaviour; apart from Lee & Park (preprint, not peer-reviewed as far as I could tell) there is no direct evidence on CoT faithfulness for pricing or bidding decisions.
