# Tie-break rules remain untested on LLM bidders

No paper found in this review varies the tie-break rule for identical bids with LLM bidders, and none studies exact-match bids or bid rotation as a tacit, communication-free coordination device among LLM agents; the same holds for reinforcement-learning bidders. Human-subject experiments have varied a tie rule (Davis and Wilson 2002 in repeated sealed-bid procurement markets; Puzzello 2008 in posted-price duopoly), but only as an equal-split versus random-winner contrast, never conditioned on win history and never replaced by a rebid. That makes the project's central manipulation (random vs. least-wins-first vs. best-and-final-offer rebid) new for LLM bidders and for learning algorithms, and new in form for human-subject work, subject to the caveat that the searches were keyword- and abstract-level and that the single closest paper, Heo, Ahn and Park (2026), was read only as an abstract when this review was first written (it has since been read in full; see the updates below). The novelty is real but the prior is not friendly: the economics literature says a risk-neutral, patient bidder gets the same expected share from a random tie-break as from least-wins-first, the only learning-algorithm study with values redrawn each round finds convergence to Bayes-Nash rather than collusion, and humans rarely collude tacitly in sealed-bid first-price auctions. The strongest support comes from McAfee and McMillan (1992), who show that identical bids with the auctioneer as allocation device are the optimal no-transfer, no-communication cartel scheme and who name the tie-break rule as the lever against it, and from Puzzello (2008), whose sharing tie rule raised perfect collusion over a coin toss in a posted-price lab market. On metrics, the literature backs the unclipped index and, most firmly, the index minus its one-shot control; it weakens the lowest-cost-wins share and the tie rate as stand-alone markers, and it suggests replacing the single cover-bid gap with the validated screen ratios and extending the rebid delta with rank-persistence and re-tie statistics. The claim that least-wins-first "mirrors a rule used in some public-sector vendor-panel procurement systems" is not supported for tied price bids: no regulation found breaks a sealed-bid tie by fewest previous awards, and the nearest tie rules (Hawaii, Utah 2006) deliberately favour the previous winner. Reviewers will press hardest on the theory-predicted null between random and least-wins-first, on calling an outcome gap "collusion" without a reward-punishment test, and on same-model lineups producing matching bids for non-strategic reasons.

A note on evidence status, which this report keeps throughout. "Read in full" means the note-taker read the paper text; "abstract only" means exactly that; "summarised" means content came through an automated page summary and numbers need re-checking; "snippet" or "unverified" means a search extract not checked against the source. **Update, 2 October 2026: Heo, Ahn and Park (2026) has now been read in full, and the novelty claim stands.** Statements below that call this paper "abstract only" or its design "unverified" predate the reading; this paragraph supersedes them. The journal paper uses two GPT-4o agents with the same prompt, lowest bid wins, 150 rounds and 30 runs per condition. The cost estimate is fixed at 1.0 with zero estimation error, so costs are common and known; rivals' bids for the last 20 rounds are disclosed, and each agent carries forward its own written observations and strategy. The only manipulation is the prompt prefix: a win-seeking sentence (B1) or a profit-seeking one (B2), plus a mixed pairing and a combined prompt. Bids converge to 1.001 under B1 and 1.107 under B2 (1.018 mixed, 1.040 combined), and reward–punishment is evidenced by regressing a bidder's bid on its rival's lagged bid (coefficients 0.615 and 0.954). The tie rule is one fixed line, "if bids are identical, the winner is selected at random"; no tie rate is reported and the rule is not varied. Market transparency, which the abstract mentions, is a discussion point and not an experimental manipulation: every condition discloses rival bids. The paper has no no-history control, no variation in bidder count or model, and no private-cost benchmark. Two consequences for this project: the supracompetitive result depends on the profit-seeking sentence, with bids at cost under the win-seeking one, so a neutral prompt may show little baseline elevation; and the lagged-bid regression is a ready-made reward–punishment test.

**Update, 3 October 2026: six further papers read in full, and one correction to the novelty claim.** Puzzello (2008), Davis and Wilson (2002), Sherstyuk (1999), Comanor and Schankerman (1976), Athey, Bagwell and Sanchirico (2004) and Heo, Park and Ahn (2024) were read in full text; the first three of those were scanned images and were read through machine OCR. Wherever the sections below still tag these papers "abstract only", this update supersedes the tag. Where they say no human experiment varied a tie rule, it supersedes that claim too.

*Correction.* Davis and Wilson (2002) varied a tie-breaking rule in a repeated, sealed-bid procurement environment: 16 sessions of four sellers over 40 periods, bidding on multi-unit contracts, half with restricted communication every fourth period and half without. In the no-communication sessions the buyer either divided purchases as equally as possible among sellers posting the same price (a rotating-purchase rule) or picked one tied seller at random and bought from that seller first (a fixed-purchase rule), switching after period 20 with the order rotated across sessions. Without communication the rule did not appear to affect convergence to the competitive price, although bids clustered more tightly around it under equal division. Puzzello (2008) varied the same contrast, sharing versus random, in posted-price duopoly. So the human-subject literature is not empty. What remains unstudied is a tie-break conditioned on win history, a rebid, a single indivisible contract with privately drawn costs, and any LLM or learning-algorithm bidder.

*Puzzello (2008).* A Bertrand–Edgeworth duopoly with capacity constraints, a simulated buyer, fixed pairs and 60 periods; treatments cross sharing versus random tie-breaking with a continuum price space versus multiples of 50. Perfect collusion, both sellers at the joint-profit-maximising price of 550, appeared in 7 of 16 markets under sharing with the coarse grid against 1 of 17 under random, and in 3 of 7 against 0 of 16 on the continuum. On the coarse grid the Mann–Whitney test on the longest run of consecutive ties gives $p = 0.06$, and a random-effects probit puts the sharing effect at about 30 percentage points (5% level); on the continuum the effect is not significant. The effect on price levels is not consistent: higher under sharing on the coarse grid, reversed on the continuum. Puzzello notes that under risk neutrality the rule should not matter, since expected profits are equal, though ex post profits are equal only under sharing, and that risk aversion on a discrete grid might change this. Her own explanation is specific to her game: under sharing it is enough to post the same price, whereas under random the sellers must also ration quantity, so sharing needs coordination on one variable instead of two. Cells hold 7 to 17 markets, so the result is suggestive, not firm.

*Davis and Wilson (2002), further findings.* Identical prices appeared in only 3 of the 40 possible periods (periods 31–40, four sessions) in the no-communication set-cost sessions, two of them under the fixed-purchase rule, and with communication in at least 9 of 10 periods in three of the four sessions; the abstract's statement that common prices appear only when sellers communicate holds up. They argue that any bid grid, even a one-cent increment, creates additional equilibria near the competitive price and may explain persistent price variability. In the cost design where rotations arise without collusion, they apply a Porter–Zona style screen: the correlation between losing bids and costs was 0.46 and 0.68 in two non-collusive rotation sessions but 0, 0.26 and 0 in three collusive ones. They also show the screen is fragile: a seller adding a last-digit signal raised one session's value from 0 to 0.26, and one endgame defection raised another from 0 to 0.69.

*Sherstyuk (1999).* Three bidders with complete information and two identical units, with four sealed-bid sessions of 21 periods and four weakly ascending oral sessions in which bid matching was allowed. Sealed-bid prices only partly converged to the competitive level: the mean trading price was 64.5 cents against a competitive level near 100, prices rose over time in all four sessions, two ended within 7 cents of competitive, and one stabilised near 40 cents. Oral sessions with bid matching averaged 25.6 cents. Her two conditions for collusion by bid matching are that actions are observable and answerable before trade, which sealed bids lack, and that the auctioneer picks randomly among equal bids, which both formats share. So it supports "sealed bids resist tacit collusion" only in part, and it does not vary the tie rule.

*Comanor and Schankerman (1976).* A verbal argument with a small empirical test, not a formal model. Identical bids leave shares to the buyer's haphazard choice among equally placed sellers; rotation sets shares by agreement and removes share variability, which risk-averse firms value, at higher coordination cost; large firms prefer rotation and small firms prefer identical bids, so identical-bid cartels should be rare when few firms are involved. Across 42 prosecuted bidding cartels from 1945 to 1974, those with 8 or fewer members were 23 rotating and 5 identical (an identical-bid share of 0.18, 95% interval about 0.07 to 0.38), against 7 and 7 for larger cartels; the difference is significant only at the 90% level, and the authors say the sample over-represents identical-bid cartels because they are easier to detect. The firm-size argument does not apply to symmetric bidders, which is what this project uses; the share-variability argument does.

*Athey, Bagwell and Sanchirico (2004).* Identical firms compete repeatedly in a Bertrand market with inelastic unit demand up to a reservation price, publicly observed prices, and privately known cost shocks redrawn every period; the market is split evenly among firms posting the lowest price. Pricing that always gives the sale to the lowest-cost firm can do no better than static competition. For patient firms, with a log-concave cost distribution or a high reservation price, the best symmetric scheme has no price wars on the equilibrium path and prices rigidly at the reservation price whatever each firm's cost, so each firm serves a $1/n$ share and efficiency is sacrificed. They note it needs no communication, and that McAfee and McMillan's weak-cartel result is the static counterpart. For impatient firms the scheme is only partly rigid, with low-cost firms permitted to cut price.

*Heo, Park and Ahn (2024), Q-learning bidders.* Reinforcement-learning agents bid against each other on a common cost estimate, with ten markup choices between 0.7 and 1.2 times the estimate, a memory of one past period of rivals' bids, and the actual cost drawn after bidding. Profit-maximising agents rose to a markup of 1.198, just under the 1.2 cap, with two bidders; win-maximising agents settled near 1.05; adding bidders lowered the markup (1.198 to 1.093 from two to five); and most of 900 learning-rate and exploration settings stayed above 1.06. The paper says convergence needed hundreds of thousands of iterations. It states that the lowest markup wins but not how ties are broken, even though a ten-point grid must produce many exact ties.

*Not verified.* Davis and Wilson and Puzzello both attribute to McAfee and McMillan (1992) the claim that government purchasing agents typically use a rotating purchase rule and switch to a fixed one when collusion is suspected. The McAfee and McMillan text extracted for this project says random tie-breaking is used in most government procurement auctions and offers a deterministic rule as the countermeasure; it contains no such passage. Treat the attribution as unverified and cite McAfee and McMillan only for what its own text says.

*Implications for the design (inference, not findings).* First, Athey, Bagwell and Sanchirico predict a sharper collusive signature than the plan currently names: bidding the reserve whatever the cost, which would show up as a tie rate near 1, a tie price index near 1, and a lowest-cost-wins share near $1/n$. The profit-maximising Q-learners that sat at the bid cap look like the same pattern. Second, three sources bear on the open bid-increment decision: Davis and Wilson say any grid creates extra equilibria, Puzzello's effect was clearest on a coarse grid, and Heo 2024 reports no tie rule on a ten-point grid, so the pilot should report the tie rate at 0.01 before the grid is fixed. Third, Davis and Wilson's loser-bid correlation is a cheap screen here, because the project knows every firm's cost and can compute it for all bids, not only losing ones, but it needs the caveats above. Fourth, a null on random against least-wins-first would not be surprising: Davis and Wilson found no tie-rule effect without communication, and the variance mechanism in Comanor and Schankerman and Puzzello needs risk aversion.

**Update, 3 October 2026 (later): Heo, Ahn, Oh and Park (2025) read in full.** This is the reinforcement-learning companion to the two Heo papers above (J. Management in Engineering 41(5), 04025045). Tabular Q-learning and deep Q-network bidders compete in a lowest-bid procurement game with a common cost (estimates close to the engineer's estimate, drawn from 1,244 Washington State projects), the actual cost drawn after the bid, a markup between 0.7 and 1.2 on an 11-point grid, full disclosure of past bids and a profit-based reward; the main experiment has two bidders and 100 sessions per condition. Both kinds of pair converged to the highest allowed bid for both bidders, which is a joint-profit-maximising outcome at the cap. It took about 700,000 steps for the tabular learners and about 5,000 for the deep ones, and agents re-paired with a new converged opponent, with exploration switched off, got back to the cap in about 1,500 and 300 steps; the paper equates the 150 or so rounds a re-paired deep learner needs with a contractor's bids in a year. A second experiment gave four deep learners one of three award rules: lowest bid, second-lowest bid (the winner is paid the second-lowest bid) and the bid closest to the mean. All three reached the cap eventually; the mean-bid rule was fastest and steadiest, the second-lowest rule showed temporary dips, and the lowest-bid rule settled slightly below the cap. The authors justify 100 sessions with a variance-stability criterion that asks for between 23 and 96 sessions depending on the condition.

Two points matter for this project. First, the collusive end state is both bidders at the cap, an exact tie on an 11-point grid, yet the paper says only that the lowest bid wins and never says how identical bids are resolved. The tie rule is where the surplus is split in the state the paper reports, and it goes unmentioned, which is a further instance of the gap this project fills. Second, a second-price-style award rule did not prevent collusion for these learners with four bidders, which differs from the Banchio and Skrzypacz result for stateless learners and is another sign that results depend on the learner's memory. As with Heo 2024, there is a common cost, no private information, no one-shot control and no Bayes-Nash benchmark.

**Update, 3 October 2026 (later still): no paper in the notes reports non-competitive unilateral bids by LLM bidders.** The `pilot_tiny` runs showed LLM bidders bidding the reserve in order not to win (loss avoidance) and bidding below their own cost while the stated reasoning claimed a profit (PLANNING.md 2.6, `results/pilot_tiny/ANALYSIS.md`). Checked against the notes, which are keyword-level and partly abstracts or automated summaries, none reports this behaviour for LLM bidders. The nearest items are related but different. Real cartels' phony or cover bids are losing bids unrelated to the bidder's costs (Porter and Zona 1993): the same signature, but by agreement. LLM overbidding relative to the equilibrium shading rule (Shah et al.: 85.9%, 97.8% and 89.9% of deviating bids for three models) and the human overbidding that Kagel and Levin survey are bounded-rationality deviations in selling auctions, not bids thrown to lose. The Tian poster (abstract only, unverified) reports DQN bidders overbidding their private costs in second-price procurement, attributed to experience replay. Rawat's budget-pacing result is a non-collusive mechanism that produces collusion-like bid suppression, the closest conceptual precedent. Kawai et al. and Chassang et al. show that backlog or capacity can produce rotation-like patterns without collusion, so unilateral withdrawal has a competitive explanation in the field. Lee and Park's finding that collusive behaviour and chain-of-thought faithfulness dissociate (abstract only) bears on the cases where the stated reasoning contradicts the bid. Inference: the behaviour looks new for LLM bidders as far as these notes show, but the claim should be written as "not found in the sources reviewed".

The conference version of the construction-bidding study is the best available proxy for the journal paper's design, and it removes cost heterogeneity entirely: the engineer's estimate is fixed and estimate error set to zero, so "all bidders derive identical estimates for the same project", with **two GPT-4o agents, full disclosure of rivals' bids over a 20-round window, and three runs per condition** ([Heo, Park & Ahn 2025](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/172_Large_Language_Models_in_Construction_Bidding__Opportunities_and_Risks_of_Algorithmic_Collusion.pdf)). Its tie rule is one fixed line, "in cases where all agents submit identical prices, the winner is chosen randomly", and no tie frequency is reported. The journal paper's abstract adds reward–punishment behaviour and a transparency lever, but the number of bidders, cost structure, benchmark and tie rule could not be verified because the publisher page returned HTTP 403 ([Heo, Ahn & Park 2026](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7144)). This paper must be read in full before the Related Work is finalised; it is the one item that could narrow the novelty claim.

Fish et al.'s auction appendix is the nearest repeated first-price sealed-bid precedent. Two bidders share a constant valuation $v$, "the winner is chosen uniformly at random in case of a tie", the one-shot Nash equilibrium is both bidding $v$, and a footnote notes that cent-level discretisation adds "additional equilibria involving bids of $v$ and $v - 1\,\text{¢}$" ([Fish et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)). The manipulation is a single prompt sentence, with four 300-period runs per cell. Tolety is the nearest tacit, no-communication, procurement-side, multi-model precedent with $N$ varied: GPT-4.1-mini agents paid a median \$11.80 at $N = 2$–$4$ against \$10.28 at $N = 5$–$7$, and in the three-driver case "converged on accepting at exactly \$11.75 with zero variance", which is an exact tie the author calls a focal point ([Tolety 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21802)). Tolety proposes that a platform "randomize price increments" to break focal points but tests neither that nor the allocation rule among simultaneous acceptors. Agrawal et al. supply the model-mix and oversight levers, with a caution worth carrying over: their no-communication arm averaged \$87.71 against a \$90 competitive price, and urgency messages overrode oversight ([Agrawal et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01413)). It should be cited as an ICML 2025 workshop paper, not a main-track paper. In the Mazur repository, rotation ("Let's rotate who gets the high bid… Next cycle S3, then S2.") and identical asks appear only as schemes proposed over a chat channel, which makes them explicit rather than tacit ([Mazur 2025](https://github.com/lechmazur/emergent_collusion)).

The answer to whether anyone has studied tie-break rules with LLM bidders is therefore no. Tie rules appear only as a fixed design line in Fish et al. and Heo et al.; nine arXiv API abstract queries run on 2 October 2026 combining tie-breaking, bid rotation, bid rigging or cartel with LLM terms returned nothing relevant. The notes state the limit plainly: a tie rule buried in a methods section is found only by reading the paper, and non-English, SSRN and NBER coverage was indirect. Inference from the notes: every lever tested so far in LLM auctions acts on the participants (how many, which model, what they are told, whether they can talk) or on the prompt; none acts on the auctioneer's allocation rule, so a tie-break manipulation is orthogonal to existing results. A second inference matters for framing: Fish et al. and Heo et al. use a common value or cost, where the competitive outcome is itself a tie at cost, so their designs cannot separate a tie used as a collusion device from a tie that is simply the Bertrand outcome. Private costs redrawn each round remove that confound, because the stage-game equilibrium predicts no exact-match bids.

Three of the project's methodological choices are also new to this strand. None of the auction papers runs a one-shot or no-history control condition, none uses a private-value Bayes-Nash bid function, and none reports a normalised index. A Bayes-Nash benchmark with LLM bidders exists only in the non-collusion literature (Shah et al., who benchmark against the "Bayes–Nash bid function in first-price auctions"), and normalised indices exist only in LLM pricing work such as Garra's $\Delta = (\bar{p} - p_N)/(p_M - p_N)$ ([Shah et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09083); [Garra 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13037)). Reasoning text in auction papers is analysed by LLM judge (Agrawal, Mazur), embedding clustering (Heo ISARC) or examples (Tolety); no auction paper found uses human hand-coding, and Agrawal et al.'s Krippendorff alpha of 0.948 measures the judge's agreement with itself, not with human coders.

Two adjacent results constrain interpretation. Xu et al. had twenty models bid in a first-price auction task and found residual pairwise correlation of **+0.053 for same-base-model pairs against +0.0001 for different-model pairs**, falling as sampling temperature rises ([Xu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26385); summarised, verify numbers). Inference: same-model lineups can produce matching bids from shared priors rather than repeated-game coordination, and only a one-shot control plus mixed-model groups can tell the two apart. Lee and Park report that across nine models in pricing competition "collusive behavior and chain-of-thought faithfulness dissociate" ([Lee & Park 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18346)), which bears directly on how much weight the hand-coded reasoning traces can carry.

## Learning algorithms tie to collude, but only with fixed common values

The reinforcement-learning literature gives the project its closest mechanistic precedent and its most uncomfortable prior. Banchio and Skrzypacz (read in full, arXiv v1 only) run two stateless Q-learners with a fixed common value of 1 on a 19-point bid grid for a million periods. First-price auctions converge to **average revenue 0.24 against a static-Nash 0.95**, second-price auctions reach Nash, and revealing the minimum bid to win restores near-competitive bidding ([Banchio & Skrzypacz 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05947)). The collusive outcome sits on the diagonal: "the algorithms do not converge on pairs of bids outside of the diagonal", and with persistent exploration bidders "spend most of the time on the diagonal (with equal bids)". Collusion in this model is identical bids resolved by an implied 50/50 tie-break, driven by "the incentive in first-price auctions to outbid opponents by just one bid increment". The authors analyse bid-rotation equilibria only in an appendix, stress that stateless learners "are not designed to learn conditional strategies like the bid rotation scheme", and warn that for algorithms with memory, extra information "could backfire by facilitating instead of discouraging tacit collusion (for example, in the form of bid rotation)". LLM bidders with the full history in context are exactly the memory-equipped case they flag.

No paper in this strand manipulates the tie-break rule. Kolumbus and Nisan, Deng et al., Bichler et al. and Rawat all break ties uniformly at random; Decarolis et al. use 1/m in simulation and bidder labels in theory; Brânzei et al. use lexicographic order (summarised) ([Kolumbus & Nisan 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11855); [Deng et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03906)). Inference from the notes: under a random rule, tying at a low bid is how two symmetric learners split the collusive surplus, so a rule that removes the expected share from an exact match (a rebid) or allocates it by win history changes exactly the payoff that makes the diagonal absorbing. That is a clean novelty claim for the algorithmic strand, with the caveat that the search was targeted rather than systematic.

The uncomfortable part is fragility. Bichler, Gupta and Oberlechner reproduce the Banchio–Skrzypacz result but find it "rel[ies] on the parameters of the Q-values", that heterogeneous algorithms bid "significantly higher", and that with values drawn i.i.d. each round bandit learners show no systematic collusion and learn the Bayes-Nash equilibrium ([Bichler et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00243v2); summarised, verify against the Information Systems Research version). Kolumbus and Nisan and Deng et al. show that for mean-based no-regret learners first-price auctions converge to Nash-like outcomes, with three or more top-value bidders giving almost-sure convergence. Rawat's factorial experiments changed conclusion across versions, ending at "no single auction format is universally superior when bidders are algorithms" ([Rawat](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09437); summarised). The Related Work should not present "first-price auctions are collusion-prone under algorithms" as settled. Inference: the project's redrawn private costs place it in the Bayesian regime, where the only learning-algorithm evidence found points to Bayes-Nash convergence, and almost every positive collusion result in auctions is for fixed, commonly known values.

Procurement-specific algorithmic work is thin. Sadoune, Joanis and Lodi model lowest-price procurement as a binary fair-or-collusive game and conclude the minimum-price rule "is generally resilient to non-engineered tacit coordination" ([Sadoune et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.03521); summarised). Heo, Ahn, Oh and Park (2025; read in full on 3 October) find that reinforcement-learning bidders converge to the highest allowed bid and that the bid evaluation method changes how fast and how stably they get there, which is an award-rule lever but not a tie-break and not LLMs (details in the update at the top) ([Heo et al. 2025](https://snu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/algorithmic-collusion-in-construction-bidding-risks-dynamics-and--2/)). A West Texas A&M poster reports DQN bidders overbidding in second-price procurement, with disclosure of past winning bids amplifying it; treat it as unverified.

Grid effects from this strand apply directly to the project's benchmark. With two equal top-value bidders, a discrete grid and random ties, Deng et al. obtain two Nash equilibria one step apart, and Calvano et al. attribute a residual profit gain "of less than 5%" for memoryless algorithms to discretisation alone ([Calvano et al., working-paper version](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_events/1494697/calzolaricalvanodenicolopastorello.pdf)). Inference: any index should be computed against the equilibrium of the discretised game under the specific tie rule, or at least report how much index the rounding produces mechanically. With the project's 0.01 increment on a 0–100 range this noise floor is small, but it should be stated rather than assumed.

## Economics names the tie-break as the lever, then predicts a null

### Identical bids are the optimal no-communication scheme

McAfee and McMillan (read in full) is the foundational citation. In a one-shot first-price auction with independent private values and no side payments, "the weak cartel can do no better than have its members submit identical bids", because "by submitting equal bids, the bidders in effect use the seller as their randomizing device" ([McAfee & McMillan 1992](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/mcafee+mcmillan-the-american-economic-review-1992A.pdf)). Two passages carry the project's argument. First, the scheme needs no communication: "the optimal mechanism can be implemented in a decentralized way: the bidders need not actually report to the mechanism, but simply bid $r$ if and only if their valuations exceed $r$." Second, the tie-break is named as the countermeasure: "the seller can easily disrupt the mechanism by refusing to randomize", for example by awarding "to the smallest firm bidding, or to the bidder whose name comes first in the alphabet. Then it would no longer be in the interest of bidders so discriminated against to remain in the cartel. The cartel members could defeat this ploy by rotating their bids." The result requires the selling-auction condition that $(1 - F)/f$ be decreasing, which the authors call "the more likely case"; mirroring it to the project's uniform procurement costs is an inference that should be checked rather than asserted.

That passage points both ways, and the paper must say so. McAfee and McMillan's remedy is a deterministic rule that permanently disadvantages someone. Least-wins-first is deterministic but equalising, so nobody is discriminated against and the remedy does not bite; they do not analyse such a rule. The repeated-game analogue is Athey, Bagwell and Sanchirico (read in full): with private i.i.d. cost shocks, the optimal symmetric collusive scheme has no price wars on the equilibrium path and a rigid price, set at the reservation price whatever each firm's cost, with each firm serving a $1/n$ share ([Athey, Bagwell & Sanchirico 2004](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v71y2004i2p317-349.html)). Inference from the notes, and the single most important theoretical point for the paper: under risk neutrality and patience, expected shares are equal under random and least-wins-first, so standard theory gives no first-order difference in the sustainability of identical bidding. Any difference has to come from the variance of realised shares over a finite horizon, from a losing bidder reading an unlucky streak as defection, or from salience. The project's planning document already concedes that least-wins-first adds certainty rather than expected value; the Related Work should state it as the theoretical baseline against which a positive result is surprising.

The historical record cuts against a naive reading. Comanor and Schankerman (read in full) conclude that "identical bid arrangements are unlikely to be maintained among colluding firms where their number in an industry is small" ([Comanor & Schankerman 1976](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/bellje/v7y1976ispringp281-286.html)); the federal identical-bid reporting requirement ran for 22 years and was revoked in 1983 as "ineffective" ([Executive Order 12430](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-12430-reports-identical-bids)); and a Department of Justice identical-bids unit "failed to uncover a single conspiracy" in six years ([Abrantes-Metz et al., working-paper version](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/variance-screen-collusion/wp275_0.pdf)). Small human cartels moved away from identical bids toward rotation with cover bids, partly because identical bids are conspicuous. The project's claim that matching avoids the execution risk of cover bidding is consistent with McAfee and McMillan's remark that rotation "requires more coordination", but the paper should acknowledge that identical bids carry higher detection risk. One sentence from Asai and Charoenwong's Singapore study (2019 draft read) states the trade-off the hypothesis relies on: "Compared to identical bidding, bid rotations provide a clear market-sharing rule, but the coordination costs will be higher" ([Asai & Charoenwong](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/micropaper19/micro1224.pdf)). Least-wins-first supplies the clear sharing rule at identical-bid coordination cost. Comanor and Schankerman's own reasoning adds two points for this project. Their firm-size argument, that large firms refuse the equal shares identical bids give, does not apply to symmetric bidders. But they also name the variability of shares under identical bids, which rotation removes and risk-averse firms dislike, as a reason cartels prefer rotation, and that is the mechanism by which least-wins-first could differ from random (see the 3 October update).

### Least-wins-first hands the cartel's ledger to the auctioneer

Skrzypacz and Hopenhayn (read in full) study repeated sealed-bid auctions with no communication where "the only information available to players is the identity of winners". Their scheme that beats simple rotation is a win-count ledger: "After each auction, the winner gives one chip to the loser. If player i runs out of chips, he is supposed to allow player j to win k auctions in a row" ([Skrzypacz & Hopenhayn 2004](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/skrzypacz+hopenhayn-journal-of-economic-theory-2004A.pdf)). Athey and Bagwell (abstract read) find optimal schemes use "future market-share favors", and in that framework the firms themselves decide how a price tie is shared ([Athey & Bagwell 2001](https://web.mit.edu/athey/www/ocpiwfig.pdf)). Field evidence matches: Ishii (abstract; working paper read by one note-taker) finds Japanese rings designate winners by a running balance of favours, and Kawai, Nakabayashi, Ortner and Chassang (NBER version read in full) find that in Japanese municipal auctions "close winners have a significantly lower backlog than close losers" ([Kawai et al. 2023](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29625/w29625.pdf)). Inference from the notes: least-wins-first is an auctioneer-run version of the chips and favour-ledger schemes. A tacit cartel needs one piece of coordination, the same number, and receives a deterministic rotation in which nobody has to submit a deliberately losing bid. No theoretical or empirical paper found studies an allocation or tie-break rule that mechanically favours bidders with fewer past wins, so condition two looks novel in economics as well. The nearest field precedent is not a regulation but a case: in Oklahoma highway asphalt auctions from 1954 to 1965, bids were identical and "the OHD awarded the contract to the nearest firm to the job site ... which, it was argued, acted as a market allocation scheme" ([Harrington, working-paper version](https://www.competitionpolicyinternational.com/assets/Uploads/Detecting-Cartels.pdf)).

### No regulation breaks price ties by fewest past awards

The notes sort real tie-break rules into four families, and none matches least-wins-first.

| Family | Examples (primary text read unless noted) | Relation to the project |
|---|---|---|
| Preference, then lots | US FAR 14.408-6; Japan Cabinet Order Art. 83; Maryland COMAR 21.05.02.14; Washington coin toss; Philippines (snippet) | The `random` condition |
| Rebid among tied bidders | Italy R.D. 827/1924 art. 77; Brazil Lei 14.133/2021 art. 60; UN Procurement Manual; West Virginia § 99-6-7 | The `bafo` condition |
| Rules written to discourage ties | Hawaii § 3-122-34; Utah S.B. 220 (2006); Louisiana LAC 34:V.529 | The mirror image of `least_wins` |
| Equitable distribution of work (not tie-breaks) | FAR 13.203(a)(1); Florida § 287.055(4)(b); DFARS PGI 236.602-1; Shire of East Pilbara panel policy; South Africa (snippet) | The closest real rules in spirit |

The rebid condition has solid institutional grounding. Italy's 1924 rule has tied bidders rebid among themselves in the same session, with lots only if they are absent or decline to improve ([R.D. 827/1924 art. 77](https://www.codiceappalti.it/rd_827_1924/Art__77_/2578)); Brazil's 2021 statute puts a "disputa final" among tied bidders first in its order of criteria ([TCU](https://licitacoesecontratos.tcu.gov.br/5-4-2-desempate-2/)); the UN manual says procurement staff "shall request a BAFO from the 'tie-bidders'" and falls back to a public drawing if that is "without result" ([UN Procurement Manual](https://elearning.un.org/CONT/PROCS/CS/1-02_PM_Modules/Module_11/M11P01S21.html)). The project's fallback to random on a tied rebid matches the Italian and UN practice.

The least-wins-first condition does not. Hawaii lets the procurement officer "award the contract to the bidder offering a low tie bid who received the previous award, and continue to award succeeding contacts to the same bidder so long as all low bids are identical" ([Haw. Code R. § 3-122-34](https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/hawaii/Haw-Code-R-SS-3-122-34)); Utah's 2006 bill adds that tie awards "shall not be made by: (a) dividing business among tied bidders" ([Utah S.B. 220](https://le.utah.gov/~2006/bills/sbillenr/SB0220.htm)); Louisiana requires award "in any manner that will discourage tie bids" ([La. Admin. Code tit. 34, § V-529](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/louisiana/La-Admin-Code-tit-34-SS-V-529)). These are McAfee and McMillan's "refuse to randomise" in statute form, although the anti-collusive intent is inferred from the wording and no official statement of purpose was found. The OECD's 2009 guidelines say "Avoid splitting contracts between suppliers with identical bids" and the 2025 update says "Whenever possible, add criteria to resolve any tie between bidders. Investigate the reasons for any identical bids and, if necessary, consider re-issuing the invitation to tender" ([OECD 2009](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2009/02/guidelines-for-fighting-bid-rigging-in-public-procurement_d4a5f7a8/8cfeafbb-en.pdf); [OECD 2025](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/09/oecd-guidelines-for-fighting-bid-rigging-in-public-procurement-2025-update_127880ea/cbe05a56-en.pdf)). Rules that steer work toward suppliers who have had less of it do exist, framed as fairness: micro-purchases "shall be distributed equitably among qualified suppliers" ([FAR 13.203](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/13.203)), and Florida's consultant-selection statute considers "the volume of work previously awarded to each firm by the agency, with the object of effecting an equitable distribution of contracts among qualified firms" ([Fla. Stat. § 287.055](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/statutes/2024/287.055)). They govern panel allocation, micro-purchases and qualifications-based selection, not tied price bids.

The vendor-panel sentence should therefore not appear in the paper. The project's planning document has already dropped it and reframed least-wins-first as a design choice, which the notes support. The defensible replacement, offered in the notes as a suggestion rather than a sourced claim, is that the rule is modelled on equitable-distribution provisions in panel, micro-purchase and qualifications-based procurement, transplanted to the resolution of tied price bids, with an explicit statement that no jurisdiction is known to use it as a sealed-bid tie-break. That framing is more interesting than the original: least-wins-first is the intertemporal equivalent of the contract splitting the OECD warns against, and the Hawaii and Utah rules serve as a foil showing that practitioners already treat tie rules as a collusion lever. Gaps remain for World Bank, EU, UK, Singapore and Australian Commonwealth rules, and the ABA Model Procurement Code text was not located.

### A rebid helps only when no winner is designated

Theory treats a final sealed round as pro-competitive. Klemperer's Anglo-Dutch design ends with "a best and final sealed-bid offer" because it "eliminates the final stage of the ascending auction ... in which phase an ascending auction is particularly vulnerable to collusive and predatory behavior" ([Klemperer 2002](https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/Users/Klemperer/designnew.pdf)), and Maskin and Riley resolve ties by a second-round auction among the tied bidders as an existence device (snippet) ([Maskin & Riley 2000](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/maskin/files/equilibrium_in_sealed_high_bid_auctions.pdf)). The main empirical study says explicit cartels pass through rebids untouched. In about 42,000 Japanese national construction auctions, Kawai and Nakabayashi find the second-lowest first-round bidder "loses to $i_{(1)}$ in the reauction more than 95% of the time", with "hundreds of auctions in which $i_{(2)}$ is outbid by $i_{(1)}$ in the reauction by a tiny margin, but almost none in which $i_{(1)}$ is outbid by $i_{(2)}$" ([Kawai & Nakabayashi 2022, authors' version](http://www.keikawai.com/detection_revised.pdf)); the 96.70% persistence figure comes from the 2014 working paper and was not checked against the published text. Inference from the notes: a rebid disrupts a tie only when the tie has no designated winner, which is the project's no-communication setting, so the mechanism that made Japanese rebids harmless to cartels is absent here, and the paper should say so explicitly. Three gaming routes follow from the sources: re-tying in the rebid so the fallback becomes the effective rule, using round one as a signal of who is owed, and abandoning exact ties for cover-bid rotation, which is the response Mund documented according to McAfee and McMillan. A competitive side effect also deserves a sentence: bidders who anticipate a rebid can bid less aggressively in round one, the multi-round pattern in Ji and Li (abstract only) ([Ji & Li 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/jae/japmet/v23y2008i7p897-923.html)). No paper found models or tests a rebid triggered specifically by an exact tie in a repeated procurement auction.

### Humans rarely tie tacitly under sealed bids

The human-subject evidence sets the benchmark. Puzzello (read in full) is the nearest precedent for the manipulation: in posted-price duopoly, "the incidence of perfect collusion is significantly higher under the sharing tie-breaking rule than under the random (coin-toss) one, especially when the price space is less divisible" ([Puzzello 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jeborg/v67y2008i1p164-179.html)). A sharing rule gives each tied seller a certain half where a coin toss gives the same expectation with risk, which is the same contrast as least-wins-first against random. Sherstyuk (read in full) found that sealed-bid outcomes only partly converged to the competitive equilibrium (mean price 64.5 cents against a competitive level near 100, with one of four sessions stabilising near 40) while oral auctions with bid matching sustained collusion ([Sherstyuk 1999](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1009929832148)). Davis and Wilson (read in full) give two warnings: "common prices are observed only when sellers communicate", and "market rotations are observed both with and without collusion" ([Davis & Wilson 2002](https://doi.org/10.1093/ei/40.2.213)). Bidding rings are less stable in first-price sealed-bid than in English auctions ([Hinloopen, Onderstal & Treuren 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijindorg.2020.102642)). Inference: the hypothesis predicts something humans mostly do not do, so tacit identical bidding by LLMs would be a departure from the human benchmark worth flagging, and a null would be consistent with it. Two human experiments did vary a tie rule, as an equal-split versus random-winner contrast: Davis and Wilson found no effect on prices without communication, and Puzzello found more perfect collusion under sharing in a posted-price duopoly. Neither used a single indivisible contract with privately drawn costs, a rule conditioned on win history, or a rebid. Puzzello's grid moderation is a design warning: her effect on collusion was clearest on a coarse price grid and was not significant on a fine one, where the effect on price levels reversed; the project's 0.01 increment is a fine grid.

## The control-differenced index holds; four metrics need rework

The detection literature was built for analysts who cannot see costs. Porter and Zona test whether bids respond to cost, Bajari and Ye test conditional independence and exchangeability, Chassang et al. test for profitable small deviations, and Kawai et al. test whether winning is as-if random among nearly tied bidders. With costs known every round, each becomes a direct test, and the project can cite these papers as the origin of statistics it computes exactly rather than approximately. The table gives the verdict per metric; the supporting reasoning follows.

| Project metric | Literature verdict | Suggested change (inference unless noted) |
|---|---|---|
| Collusion index vs. Bayes-Nash, unclipped | Supported; unclipped reporting has precedent at both ends | Add a profit-based companion; report the grid-rounding noise floor; state that the benchmark is risk-neutral |
| Index minus matched one-shot control | Most strongly supported | Add a reward-punishment test; consider a third arm separating feedback from repeated partners; check one-shot bid-on-cost slope |
| Lowest-cost-wins share | Weakened as a stand-alone marker | Read jointly with the index; do not label high-share, history-driven elevation "overbidding" |
| Win-count chi-square, repeat-win rate | Direction problem confirmed; invalid under least-wins-first | Two-sided dispersion or winner entropy; regress win on lagged wins controlling for cost rank |
| Cover-bid gap | Supported in spirit; single gap conflates two signals | Report DIFFP, RD or RDNOR, CV of bids, and the Chassang $\Delta$ density |
| Tie rate early vs. late, tie price | Supported by theory; contradicted as a stand-alone screen | Compare with one-shot and grid-rounded Bayes-Nash tie rates; add tied bidders' margin and forgone one-step undercut gain |
| Rebid price minus tied bid | Supported, but the literature's statistics are rank-based | Add re-tie probability, who cuts, cut relative to remaining margin |

The index is a legitimate auction analogue of Calvano et al.'s profit gain

$$\Delta \equiv \frac{\pi - \pi^N}{\pi^M - \pi^N},$$

with the reserve as upper anchor justified by McAfee and McMillan's weak-cartel optimum. Unclipped reporting is established: Garra reports $\Delta = -0.166$ and lower, and Lee and Park define $\Delta < 0$ as "destructive competition below Nash levels" on a "$[-\infty, 1]$ scale". Unclipped is also the informative choice, because the cited evidence predicts negative values absent collusion. Kagel and Levin document "persistent reports of significant bidding above the risk neutral Nash equilibrium" in first-price auctions ([Kagel & Levin draft](https://www.asc.ohio-state.edu/kagel.4/HEE-Vol2/Auction%20survey_all_1_31_15.pdf)), and Shah et al. find GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Haiku and Gemini 2.0 Flash overbid in 85.9%, 97.8% and 89.9% of deviating bids in selling auctions. The procurement mirror image is bidding below the Bayes-Nash price, so the one-shot control should show a negative index for non-reasoning models; no source tested this in a procurement auction. The benchmark formula

$$b(c) = c + \frac{c_{\max} - c}{n}$$

and the expected winning bid were derived by the note-taker from the selling-auction formula rather than quoted from a source and should be checked against a textbook; the project's choice of reserve equal to the top of the cost range keeps the simple formula valid under that derivation. Two differences from Calvano should be stated: the index is price-based, so an index of 1 is not maximum joint bidder profit unless the lowest-cost bidder wins, and a profit-based companion would fold the index and the lowest-cost-wins share into one number comparable with the pricing literature. No source establishes equilibrium bidding under least-wins-first or a tie-triggered rebid, so the benchmark for those two arms is the random-rule benchmark by assumption, and the paper should say that.

The control-differenced index is the most defensible metric in the set. Calvano et al. require the gain to vanish where collusion is impossible ("collusion is impossible when $k = 0$ (the algorithms have no memory)"), Keppo et al. use myopic agents as baseline, and Dufwenberg and Gneezy use random matching "to allow for experience while avoiding repeated game effects" ([Dufwenberg & Gneezy 2002](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/information-disclosure.pdf)). Harrington's observation that screening for changes produces fewer false positives than screening for levels argues for leading with the difference. The literature also marks its limits. By the Harrington and Calvano definition, collusion is "a reward-punishment scheme" and "what is crucial is not the level of profits as such but the way the supra-competitive result is achieved"; a positive difference shows that history raises prices, which is necessary and not sufficient. A forced-deviation test (override one bidder to a one-step undercut late in a session and trace rivals' responses) or Fish et al.'s lag regression adapted to bids would close the gap cheaply; no LLM auction paper found has run one. Dufwenberg and Gneezy also found that feedback about losing bids raised prices even without fixed partners, so a no-history control removes both the relationship and the feedback and the difference bundles the two. Fish et al.'s monopoly ablation, where clearing memory cut convergence from 12 of 12 to 6 of 12 runs, shows that removing state can reduce competence as well as strategic memory. And Xu et al. plus McAfee and McMillan imply the one-shot control is not guaranteed competitive: homogeneous bidders can coordinate on focal bids with no history, so a positive one-shot tie rate is itself a finding and the difference is a lower bound on total supracompetitive pricing.

The lowest-cost-wins share is supported by McAfee and McMillan (a no-transfer cartel cannot allocate efficiently) but qualified by real cartels that did. The Ticino road-construction cartel "implemented a cost-based allocation mechanism that closely approximated the first-best collusive outcome" and passed Bajari–Ye tests "while being fully collusive", with an estimated overcharge of at least 45% ([Imhof, Madiès & Huber 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30470)). Inference specific to this project: the planning rule that a cell counts as consistent with tacit rotation only when the difference is positive and the share falls is right for rotation, but its complement should not be labelled "overbidding". A positive difference with a share near one is history-dependent price elevation with efficient allocation, which is the Ticino pattern and the Athey–Bagwell prediction, and deserves its own category.

For win patterns, the notes confirm what the project already documents: with i.i.d. costs, competitive win counts are already uniform, so rotation makes them more even than multinomial and an upper-tail chi-square has no power. A two-sided dispersion statistic or Rawat's winner entropy is the alternative, and the closer analogue of Kawai et al. and Ishii is a regression of the win indicator on lagged wins controlling for own cost and cost rank, where the lagged-win coefficient is zero under competition. The Kawai regression discontinuity cannot be applied literally: its key lemma needs smooth residual demand, and least-wins-first mechanically creates a backlog discontinuity among tied bidders. Under that arm the rule produces the very pattern the literature treats as a signature of collusion, which is a line worth putting in the paper.

The cover-bid gap needs the most rework. The validated statistics separate the gap between the two lowest bids from the spacing among losers:

$$\mathrm{DIFFP} = \frac{b_2 - b_1}{b_1}, \qquad \mathrm{RD} = \frac{b_2 - b_1}{s},$$

with $s$ taken over the losing bids, plus RDNOR and the per-round coefficient of variation ([Wallimann, Imhof & Huber](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05629); [Imhof, Karagök & Rutz, working-paper version](https://crese.univ-fcomte.fr/uploads/wp/WP-2017-09.pdf)). With three bidders, kurtosis is undefined and RD rests on a two-point standard deviation, so DIFFP, spread and CV are the usable ones. Field thresholds (CV at or below about 0.06, RD above 1) are calibrated on Swiss road construction and screen levels differ roughly tenfold across countries, so the reference must be the screen's distribution under the Bayes-Nash function applied to realised costs and under the one-shot control. Chassang et al.'s $\Delta$, own bid minus lowest rival bid over the reserve, is a ready-made citable form, with a "missing mass of close losing bids" as the collusive signature ([Chassang et al., NBER version](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25654)). Clark, Coviello and De Leverano show that a real cartel produced "both clustering and a gap around the winning bid" ([Clark et al. 2025](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mic.20210450)). Inference: identical bidding and cover bidding have opposite signatures (mass at zero gap against a hole just above zero), and in a tacit setting with no designated winner the missing-bids signature can be absent even when prices are supracompetitive, so absence of a gap is not evidence of competition.

Tie metrics are well grounded in theory and weak as a stand-alone screen. Chassang et al. show that a mass of tied lowest bids at positive margins cannot be competitive, because a tiny undercut "increases a bidders likelihood of winning by a non-vanishing amount, while reducing profits by a negligible amount". The tie price level is the right companion: ties near the reserve are the McAfee–McMillan scheme, ties near cost are focal bidding. The identical-bids unit's failure and Marshall and Marx's note that with a discrete increment one would "expect to see ties occasionally" under competition mean the defensible claim is comparative, against the one-shot control and the grid-rounded Bayes-Nash tie rate. Ishii's bid-roundness marker was not read, but the concern it raises is plausible: LLMs that favour round numbers will tie for non-strategic reasons.

For the rebid delta, Kawai and Nakabayashi support using rebids as evidence, but their statistics concern rank persistence and who undercuts whom. Additions that follow: the probability the rebid ties again, the probability each tied bidder cuts, the cut relative to remaining margin, and whether the one-step undercut was available and unprofitable or simply not taken. Inference: the rebid doubles as a sanctioned deviation test, so small cuts at positive margins and repeated re-ties are the rebid analogue of no profitable deviation taken.

On statistical practice the project sits ahead of the field. Comparable LLM studies use 3 to 21 runs per condition (Heo ISARC 3, Garra 7, Keppo and Agrawal 10, Fish 21), treat the run as the unit and rarely correct for multiple comparisons; only Lee and Park, Rawat and Xu et al. mention any correction. Session-level inference, seed-paired controls, bootstrap intervals over sessions and two Holm-corrected confirmatory contrasts at 18 sessions match or exceed that practice, and Agarwal et al. recommend exactly the interval approach "with 3-10 runs per task" ([Agarwal et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264)). Sampling temperature belongs in the condition description, given Xu et al.'s temperature result. For reasoning traces the register to copy is Garra's "descriptive evidence about the agents' reasoning": Chen et al. find models reveal a hint they used at a rate "often below 20%" ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410)), so hand-coded traces corroborate mechanism and cannot establish or rule out collusion. If an LLM judge supplements the hand-coding, agreement against a human-coded subsample fills a gap no prior LLM-collusion paper has filled.

## What to cite, and where reviewers will push

A workshop-length Related Work needs roughly fifteen citations across the four strands. For LLM bidders: Fish et al. including Appendix A.6, both Heo et al. LLM papers, Tolety, and Agrawal et al., with Shah et al. for the Bayes-Nash benchmark and Xu et al. for same-model correlation. For algorithmic collusion: Calvano et al. for the index and the memoryless control, Banchio and Skrzypacz for identical-bid collusion and the bid-rotation warning, and Bichler et al. for Bayes-Nash convergence under redrawn values. For economics: McAfee and McMillan with the page 586 passage, Skrzypacz and Hopenhayn for the chips ledger, Athey, Bagwell and Sanchirico for rigid pricing under private costs, Puzzello for the tie-rule experiment, and Kawai and Nakabayashi for rebids. For detection: Kawai et al. on rotation and backlog, Chassang et al. on missing bids and ties, and Imhof et al. or Wallimann et al. for the screens. For practice: one rule per family (FAR 14.408-6, Italy's art. 77 or the UN manual, Hawaii § 3-122-34) and the OECD guidelines. Athey, Bagwell and Sanchirico, Puzzello, Comanor and Schankerman, Sherstyuk, Davis and Wilson, and Heo, Park and Ahn (2024) were first read as abstracts only and have since been read in full (3 October). Add Davis and Wilson to the economics list, as the closest human precedent for varying a tie rule. Mund is known only through McAfee and McMillan; McAfee and McMillan has no confirmable DOI.

The novelty sentence the evidence supports is narrow and should be written narrowly: to the authors' knowledge, no prior work manipulates the tie-break rule for identical bids with LLM bidders or learning algorithms; human-subject experiments have varied an equal-split versus random-winner rule (Davis and Wilson 2002; Puzzello 2008), and none found varies a tie-break that conditions on win history or replaces it with a rebid. Secondary novelty claims hold for the LLM auction strand only: private costs redrawn each round, a matched no-history control, a Bayes-Nash benchmark and a normalised index. The paper should not claim that tie-break rules are unexplored as a collusion lever in general, since McAfee and McMillan, Davis and Wilson, Puzzello, the OECD and three US states got there first.

Reviewers who know this literature will raise the following, roughly in order of force. First, theory predicts no first-order difference between random and least-wins-first, so the paper needs a stated mechanism (variance, perceived fairness, salience) and should present the contrast as an empirical question about LLM bidders rather than a theoretical prediction. Comanor and Schankerman and Puzzello supply the variance mechanism (rotation removes share variability that risk-averse firms dislike), but the closest human precedent in procurement, Davis and Wilson, found no tie-rule effect without communication, so a null here would be unsurprising. Second, outcome is not conduct: without a deviation-response test the result is, in Rawat's deliberately neutral vocabulary, a price gap "without presupposing its cause", and Calvano et al. would read a history-free gap as "a failure to learn to play Nash". Third, same-model lineups: Xu et al.'s result means matching bids can come from shared priors, and a reduced-scale, descriptive-only model-lineup ablation at nine sessions will look thin as the only defence. Fourth, the Bayesian-regime prior: learning algorithms reach Bayes-Nash with redrawn values and humans do not collude tacitly under sealed bids, so a positive result will be suspected of being prompt-induced, especially since Fish et al. and Heo et al. show one sentence moves bids substantially and the project's prompt states the tie-break rule in every condition. Fifth, realism: least-wins-first exists nowhere as a price tie-break and contradicts OECD guidance, so the paper must own it as a constructed stress condition. Sixth, the rebid arm: Kawai and Nakabayashi show rebids do not disrupt real cartels, a non-significant BAFO-versus-random contrast does not show resistance (which the planning document already concedes), and anticipation of a rebid changes round-one bidding in ways the random-rule benchmark does not capture. Seventh, tie frequency: on a 0.01 grid ties can be too rare for the manipulation to act, or frequent only because models like round numbers, and Puzzello's effect was weakest on fine grids. Eighth, win-pattern evidence: Davis and Wilson saw rotation without collusion, so evenness of wins proves little under any arm. Ninth, the control: it removes feedback and partners together, retains the word "repeated" in the prompt, and can degrade competence. Tenth, chain-of-thought evidence: the faithfulness literature limits it to corroboration.

## Conclusion

The literature repositions the project from "LLMs collude in auctions" to a sharper question that nobody has run: whether an auctioneer's tie-break rule can do for bidders what real cartels had to do for themselves. Skrzypacz and Hopenhayn's chips, Ishii's favour balance sheets and Kawai's backlog discontinuities all describe a ledger the ring must keep and enforce; least-wins-first is that ledger kept by the buyer, and the rebid is the OECD's "re-issue the tender" in miniature. Read this way, the informative comparison is not only least-wins-first against random, where theory predicts little, but the pair against the rule regulators actually wrote to kill ties: Hawaii's "previous winner keeps winning" rule is McAfee and McMillan's discriminatory tie-break, is cheap to add as a fourth arm, and would turn a two-contrast study into a test of the full ordering the theory implies, at the cost of a third confirmatory contrast and the power that goes with it. That is a suggestion from this synthesis, not a finding in the notes.

A null is also publishable, and the literature says why. If LLM bidders with redrawn private costs do not tie under any rule, they behave like Bichler's bandits and Sherstyuk's sealed-bid subjects, and the exploitable-tie-break worry attaches only to common-cost settings such as those of Fish et al. and Heo et al. If they do tie, and tie more under least-wins-first at prices near the reserve, they are doing something small human cartels abandoned and human lab subjects managed only with communication. Either result needs the same three additions to be credible: a deviation-response test, a tie rate benchmarked against the one-shot and grid-rounded Bayes-Nash rates, and a full reading of Heo, Ahn and Park (2026) before the novelty sentence is written.
